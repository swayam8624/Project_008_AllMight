## Pose estimation, transforms, and robust fitting

Pose estimation combines geometric constraints with robust fitting to recover transformations despite outliers and ambiguity.

### A planar image warp is a constrained camera pose

Perspective projection in camera-centered coordinates divides by depth and is nonlinear. A common industrial special case is a planar work surface: choose world coordinates with its height $Z_w=0$. Let the rigid world-to-camera transform be $\mathbf{X}_c=R\mathbf{X}_w+\mathbf{t}$, and let $K$ be camera calibration. For a plane point $(X_w,Y_w,0)$, homogeneous image coordinates satisfy

$$
\lambda\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix}=K\begin{bmatrix}r_1&r_2&t\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X_w\\Y_w\\1\end{bmatrix}=H\begin{bmatrix}X_w\\Y_w\\1\end{bmatrix},
$$

where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the first two columns of $R$, $H$ is a planar homography, and $\lambda$ is an arbitrary projective scale. The third rotation column is omitted because it multiplies the plane’s zero $Z_w$ coordinate; once the first two columns are known, the third is their cross product for a proper right-handed rotation. Under calibrated intrinsics, those columns must each have unit length and be mutually orthogonal. This physical structure leaves six pose degrees of freedom (three translations and three rotations), not nine arbitrary entries. An unconstrained least-squares fit to a 3-by-3 matrix can violate those constraints even if its point reprojection error is small. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-14 - Inspection in PatQuick, Hough Transform, Homography, Position Determination, Multi-Scale|MITV-14]] (video 00:35:37–00:50:47)

This restriction matters whenever one uses homographies for metric work. The mapping is appropriate for a phone on a table, a PCB on a conveyor, or road points viewed by a slightly oblique intersection camera, as long as the measured points lie on the modeled plane. It cannot recover arbitrary 3-D object structure from that single planar map. The homogeneous scale ambiguity also means multiplying $H$ by a nonzero scalar does not change image coordinates; a fitted projective matrix is not automatically a physically valid camera pose. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-14 - Inspection in PatQuick, Hough Transform, Homography, Position Determination, Multi-Scale|MITV-14]] (video 00:50:47–00:53:30)

### Photogrammetry: orientation is a family of related unknowns

Photogrammetry recovers geometric relations from image measurements. It distinguishes several orientations because they solve different parts of the camera-scene relationship: interior orientation concerns camera intrinsics and image-coordinate corrections; exterior orientation concerns camera position and attitude in the world; relative orientation relates two cameras or views; absolute orientation aligns a reconstructed model to an external coordinate frame. These stages are coupled in real systems but should remain conceptually distinct. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-17 - Photogrammetry, Orientation, Axes of Inertia, Symmetry, Orientation|MITV-17]] (notes pp. 106–115)

The first stereo derivation assumes equal focal geometry and a rectified pair, with camera centers at $(-B/2,0,0)$ and $(B/2,0,0)$. For a world point $(X,Y,Z)$, similar triangles give $x_L=F(X+B/2)/Z$ and $x_R=F(X-B/2)/Z$. Thus disparity $d=x_L-x_R=FB/Z$ and depth $Z=FB/d$; the mean image coordinate gives $X=Z(x_L+x_R)/(2F)$. A small disparity error produces $\delta Z\approx-(FB/d^2)\delta d$, so depth uncertainty grows rapidly with distance. A longer baseline or focal distance helps, but widens sensor packaging and magnifies mechanical misalignment. The lecture uses this simplified pair to motivate, rather than replace, later calibration of real relative camera orientation. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-17 - Photogrammetry, Orientation, Axes of Inertia, Symmetry, Orientation|MITV-17]] (video 00:10:02–00:17:21)

Absolute orientation is the same rigid-fit problem whether “left/right” means two sensors, two views before and after motion, or two reconstructed point clouds. From paired points, estimate $R$ and $\mathbf{t}$ in $\mathbf{p}_{Ri}=R\mathbf{p}_{Li}+\mathbf{t}$. Proper rotation requires both orthonormality and positive determinant: a reflection can satisfy $R^TR=I$ but reverses handedness and is not a physical rotation. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-17 - Photogrammetry, Orientation, Axes of Inertia, Symmetry, Orientation|MITV-17]] (video 00:17:24–00:33:10)

The lecture contrasts two closed-form but ad hoc orientation estimates with the later correspondence-based solution. A three-point construction builds an orthogonal frame by selecting an origin, a normalized first axis, Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization of a third point into the second axis, and their cross product. It gives a quick pose, but depends on which three matches were selected and throws away additional measurements. An inertia-axis construction centers all points, builds the second-moment matrix, and aligns its eigenvectors; this treats points symmetrically but ignores their known pairings. Both are vulnerable to shape symmetry. In addition to a sphere, regular tetrahedra and octahedra have direction-independent inertia, and a cube’s three principal moments are equal despite its visually obvious face axes. Correspondences can disambiguate these shapes, which is precisely the information the inertia-only method discarded. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-17 - Photogrammetry, Orientation, Axes of Inertia, Symmetry, Orientation|MITV-17]] (video 00:34:25–01:08:14)

With all correspondences retained, minimize $E(R,\mathbf{t})=\sum_i\|\mathbf{p}_{Ri}-(R\mathbf{p}_{Li}+\mathbf{t})\|^2$. Center each cloud by its centroid. Their centered coordinate sums are zero, so the translation cross-terms vanish; the best translation is $\mathbf{t}=\bar{\mathbf{p}}_R-R\bar{\mathbf{p}}_L$, which maps centroid to centroid. The remaining rotation minimizes centered squared residuals, equivalently maximizes the sum of corresponding centered-vector dot products. Each dot product rewards alignment of a “spine” from one centroid to its counterpart. This explains the least-squares target geometrically and tees up the quaternion eigenvalue method used in the following lecture. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-17 - Photogrammetry, Orientation, Axes of Inertia, Symmetry, Orientation|MITV-17]] (video 01:13:05–01:27:31)

Axes of inertia give a shape-derived orientation cue. For points or pixels weighted by mass/intensity, compute the centroid and covariance-like second moments; eigenvectors give principal directions. A long object often has a dominant axis. Symmetric shapes produce equal or repeated eigenvalues, so orientation can be ambiguous or unstable. The same eigenstructure used to estimate orientation also diagnoses whether the shape contains enough anisotropy to define it.

### Rotations and the geometry of their parameter space

A rotation in three dimensions has three degrees of freedom, but no single three-number coordinate chart represents every rotation without singularities. Euler angles are intuitive but can gimbal-lock. A $3\times3$ rotation matrix $R$ obeys $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=1$, giving nine entries under six constraints. Axis-angle uses a unit axis $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and angle $\theta$. The Rodrigues formula is

$$
R=I\cos\theta +(1-\cos\theta)\hat{\mathbf{a}}\hat{\mathbf{a}}^T+[\hat{\mathbf{a}}]_\times\sin\theta,
$$

where $I$ is the identity matrix and $[\hat{\mathbf{a}}]_\times$ is the skew-symmetric matrix implementing cross product with the axis. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-18 - Rotation and How to Represent It, Unit Quaternions, the Space of Rotations|MITV-18]] (notes pp. 115–128)

A unit quaternion is $q=(q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3)$ with $q_0^2+q_1^2+q_2^2+q_3^2=1$. For axis $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and angle $\theta$, choose $q=(\cos(\theta/2),\hat{\mathbf{a}}\sin(\theta/2))$. A 3-D vector can be represented as a pure quaternion $v=(0,\mathbf{v})$ and rotated by $v'=qv q^{-1}$ (with consistent multiplication conventions). Unit quaternions avoid a local singularity in common rotation calculations, but $q$ and $-q$ represent the same physical rotation; optimization must respect the unit constraint and this double cover. Quaternion coordinates are four-dimensional points on a 3-sphere, not unconstrained four-vectors. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-18 - Rotation and How to Represent It, Unit Quaternions, the Space of Rotations|MITV-18]] (video 00:57:49–00:59:42)

The representation is useful because the operations needed in vision have clean forms. A pure-vector quaternion has zero scalar part, and rotating it by $qvq^{-1}$ leaves the scalar part zero; the resulting $3\times3$ block is an ordinary proper rotation matrix. Quaternion multiplication composes rotations: applying $q$ and then $p$ gives the product $pq$ under the lecture’s convention. The inverse of a unit quaternion is its conjugate, just as the inverse of an orthonormal matrix is its transpose. This is the useful computational core behind the formalism, rather than the historical notation alone. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-18 - Rotation and How to Represent It, Unit Quaternions, the Space of Rotations|MITV-18]] (video 00:52:20–00:56:10)

The lecture’s algebra explains why this works. Write a quaternion as $(a,\mathbf{u})$ with scalar $a$ and vector part $\mathbf{u}$. Then $(a,\mathbf{u})(b,\mathbf{v})=(ab-\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v},\;a\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v})$. The dot product and cross product are both present, which makes multiplication noncommutative. Conjugation changes $(a,\mathbf{u})$ to $(a,-\mathbf{u})$, and $q\bar q=\|q\|^2$ enables division; for unit $q$, this gives $q^{-1}=\bar q$. The historical way to remember the multiplication rules is Hamilton’s $i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1$, so $ij=k$ while $ji=-k$. The lecture motivates the fourth component through Hamilton’s search for a divisible algebra of triples, not as an arbitrary fourth spatial coordinate. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-18 - Rotation and How to Represent It, Unit Quaternions, the Space of Rotations|MITV-18]] (video 00:35:00–00:48:20)

There is a concrete computational tradeoff. Naively composing two quaternions takes 16 multiplies and 12 additions, compared with 27 multiplies and 18 additions for a $3\times3$ matrix product. But directly rotating a vector with a quaternion takes more arithmetic than multiplying by a precomputed rotation matrix; the lecture compares 22 multiplies and 16 additions in a direct quaternion expansion with 9 and 6 for a matrix-vector product, or 15 and 12 when shared terms are reused. Quaternions are especially attractive for composing many rotations, interpolating intermediate orientations, differentiating a constrained orientation objective, and renormalizing after floating-point drift. For a single vector rotation, a matrix may be cheaper. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-18 - Rotation and How to Represent It, Unit Quaternions, the Space of Rotations|MITV-18]] (video 01:19:50–01:23:00)

The space of rotations is not an ordinary cube of three Euler angles. Unit quaternions form $S^3$, and antipodal points are identified because $q$ and $-q$ produce the same rotation. This explains why a four-component unit vector describes three degrees of freedom, and why uniform sampling or averaging must respect the curved geometry and double cover. Latitude-longitude sampling is dense near the poles, so coordinate-uniform samples are not surface-uniform. For a coarse regular rotation sample, the lecture uses symmetry groups of regular solids: the tetrahedron has 12 self-aligning rotations, cube/octahedron 24, and dodecahedron/icosahedron 60. These give evenly spaced initial orientations for search or averaging, though 60 samples remain coarse in a three-dimensional rotation space. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-18 - Rotation and How to Represent It, Unit Quaternions, the Space of Rotations|MITV-18]] (video 01:23:08–01:26:22)

Several alternatives clarify what is gained. Axis-angle is intuitive and identifies the fixed axis guaranteed by Euler’s rotation theorem, but composition is awkward. The Gibbs vector folds angle into the axis as $\hat{\mathbf{a}}\tan(theta/2)$, but becomes infinite at a perfectly valid $180^\circ$ rotation. Euler angles (roll, pitch, yaw) are intuitive locally but depend on order and intrinsic-versus-extrinsic convention; 24 conventions exist, and gimbal lock occurs when axes align. The lecture links this singularity to the Apollo gyroscope warning: aligning two gimbals loses a degree of freedom. Rotation matrices encode rotated basis axes in their columns but use nine values with orthonormality and handedness constraints. No representation eliminates every tradeoff, but unit quaternions make composition and constrained least squares particularly manageable. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-18 - Rotation and How to Represent It, Unit Quaternions, the Space of Rotations|MITV-18]] (video 00:13:18–00:18:57, 00:32:38–00:34:50)

The rotation axis also connects finite rotations to instantaneous motion. Angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is an ordinary three-vector whose direction is the instantaneous axis and whose magnitude is angular speed; a point at position $\mathbf{r}$ has velocity $\mathbf{v}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}$. Instantaneous angular velocities add, while finite rotations do not commute. In $n$ dimensions, a rotation is associated with preserved planes and has $n(n-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, which gives 3 in 3-D and 6 in 4-D. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-18 - Rotation and How to Represent It, Unit Quaternions, the Space of Rotations|MITV-18]] (video 00:07:28–00:13:18)

### Absolute orientation in closed form and RANSAC

Absolute orientation asks for the rigid transform aligning corresponding 3-D point sets. Let source points be $\mathbf{x}_i$ and target points be $\mathbf{y}_i$. The transform is $\mathbf{y}_i\approx R\mathbf{x}_i+\mathbf{t}$, where $R$ is a rotation and $\mathbf{t}$ a translation. First compute centroids $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\bar{\mathbf{y}}$; translation is then determined by $\mathbf{t}=\bar{\mathbf{y}}-R\bar{\mathbf{x}}$. Center the points so the remaining fit estimates rotation. A cross-covariance matrix built from centered correspondences yields a least-squares rotation through an eigen/SVD or quaternion formulation. The notes emphasize a closed-form solution, not an iterative search over all nine matrix entries. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]] (notes pp. 128–140)

Outliers—incorrect feature matches or points that violate the rigid-scene model—can dominate least squares because squared residuals grow rapidly. RANSAC repeatedly samples a minimal subset, estimates a candidate transform, counts correspondences whose residual lies below a threshold, and retains the hypothesis with strongest support. It then refits using inliers. If the inlier fraction is $w$ and a minimal model requires $s$ samples, the probability a trial contains only inliers is $w^s$. To achieve success probability $p$, the needed trial count is

$$
N\ge\frac{\log(1-p)}{\log(1-w^s)}.
$$

This is probabilistic, not a guarantee: too few trials, a poor threshold, degenerate samples, or a low inlier fraction can fail. The result is also only as meaningful as the correspondence generation and residual metric. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]]

### Making the quaternion fit stable, and knowing when it is ambiguous

For centered paired point clouds, the rotation-only objective is equivalent to maximizing $q^TNq$ over a unit quaternion $q$. The matrix $N$ is not an arbitrary matrix: each paired source/target vector contributes to a cross-covariance-like $3\times3$ dyadic sum $M=\sum_i \mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{y}_i^T$, and $N$ is assembled from those nine data quantities. The Rayleigh quotient $q^TNq/(q^Tq)$ is constant along each ray in quaternion coordinates; differentiating it gives $Nq=\lambda q$. The maximizing rotation is the eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue. This both enforces the unit constraint geometrically and turns the constrained fit into an eigenproblem. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]] (notes pp. 128–139; video 00:26:10–00:34:36)

Two correspondences cannot determine a fully general rigid pose: after aligning two points, one degree of freedom remains as rotation around their connecting axis. Three non-collinear point correspondences determine a proper rigid transform, whereas a general affine transform in three dimensions has 12 unknowns including translation and needs four noncoplanar correspondences. The extra flexibility of a general affine fit admits skew and independent axis scaling, so it no longer models a rigid sensor-to-sensor relationship. Minimal sample size matters directly in RANSAC: three is enough for a rigid orientation hypothesis, but those three must avoid degenerate geometry. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]] (video 00:35:01–00:42:05)

Coplanar point sets are a useful special case, not simply a numerical nuisance. Their cross-covariance determinant vanishes, and the characteristic polynomial of the quaternion matrix reduces to a biquadratic, solvable as a quadratic in $\lambda^2$. Geometrically, when both clouds are planar, align their plane normals first: the rotation axis is parallel to $\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2$, and the signed angle follows from dot and cross products using $\operatorname{atan2}$. A final in-plane 2-D rotation aligns the point patterns. The lecture thus connects an algebraic singular case to a clean geometric decomposition. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]] (notes pp. 135–139; video 00:46:00–00:52:49)

Scale deserves a separate error model. If translation is removed by centering, a one-sided squared error can spuriously reduce its residual by shrinking the transformed cloud. Worse, reversing source and target does not in general return the inverse scale. For the lecture’s specific symmetric objective, compare the scaled clouds as $J(s)=\sum_i\|\mathbf r'_{R,i}/\sqrt{s}-\sqrt{s}R\mathbf r'_{L,i}\|^2=S_R/s-2D+sS_L$, where $S_L=\sum_i\|\mathbf r'_{L,i}\|^2$, $S_R=\sum_i\|\mathbf r'_{R,i}\|^2$, and $D=\sum_i(\mathbf r'_{R,i})^TR\mathbf r'_{L,i}$. Differentiating gives $s^2=S_R/S_L$; reversing the clouds gives the reciprocal. This scale ratio depends only on the two centered cloud spreads, not point pairings, for this objective. Translation and scale can therefore be estimated without correspondences or rotation, leaving correspondences to determine rotation. This is appropriate when baseline or reconstruction scale is slightly uncertain, but not when metric scale is already trusted. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]] (notes pp. 128–139; video 00:17:00–00:25:25)

The matrix-to-quaternion conversion illustrates a broader numerical lesson: an algebraically valid formula may be unstable near a singular configuration. The rotation-matrix trace gives $\cos\theta=(\operatorname{tr}R-1)/2$, which is a useful quick plausibility check but loses angular sensitivity near zero rotation. The off-diagonal skew terms retain the sine component and pair naturally with $\operatorname{atan2}$. For direct matrix-to-quaternion recovery, four diagonal-derived squared component candidates are formed; choose the largest candidate to avoid dividing by a small component, take that component’s sign by convention (since $q$ and $-q$ coincide), and recover the other three from corresponding symmetric and antisymmetric off-diagonal sums. Choosing the dominant component makes the method robust when a published conversion’s selected denominator is near zero. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]] (video 00:08:00–00:16:23)

Least squares remains valuable under well-behaved Gaussian-like noise, but one gross correspondence error can dominate a squared residual. RANSAC samples the minimum needed correspondences, estimates a pose, and counts all matches within a residual band. Both the inlier residual threshold and the required consensus size are model choices based on expected measurement noise and outlier prevalence; a finite trial cap is also needed. For inlier fraction $w$, sample size $s$, and desired probability $p$ of at least one all-inlier sample, $N\ge\log(1-p)/\log(1-w^s)$. More sampled correspondences can improve precision, while minimal samples increase the chance that a trial avoids outliers. The lecture’s example of a wildly erroneous point pulling a least-squares line makes the failure intuitive. It also distinguishes original RANSAC consensus testing from a possible hybrid in which repeated parameter estimates vote in a Hough-like accumulator. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]] (video 00:53:03–01:03:00)

Rotation sampling gets a second practical construction. Sampling latitude and longitude uniformly clusters near poles. Sampling a uniform point in a 4-D cube and normalizing is also biased; a simple rejection sampler draws four independent uniform components, discards vectors outside the unit 4-ball (and near zero to avoid unstable normalization), then normalizes the retained vector to the unit 3-sphere. This gives a uniform distribution on $S^3$, with the caveat that antipodal quaternions encode the same rotation. For regular rather than random sampling, the lecture generates the cube’s 24 self-aligning rotations by quaternion multiplication; composing two quarter-turn-derived cube symmetries yields a new 120-degree rotation about a body diagonal, demonstrating how group closure builds the table. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]] (video 01:03:03–01:25:32)

### Proper rotation from centered point clouds

Given $N$ matched point pairs $\mathbf x_i,\mathbf y_i\in\mathbb R^3$, minimize

$$
J(R,\mathbf t)=\sum_{i=1}^N\|R\mathbf x_i+\mathbf t-\mathbf y_i\|^2
$$

subject to $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=+1$. The translation derivative sets the mean residual to zero, giving $\mathbf t=\bar{\mathbf y}-R\bar{\mathbf x}$. Define centered points $\mathbf x_i'=\mathbf x_i-\bar{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf y_i'=\mathbf y_i-\bar{\mathbf y}$. Substitution removes translation and reduces the objective to minimizing $\sum_i\|R\mathbf x_i'-\mathbf y_i'\|^2$. Expanding the squared norm, the sums of point norms are constant under rotation; minimizing is equivalent to maximizing $\sum_i(\mathbf y_i')^TR\mathbf x_i'$.

The quaternion representation expresses that alignment score as a quadratic form $q^TNq$ for a symmetric matrix $N$ assembled from the cross-covariance of the centered source points $\mathbf x'_i$ and target points $\mathbf y'_i$. Here the active map is specifically $\mathbf x'_i\mapsto R\mathbf x'_i\approx\mathbf y'_i$, matching the score $\sum_i(\mathbf y'_i)^TR\mathbf x'_i$ above; swapping the covariance order without also changing the map estimates the inverse rotation. Subject to $q^Tq=1$, the maximum is the greatest eigenvalue and its normalized eigenvector. This produces a proper rotation encoded by a unit quaternion. Finally, restore translation. The approach requires meaningful correspondences and nondegenerate point geometry. If all observations collapse to a point, rotation is unconstrained; collinearity or planarity can leave some modes weakly constrained. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-17 - Photogrammetry, Orientation, Axes of Inertia, Symmetry, Orientation|MITV-17]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-19 - Absolute Orientation in Closed Form, Outliers and Robustness, RANSAC|MITV-19]]

A numerical rigid-transform check is useful after implementation: transform a known non-collinear source triangle by a quarter-turn and a translation, fit back, and verify three independent quantities: determinant is approximately $+1$, orthogonality residual $\|R^TR-I\|$ is small, and transformed points match the targets. Check translation only after centroids and rotation have been computed; otherwise rotation error can masquerade as translation error. With noisy data, report residual spread rather than rounding the pose to a falsely exact value.

### RANSAC threshold and trial count are part of the model

RANSAC’s inlier threshold is in the units of the residual. If the residual is image reprojection error, a threshold of two means two pixels; if it is 3-D point distance, it means a world-unit tolerance. A threshold that is too small rejects valid noisy correspondences, while one that is too large admits outliers and biases refitting. A common robust flow is: sample a minimal set; reject degenerate geometry; fit; score all observations; keep the model with strongest inlier support or a robust score; refit on inliers; then optionally run a continuous optimizer. The probability formula sizes the number of random draws but does not select the correct threshold or guarantee a valid model when the assumed inlier fraction is wrong. ### CORDIC detail: why shift-add can replace a square root and arctangent

The edge detector needs gradient magnitude $G_0=\sqrt{G_x^2+G_y^2}$ and direction $G_\theta=\arctan(G_y/G_x)$. CORDIC rotates the coordinate frame through small candidate angles, monitoring whether the residual vertical component decreases while the horizontal component increases. Let the predetermined angles be $\alpha_i=\arctan(2^{-i})$ for iteration $i$. A rotation step can be written

$$
x_{i+1}=x_i-d_i2^{-i}y_i,\qquad y_{i+1}=y_i+d_i2^{-i}x_i,
$$

where sign $d_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ selects the direction that reduces the current residual angle. The accumulated angle is $\theta=\sum_i d_i\alpha_i$. Multiplication by $2^{-i}$ is a binary shift, so the recurrence uses shifts and additions/subtractions; a fixed gain factor corrects the predictable length change after the iterations. The MIT notes describe a sequence of progressively smaller rotations and a precomputable gain of about 1.16 for the selected convention. CORDIC trades a finite number of simple operations and controlled approximation error for avoiding direct square root and inverse tangent operations. Its output accuracy depends on iteration count and quantization; it does not improve the quality of the input gradient stencil itself. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-11 - Edge Detection, Subpixel Position, CORDIC, Line Detection (US 6,408,109)|MITV-11]] (notes pp. 60–61, 73)

### The explicit quaternion matrix makes absolute orientation computable

For paired source points $\mathbf x_i$ and target points $\mathbf y_i$, first subtract their respective centroids, producing $\mathbf x_i'$ and $\mathbf y_i'$. Define the cross-covariance in this particular order:

$$
C=\sum_i\mathbf x_i'(\mathbf y_i')^{\mathsf T},\qquad C=(c_{jk}).
$$

Use a scalar-first unit quaternion $q=(w,x,y,z)^{\mathsf T}$ for an active rotation taking source coordinates toward target coordinates. The symmetric matrix for this convention is

$$
N=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{tr}C&c_{23}-c_{32}&c_{31}-c_{13}&c_{12}-c_{21}\\
c_{23}-c_{32}&c_{11}-c_{22}-c_{33}&c_{12}+c_{21}&c_{13}+c_{31}\\
c_{31}-c_{13}&c_{12}+c_{21}&-c_{11}+c_{22}-c_{33}&c_{23}+c_{32}\\
c_{12}-c_{21}&c_{13}+c_{31}&c_{23}+c_{32}&-c_{11}-c_{22}+c_{33}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Why this matrix? Expand the squared alignment error. Its terms that depend on rotation are $-2\sum_i(\mathbf y_i')^{\mathsf T}R(q)\mathbf x_i'$. Substituting the unit-quaternion rotation formula and collecting the coefficients of $w^2,wx,wy,\ldots,z^2$ gives $q^{\mathsf T}Nq$ for the correlation being maximized. The constraint $q^{\mathsf T}q=1$ turns this into a symmetric Rayleigh-quotient problem: choose an eigenvector for the largest algebraic eigenvalue of $N$. This qualification matters. Plain power iteration selects the eigenvalue with largest magnitude and can select a negative eigenvalue instead.

Recover the active rotation using $\mathbf v=(x,y,z)^{\mathsf T}$:

$$
R(q)=(w^2-\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v)I+2\mathbf v\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}+2w[\mathbf v]_\times,
\qquad [\mathbf v]_\times\mathbf a=\mathbf v\times\mathbf a.
$$

Then restore translation as $\mathbf t=\overline{\mathbf y}-R\overline{\mathbf x}$. The quaternions $q$ and $-q$ give the same rotation. If covariance was assembled in the reversed order, transpose it before using this displayed matrix; otherwise the estimated rotation direction reverses. Repeated largest eigenvalues can signal a nonunique orientation in degenerate data, rather than a reason to choose an arbitrary pose confidently.

As a numerical reconstruction check, rotate four noncoplanar source points by a positive ninety-degree rotation about the third axis and translate by $(0.2,-0.3,0.5)$. Centering, assembling $C,N$, taking the largest-eigenvalue eigenvector and restoring the centroid shift recovers both that rotation and translation. The six checks used here verify symmetry, the recovered rotation, orthogonality, determinant one, translation, and all reconstructed point correspondences. ## Stereo, relative orientation, and reconstruction

Stereo reconstructs shared 3-D geometry from multiple views, with critical configurations and gauge freedoms limiting recovery.

### Stereo and relative orientation

Binocular stereo observes the same scene from two camera centers. A 3-D point lies on a ray from each center; their intersection estimates the point. With rectified cameras separated by baseline $B$, common focal length $f$, and disparity $d=x_L-x_R$, depth is

$$
Z=\frac{fB}{d}.
$$

Here $x_L$ and $x_R$ are horizontal image coordinates in the left and right images. Small disparity yields large depth and high sensitivity: $\partial Z/\partial d=-fB/d^2$. Thus distant points are poorly localized in depth for a fixed pixel error. Stereo also fails or weakens on textureless regions, repeated patterns, occlusions, and surfaces visible in only one camera. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]] (notes pp. 140–160)

Relative orientation estimates the relation between two camera views from corresponding image points. Epipolar geometry states that a point in one image constrains its mate to an epipolar line in the other. In calibrated coordinates, the essential matrix encodes relative rotation and translation direction; in uncalibrated pixel coordinates, the fundamental matrix also includes camera intrinsics. Translation scale is not determined by two monocular views alone, so the recovered structure is defined up to a global scale unless a known baseline or object dimension supplies metric scale.

Structure from motion generalizes this reasoning across multiple images. Triangulation intersects rays; nonlinear refinement can minimize reprojection error. For observed image point $\mathbf{u}_{ij}$ of world point $\mathbf{X}_j$ in camera $i$, predicted pixel position is $\pi(P_i\mathbf{X}_j)$, where $P_i$ is that camera’s projection matrix and $\pi$ converts homogeneous coordinates to ordinary image coordinates. Reprojection residual is

$$
\mathbf{r}_{ij}=\mathbf{u}_{ij}-\pi(P_i\mathbf{X}_j).
$$

Summing squared residuals across observations provides a consistency criterion, but low reprojection error alone does not prove correct metric scale, uniqueness, or an appropriate scene model. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]]

Quadrics provide compact algebraic descriptions for common curved surfaces. A quadric is the zero set of a quadratic form in homogeneous 3-D coordinates, $\mathbf{X}^TQ\mathbf{X}=0$, where symmetric matrix $Q$ encodes the surface. Sphere, ellipsoid, cone, and other familiar forms are instances. Such parameterizations can regularize shape recovery when the object is known to belong to a class, but impose model bias when it does not.

### Exterior orientation and bundle adjustment

Exterior orientation recovers a camera’s position and attitude relative to a world coordinate frame from control points or geometric observations. Position is translation; attitude is rotation. Measuring angles and lengths can constrain pose, but ambiguities and noisy correspondences remain. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]] (notes pp. 160–171)

Bundle adjustment jointly optimizes camera parameters and 3-D point coordinates so all observations agree in projection. It is a nonlinear least-squares problem over many coupled variables. Sparse structure helps computation: each image observation depends only on one camera and one point. Gauge freedoms must be fixed—for example by anchoring one camera and setting a scale—because translating, rotating, or scaling the entire reconstruction may leave projections unchanged. Initialization, outliers, calibration errors, and local minima all matter. “Bundle adjustment succeeded” means the chosen objective was numerically reduced under a model; it does not guarantee the model or global geometry is true.

The calibration discussion distinguishes the ideal pinhole model from the optical system that produced a real image. Radial distortion shifts image points along a radius from a distortion center; a simple model writes $\delta x=x(k_1r^2+k_2r^4+\cdots)$ and $\delta y=y(k_1r^2+k_2r^4+\cdots)$, where $r$ is distance from the center and $k_1,k_2,\ldots$ are fitted coefficients. Straight plumb lines, known to be straight and parallel in the world, reveal barrel or pincushion bending and help estimate this radial effect. The notes also distinguish tangential distortion, decentering when the distortion center differs from the principal point, and tilted image planes. More parameters can fit more optical behavior, but each added parameter needs evidence; an over-flexible calibration can fit the calibration object and generalize poorly. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]] (notes pp. 150–159)

The notes’ Tsai calibration discussion makes explicit how interior and exterior orientation become coupled. Interior parameters include principal point and focal length; exterior pose has three translation and three rotation degrees of freedom. Known 3-D calibration-object points and their image correspondences constrain these together. A calibration procedure should therefore account for the pose of the target and should use observations that spread constraints across the image. A center-only calibration cannot reveal radial distortion well because its radial displacement is nearly zero close to the center. The notes caution that simple vanishing-point calibration does not by itself handle radial distortion and may be inadequate for high-precision aerial imaging.

### Relative orientation: recover the two rays’ shared geometry

Relative orientation estimates the rotation and baseline direction relating two cameras from corresponding image rays. Once that geometry is known, triangulation is simple: each image point and camera center define a ray, and the rays intersect at the scene point. Before calibration, the same unknown geometry makes matching expensive. A point seen in one image defines a plane through the two camera centers; projecting that epipolar plane into each image gives a corresponding epipolar line. Thus a match found at one pixel need only be sought along one line in the other image. The baseline projects to the epipoles, where the epipolar lines meet; for nearly parallel cameras the epipoles may fall outside the image and the lines look almost parallel. The lecture ties this to real setup: parallel cameras maximize overlap, while convergent cameras may suit nearer objects but reduce common field of view. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] (notes pp. 140–149; video 00:16:40–00:22:54)

The unknowns are three rotational degrees of freedom plus the direction of the baseline, which has two degrees of freedom because monocular perspective does not reveal absolute scale. If every scene point and the baseline are multiplied by the same positive factor, image ratios $X/Z$ stay unchanged. Relative orientation therefore has five degrees of freedom; metric baseline length requires a known dimension or another scale reference. An ideal corresponding ray pair gives one coplanarity constraint. With baseline $\mathbf{b}$ and the left and right rays expressed in a common frame as $\mathbf{r}_L$ and $\mathbf{r}_R$, their signed volume must vanish:

$$
\mathbf{b}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_L\times\mathbf{r}_R)=0.
$$

This scalar triple product is the coplanarity condition. Five correspondences are the minimal count, but the system is nonlinear and may have as many as 20 mathematical solutions. Additional matches and known camera layout select a physically plausible solution; minimum count is not a promise of uniqueness. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] (notes pp. 140–149; video 00:23:03–00:33:52, 00:34:03–00:37:30)

The lecture warns against minimizing a quantity merely because it vanishes at a perfect answer. The triple product is zero for exact geometry, but its magnitude weights errors according to scene depth and ray angle. A small pixel displacement for a far point can correspond to a large distance between skew rays, so raw 3-D gap or unweighted volume does not match the actual observation noise. For noisy data, minimize image-coordinate reprojection error, or scale a geometric residual by the local conversion factor from image error to ray-space error. That factor depends on the current baseline and rotation; therefore practical fitting iterates: estimate pose, update the residual weights, and solve again. Initialization matters because the correspondence between pixel error and 3-D error changes with geometry. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] (video 00:37:36–00:52:16, 01:13:28–01:15:07)

A geometric alternative describes the shortest gap between two skew rays. At the closest points, the gap vector is perpendicular to both ray directions, so it is parallel to $\mathbf{r}_L\times\mathbf{r}_R$. Starting from the baseline and moving along each ray gives a vector closure equation. Dotting it with the cross product eliminates the unknown along-ray distances and returns the triple-product gap; other dot products solve the distances $\alpha$ and $\beta$ along the rays. Their signs matter: negative values mean the closest intersection lies behind one or both cameras. Since projective equations describe infinite lines, not forward-facing rays, cheirality checks discard such mathematically valid but physically impossible matches. The lecture also notes that the same algebra appears whether the camera moved between frames or the world moved around a fixed camera. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] (video 00:39:23–00:47:42)

The equations can be written in quaternion form by treating each direction and the baseline as a pure quaternion, then using conjugation to shift factors across dot products. A reparameterization trades baseline and rotation variables for two unit quaternions constrained to be perpendicular, leaving the same five degrees of freedom but adding three coupled constraints. Unlike the single norm constraint in absolute orientation, this is harder to optimize directly. The residual has symmetries: exchanging left and right rays preserves the line geometry, and swapping the two quaternion roles yields related candidates. Together with the $q\sim -q$ equivalence, a numerical search can encounter up to eight equivalent or sign-related interpretations. These symmetries can accelerate a search but require cheirality and camera-convention checks to select the intended physical pose. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] (video 00:53:03–01:05:05)

There is no comparable closed-form solution presented for relative orientation. A practical heuristic alternates: hold baseline fixed and solve a linear least-squares rotation update, then hold rotation fixed and solve for baseline, repeating until stable. This can converge quickly but has no general convergence guarantee. Nonlinear least-squares packages such as Levenberg–Marquardt offer another route: provide residual equations and parameters, but use a nonredundant local parameterization or explicitly enforce unit-quaternion and baseline constraints. The choice of residual remains crucial; the optimizer cannot repair an objective that weights world-space ray errors inconsistently with image measurement precision. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] (video 01:05:06–01:10:46)

### Critical surfaces: geometry can make stereo ill-conditioned

The relative-orientation material identifies critical surface configurations in which different camera motions or scene structures produce indistinguishable or nearly indistinguishable observations. A one-sheet hyperboloid and intersections of planes arise in the algebraic discussion. The important practical point is not that ordinary scenes are exactly these special surfaces; it is that scenes locally close to them amplify noise. In binocular stereo, a surface can pass through configurations tied to both camera centers, making depth or motion interpretations ambiguous. In structure from motion, a sequence can move along a critical surface and retain poor constraints. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]] (notes pp. 140–153)

Broader field of view can make it less likely that the entire observed scene resembles one of these critical arrangements. The notes mention a rigid “spider head” with eight calibrated cameras whose views can be stitched into a mosaic. That improves angular coverage, but introduces a second calibration problem: relative positions and orientations of the cameras must be known well enough that the mosaic is geometrically coherent. More sensors create more observations and also more parameters that can be wrong.

### Why critical surfaces destroy relative orientation

The algebraic relative-orientation constraint is a homogeneous quadratic in scene coordinates. In principal axes it belongs to the quadric family $x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2+z^2/c^2=1$ with sign changes; one negative sign gives a one-sheet hyperboloid, two give a two-sheet hyperboloid, and all positive signs give an ellipsoid. For the particular relative-orientation constraint there is no constant term, so the origin of one camera lies on the quadric. Substitution of the other camera center (at $-\mathbf{b}$) also gives zero, and homogeneity places the entire baseline line on the surface. The relevant quadric is ruled: two families of straight lines lie on it. This special geometry explains why some scenes make camera motion difficult to recover. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]] (notes pp. 140–149; video 00:00:12–00:07:39)

A pair of planes is a special quadric because the product of their linear equations is a quadratic equation equal to zero. If one plane contains the baseline, it is an epipolar plane and appears edge-on as a line; the second plane can be arbitrary. Thus ordinary planar surfaces are not exempt from criticality. A local patch on a curved object can also approximate a critical quadric closely enough to amplify noise even when it is not exactly degenerate. Larger field of view helps by observing more scene variation; aerial mapping historically used rigid multi-camera “spider head” structures to gain coverage before lenses could provide both wide angle, low distortion, and sharp imagery. Those camera mosaics themselves require careful relative calibration. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]] (video 00:07:42–00:13:12)

### D. How do scale and pose ambiguities enter reconstruction?

Two calibrated monocular views determine relative translation direction and a reconstruction up to scale. Bundle adjustment can refine all coordinates while preserving that scale ambiguity. Fix one baseline length, known object dimension, or control point coordinate to set metric scale. Likewise, fix one reference camera to define the world coordinate frame. If these gauge choices are omitted, a numerical optimizer may have flat directions, and a reported covariance or Hessian may be singular. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]]

### Stereo, essential geometry, and metric scale

In rectified stereo, the left and right optical axes are parallel and image rows correspond. Let the camera centers be separated horizontally by baseline $B$. Similar triangles give $x_L=fX/Z$ and $x_R=f(X-B)/Z$ under one common sign convention, so disparity $d=x_L-x_R=fB/Z$ and $Z=fB/d$. The average coordinate estimates lateral position after depth is known. If the coordinate sign convention or camera ordering is reversed, disparity sign changes; depth remains positive under a consistent camera model. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-20 - Space of Rotations, Regular Tessellations, Critical Surfaces, Binocular Stereo|MITV-20]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]]

For unrectified cameras, matching is constrained by epipolar lines rather than same-row search. The essential relation $\mathbf{x}'^T[\mathbf t]_\times R\mathbf{x}=0$ expresses coplanarity of the two rays and baseline. This relation estimates rotation and the direction of translation, but multiplying $\mathbf t$ by any positive scale gives the same essential matrix up to scale. A known baseline converts relative structure to metric scale. Triangulated points should project in front of both cameras; the algebraic epipolar equation alone also permits mirror or behind-camera solutions. Reprojection error measures image agreement, while cheirality checks visibility orientation. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-21 - Relative Orientation, Binocular Stereo, Structure, Quadrics, Calibration, Reprojection|MITV-21]]

### Bundle adjustment objective and gauge

Let camera $i$ have pose/intrinsics parameter vector $\boldsymbol\theta_i$, and point $j$ have coordinate $\mathbf X_j$. For each observed image point $\mathbf u_{ij}$, predict $\hat{\mathbf u}_{ij}=\pi(P(\boldsymbol\theta_i)\mathbf X_j)$, where $P$ is the projection model and $\pi$ divides homogeneous coordinates by their final scale. The bundle objective is

$$
\min_{\{\boldsymbol\theta_i\},\{\mathbf X_j\}}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal O}w_{ij}\|\mathbf u_{ij}-\hat{\mathbf u}_{ij}\|^2,
$$

where $\mathcal O$ is the set of visible camera-point observations and $w_{ij}$ weights confidence. The Jacobian is sparse because a residual depends only on one camera and one point. Gauss–Newton linearizes residuals and solves normal equations; Levenberg–Marquardt adds damping $\lambda I$ to stabilize steps when the local quadratic approximation is poor. The notes connect this optimization to their earlier conditioning discussion: nearly unobservable directions produce a poorly conditioned Hessian.

If every camera and point is translated or rotated together, image projections do not change. Monocular structure also has a scale gauge. Fixing a reference camera and a scale (or anchoring enough control points) removes these null directions. If gauge is not fixed, the solver can still reduce reprojection error while wandering along equivalent solutions, and the normal matrix may be singular. Additional constraints such as known focal length, known baseline, ground control, or distortion calibration change which degrees of freedom are recoverable. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]]

## Surface orientation from illumination

Controlled lighting supplies independent measurements of surface orientation, while albedo and reflectance determine identifiability.

### Recovering surface orientation from controlled illumination

Photometric stereo estimates a surface normal by observing the same point under several known light directions. For an ideal Lambertian surface, brightness is proportional to the cosine between unit normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ and unit light direction $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_i$. In radiometric units, diffuse albedo $\rho$ gives BRDF $f_r=\rho/\pi$ and outgoing radiance $L_o=(\rho/\pi)E_{\mathrm{inc}}$, where $E_{\mathrm{inc}}$ is incoming irradiance. Below, albedo combines with the fixed $1/\pi$, source, exposure, and camera-gain scales; these factors act together as $\rho$, an effective image scale, unless calibrated separately. Then measurement $i$ is

$$
E_i=\rho\hat{\mathbf{n}}^T\hat{\mathbf{s}}_i.
$$

Define the scaled normal $\mathbf{n}=\rho\hat{\mathbf{n}}$. With three light observations,

$$
S\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{E},
$$

where row $i$ of $S$ is $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_i^T$ and $\mathbf{E}=(E_1,E_2,E_3)^T$. If $S$ has full rank, solve for $\mathbf{n}$, recover albedo as $\rho=\|\mathbf{n}\|$, and normalize to get $\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{n}/\|\mathbf{n}\|$. Folding unknown albedo into $\mathbf{n}$ makes the measurement equations linear. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-06 - Photometric Stereo, Noise Gain, Error Amplification, Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors Review|MITV-06]] (notes pp. 33–36)

A rank failure occurs when the illumination directions do not span 3-D. The notes’ lunar-crater example makes the problem concrete: viewed from Earth under sunlight, the source directions available for observations can lie in essentially one plane, so lighting changes fail to provide the independent constraints needed for a full normal estimate. The remedy is experimental design: move or add lights so their directions are as independent and well spread as possible. Orthogonal directions are an idealized case because they probe independent components. In noisy data, use an overdetermined least-squares solution with more than three light positions and inspect conditioning rather than merely whether a numerical inverse exists.

Surface slopes describe a height field. If the surface is $z(x,y)$, then $p=\partial z/\partial x$ and $q=\partial z/\partial y$. Tangent directions are $(1,0,p)^T$ and $(0,1,q)^T$, whose cross product is proportional to $(-p,-q,1)^T$. Thus

$$
\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{(-p,-q,1)^T}{\sqrt{p^2+q^2+1}},\qquad p=-\frac{n_x}{n_z},\quad q=-\frac{n_y}{n_z}.
$$

A normal field is locally measurable with photometric stereo, but a height field requires integration. The slopes must also be integrable: ideally $p_y=q_x$. Noise can violate this condition, so independently estimated local normals may not correspond exactly to any global surface. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-06 - Photometric Stereo, Noise Gain, Error Amplification, Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors Review|MITV-06]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-07 - Gradient Space, Reflectance Map, Image Irradiance Equation, Gnomonic Projection|MITV-07]]

### A. Is a lighting setup informative enough?

1. Write the per-light model $E_i=\mathbf{s}_i^T\mathbf{n}$, where $\mathbf{s}_i$ is the known illumination vector and $\mathbf{n}$ is the scaled surface normal.
2. Stack the rows into matrix $S$ and check its rank.
3. If $S$ has rank below three, some component of $\mathbf{n}$ is unobservable, regardless of how accurately brightness is measured.
4. If rank is three but the smallest singular value is small, the estimate exists but will amplify noise.
5. Redesign the lights to span different directions; if possible, collect more measurements and solve least squares.
6. Inspect residuals for violations of the Lambertian/constant-albedo model before trusting the recovered normal.

This sequence separates structural identifiability (rank) from practical accuracy (conditioning) and model adequacy (residual pattern). [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-06 - Photometric Stereo, Noise Gain, Error Amplification, Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors Review|MITV-06]]

### Photometric stereo: uniqueness from albedo absorption

A unit normal has two degrees of freedom because it lies on the unit sphere. If albedo $\rho$ is unknown too, the Lambertian equation $E_i=\rho\hat{\mathbf n}^T\hat{\mathbf s}_i$ seems to have three unknowns plus a nonlinear unit-length condition. Introduce $\mathbf n=\rho\hat{\mathbf n}$ and instead solve $E_i=\mathbf n^T\hat{\mathbf s}_i$. This is three linear unknowns for three independent measurements. After solving, $\rho=\|\mathbf n\|$ and normalization recovers the unit normal. The transformation does not create information; it reorganizes unknowns so the equations are linear and the norm constraint is applied afterward. If $\mathbf n$ is close to zero because the surface is dark or the lighting yields weak responses, normalization becomes unstable, another conditioning issue.

For $m>3$ light directions, form an $m\times3$ matrix $S$ whose rows are light vectors and a brightness vector $\mathbf E$. The least-squares solution is $\hat{\mathbf n}=(S^TS)^{-1}S^T\mathbf E$ when $S$ has full column rank. Orthogonal, balanced lighting makes $S^TS$ close to a scaled identity, so errors in one component do not leak strongly into another. If all lights lie in a plane, $S$ cannot observe the normal component orthogonal to that plane. The notes use the moon example to emphasize that natural lighting may make a theoretically straightforward algorithm impossible to identify from the available images. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-06 - Photometric Stereo, Noise Gain, Error Amplification, Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors Review|MITV-06]]

### A second photometric-stereo check with orthogonal lights

If three unit lights point along the camera-frame axes, the lighting matrix is $S=I$. Then measured brightness vector equals scaled normal directly: $\mathbf E=\mathbf n$. For example, measurements $(0.3,0.4,0.866)$ have norm approximately one, so the recovered normal is approximately that vector and albedo is approximately one. If the same measurements are all multiplied by two because surface reflectance doubles, the scaled normal becomes $(0.6,0.8,1.732)$ with norm about two; normalizing gives the same orientation. This illustrates why scaled-normal estimation separates albedo from orientation after the linear solve. In practice, shadows can clip a measurement to zero and specularity can add an inconsistent bright value; robust estimation or a calibrated reflectance model is then required. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-06 - Photometric Stereo, Noise Gain, Error Amplification, Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors Review|MITV-06]]

## Shape from shading and reflectance

Shape from shading turns reflectance and illumination into a nonlinear equation for surface shape, requiring assumptions and global consistency.

### Gradient space, reflectance maps, and the irradiance equation

Gradient space is the $(p,q)$ plane: each point represents a local surface tilt. A [[Surface orientation from image brightness|reflectance map]] $R(p,q)$ predicts image brightness for a surface of slope $(p,q)$ under a chosen illumination and reflectance model. For a Lambertian surface under a distant light, brightness is proportional to the dot product of normal and light. For fixed brightness, the permitted slopes form a contour (an isophote) in gradient space. One brightness value therefore usually leaves a curve of possible normals; multiple light measurements intersect those curves and reduce the ambiguity. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-07 - Gradient Space, Reflectance Map, Image Irradiance Equation, Gnomonic Projection|MITV-07]] (notes pp. 36–40)

The image irradiance equation writes the measured image brightness as a function of surface orientation and viewing/lighting conditions. It is a forward model, not a direct height formula. Shape from shading must infer slopes from a brightness equation and then enforce that these slopes arise from a coherent surface. Gnomonic projection appears in the course as a way to represent orientation directions on a plane; like any projection, it is convenient over a domain but introduces its own distortion and singular region. One should keep “orientation coordinates” distinct from physical image coordinates.

### Reflectance models matter: lunar soil and electron microscopy

Lambert’s cosine law assumes an ideal diffuse reflector. Real materials can have directional scattering, surface roughness, and shadowing. The notes discuss Hapke reflectance for lunar material: interpreting lunar brightness with a Lambertian lookup can confuse reflectance change with surface tilt. A calibrated reflectance map can accommodate an empirical material response, but it does not remove the need to know or control illumination. Scanning electron microscopy gives a different illumination and sensing geometry, so the same brightness-to-normal assumptions cannot be transferred unchanged. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-08 - Shading, Special Cases, Lunar Surface, Scanning Electron Microscope, Green's Theorem|MITV-08]] (notes pp. 40–46)

Green’s theorem converts a planar area integral into a line integral around the positively oriented boundary:

$$
\iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA=\oint_{\partial D}(P\,dx+Q\,dy).
$$

 Choosing $P=-y/2$ and $Q=x/2$ gives area $A=\tfrac12\oint(x\,dy-y\,dx)$. Choosing $(P,Q)=(0,x^2/2)$ gives the first moment $\iint_Dx\,dA=\tfrac12\oint x^2\,dy$; choosing $(P,Q)=(-y^2/2,0)$ gives $\iint_Dy\,dA=-\tfrac12\oint y^2\,dx$. Divide the first moments by $A$ to get the centroid. For polygon vertices $(x_i,y_i)$ in counterclockwise order, these become the shoelace area $A=\tfrac12\sum_i c_i$, centroid $\bar x=\frac{1}{6A}\sum_i(x_i+x_{i+1})c_i$, and $\bar y=\frac{1}{6A}\sum_i(y_i+y_{i+1})c_i$, where $c_i=x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i$. The boundary must be sufficiently regular and correctly oriented; this changes how an integral is evaluated, not whether the reflectance model is correct.

The first photometric-stereo lecture also states the remaining ambiguity before solving it. A single Lambertian observation fixes the angle between the normal and the light, so possible normals lie on a cone. A second light gives another cone; their intersection can yield two candidate lines, and the unit-normal sphere selects candidate directions. If reflectance is unknown, brightness scales with both albedo and normal. Define the scaled normal $\mathbf{n}=\rho\hat{\mathbf n}$; then three brightness observations become three linear equations $S\mathbf n=\mathbf E$. Recover albedo from $\rho=\|\mathbf n\|$ and direction by normalization. But the light directions must span three-dimensional space: repeating one source gives a duplicate row and zero determinant, while placing all sources in a plane makes one combination predictable from the others. In a controlled factory, calculate $S^{-1}$ once and apply it to each pixel’s three measurements. Sequential LEDs are one option; three colored sources can measure simultaneously through RGB channels, but a multicolored object’s reflectance confounds channel intensity with orientation. Horn mentions Cognex patents as an industrial motivation. For non-Lambertian materials, measure a known sphere under the three lights, tabulate observed $(E_1,E_2,E_3)$ by known normal, then numerically invert the lookup. The lecture distinguishes the image-formation parameter informally called albedo here from the more specialized astronomical definition based on total reflected power over all directions. The gradient coordinates follow directly from a local height graph. If surface height is $z(x,y)$, its first-order change is $dz=p\,dx+q\,dy$, where $p=z_x$ and $q=z_y$. Two tangent directions are $(1,0,p)$ and $(0,1,q)$. Their cross product gives a normal proportional to $(-p,-q,1)$, so normalization yields $\hat{\mathbf n}=(-p,-q,1)/\sqrt{1+p^2+q^2}$. Conversely, for a normal with $n_z\ne0$, $p=-n_x/n_z$ and $q=-n_y/n_z$. Every point in the gradient plane is an orientation, not an image location or a height. The origin $(p,q)=(0,0)$ means locally flat, and increasing radius means steeper tilt. Surfaces at different depths can share the same gradient-space point because it records orientation only. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-06 - Photometric Stereo, Noise Gain, Error Amplification, Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors Review|MITV-06]] (video 00:58:01–01:07:15)

For one fixed Lambertian light, write its gradient-space point as $(p_s,q_s)$, the slope whose normal faces directly toward the source. A unit normal and source direction have dot product proportional to brightness; substituting the normalized $(p,q)$ normal creates an equation with a square root. Squaring gives a quadratic in $p,q$, so equal-brightness curves are conic sections: ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas, or degenerate lines/points. At $(p_s,q_s)$ the incident angle is zero and normalized brightness reaches its maximum. If one brightness observation fixes a value $E$, then the matching conic is a whole set of candidate orientations. A second lighting direction changes the reflectance map and produces another contour; their intersection can still contain two candidates, which is why three noncoplanar directions are useful. Back-facing normals must be clamped with $\max(0,\cos\theta_i)$ because negative incident energy is not physical; the zero-brightness boundary marks the transition to shadow. The lunar example shows how a theoretically sound inverse can fail through geometry. Imagine an ideal Lambertian Moon, viewed from Earth over its monthly cycle. The surface is illuminated from many apparent directions, so it seems to provide repeated photometric-stereo measurements. Yet the Sun, Earth, and Moon motions lie almost in one plane. The light-direction vectors are coplanar or nearly so, making the lighting matrix singular or ill-conditioned. The lecture’s provocative consequence is that one cannot recover lunar topography from Earth’s changing illumination alone using this three-light method. The same eigenvalue test used for optical flow detects the weakness: one component of the scaled normal has little or no effect on the measurements. The analogy makes “independent views” precise: variety in light count is not enough; their directions must span the unknown’s space. Lambert’s cosine rule is presented as a phenomenological reflectance model rather than a universal law of materials. This setup helps explain how Lambert investigated illumination and motivated the inverse-square relation and cosine behavior. ### Measuring a reflectance map without assuming Lambertian material

Lambert’s cosine model is useful because it makes three-light recovery algebraic, but Lecture 7 removes that restriction by treating surface reflection as a measured map. For a fixed material and illumination, a reflectance map $R(p,q)$ assigns expected image brightness to every surface slope $(p,q)$. One observation $E_1$ selects an isophote $R_1(p,q)=E_1$; it usually leaves an entire curve of possible orientations. A second illumination produces another map, and the same patch’s true orientation must lie at the intersection of the two brightness contours. The construction works for a non-Lambertian surface if its map is calibrated. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-07 - Gradient Space, Reflectance Map, Image Irradiance Equation, Gnomonic Projection|MITV-07]] (video 00:00:12–00:09:21)

A sphere provides the calibration shape. Floodlight it so its boundary can be fit as a circle, estimate the center and radius at subpixel precision, then use each interior pixel to find the corresponding surface point. For a sphere, the normal is the normalized vector from sphere center to surface point; perspective projection gives the depth relation needed to recover local slopes $p$ and $q$. Capture the sphere under three controlled light directions, yielding measurements $(E_1,E_2,E_3)$ for every known orientation. These samples let a system build an inverse lookup from quantized brightness triples back to $(p,q)$. At runtime, the three measurements at a pixel index a table. The computation is a few array accesses and an output read, avoiding nonlinear iteration, though it returns local orientation only; the slopes still need integration to recover height. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-07 - Gradient Space, Reflectance Map, Image Irradiance Equation, Gnomonic Projection|MITV-07]] (video 00:09:22–00:18:10)

The lookup has a precise geometry. With fixed albedo, orientations form a two-dimensional surface embedded in brightness space $\mathbb R^3$, not a filled volume. Most brightness triples are therefore invalid and many grid cells stay empty. Coarse quantization can make distinct orientations land in one cell; store a weighted average or other local representative, and choose resolution with memory in mind. At 200 bins per channel, the full table has $200^3=8{,}000{,}000$ cells; this is why quantization and sparse occupancy matter. If the calibration sphere is white, its measured surface corresponds to albedo $\rho=1$. For darker materials, reflectance scales all three measurements along a ray from the origin, so extend the table to three-dimensional brightness space and store $(p,q,\rho)$. A known expected albedo then becomes a useful consistency check. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-07 - Gradient Space, Reflectance Map, Image Irradiance Equation, Gnomonic Projection|MITV-07]] (video 00:18:12–00:23:36)

Off-surface brightness measurements expose model violations instead of returning a false normal. A cast shadow makes one channel unusually dark; an incorrect albedo moves the triple to the wrong radial level; interreflection between touching glossy objects can make brightness too high. In the lecture’s example of overlapping shiny donuts, these departures from the calibrated surface mark boundaries: use shadow and interreflection regions to split one image mask into separate objects. To guarantee table coverage, one can iterate over every desired brightness voxel and numerically find a corresponding orientation rather than hoping calibration samples happen to populate the cells. This is an important difference between a reflectance model and a universal inverse: the table covers only observations the calibration and material family support. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-07 - Gradient Space, Reflectance Map, Image Irradiance Equation, Gnomonic Projection|MITV-07]] (video 00:23:39–00:26:17)

### A reflectance function includes viewing direction

For arbitrary materials, brightness depends on both illumination and viewing geometry. A bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF) records how incident light from one direction is reflected toward an outgoing direction, for a surface with a chosen normal. In full form, that is four angular degrees of freedom: incident zenith and azimuth, plus emitted zenith and azimuth. A gonioreflectometer samples these directions by moving a source or detector around a surface and can build a measured lookup for materials such as skin, where measurement may be more reliable than an assumed Lambertian formula.

Many ordinary materials are approximately isotropic around the surface normal: rotating the surface in its own tangent plane does not change the response. Then only the difference between incident and outgoing azimuth matters, reducing the lookup from four dimensions to three. Oriented microstructure breaks that symmetry. Iridescent hummingbird feathers use aligned structure and interference, so a male’s red throat can appear black from the wrong angle; tiger-eye stone and straight dark hair also show rotation-dependent highlights. The full four-dimensional model is needed. Helmholtz reciprocity supplies a symmetry: swapping incident and outgoing directions leaves the BRDF unchanged. This reduces calibration work by making half the directional measurements redundant. ### Shape from shading as a PDE, then a numerical surface

Photometric stereo changes illumination and solves independently at each pixel. Shape from shading instead tries to infer shape from one image using a known or assumed light and reflectance model. Let $z(x,y)$ be the unknown height. Its brightness prediction depends on slopes $p=z_x$ and $q=z_y$; write the irradiance equation as

$$
E(x,y)=R(x,y,p,q).
$$

This is a first-order nonlinear partial differential equation (PDE) for $z$, because $p$ and $q$ are its first derivatives. A single image brightness value constrains slope to a curve in $(p,q)$ space. Recovering an entire surface is therefore not the same as selecting an independent normal at each pixel: neighboring slopes must be compatible as derivatives of one height function. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-09 - Shape from Shading, General Case - From First Order Nonlinear PDE to Five ODEs|MITV-09]] (notes pp. 46–52)

The method of characteristics converts a first-order PDE into ordinary differential equations (ODEs) along curves called characteristics. In broad form, the PDE defines a relation among position, height, and derivatives; the characteristic equations specify how these quantities evolve along a curve on the image plane. The lecture notes derive five coupled ODEs for the general shape-from-shading case. The strategy is valuable because ODE initial-value methods are familiar and can propagate boundary information. It also exposes the limitation: the characteristic curves can intersect, become singular, or carry boundary errors into the interior. One needs a boundary condition and a valid reflectance/light model; brightness alone does not select a unique global surface in every configuration.

A characteristic strip expands a solved curve into a neighboring strip of surface. Starting from boundary values and slopes, update position, height, and derivatives along the characteristic system. Iterative solutions use the current estimate to improve consistency with the irradiance equation and integrability. The notes treat these as numerical constructions rather than a universal closed-form recovery. Step size, initial curve choice, noise, and characteristic geometry all affect stability. A robust explanation of any result must say which boundary data were known and where the method was numerically propagated. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-10 - Characteristic Strip Expansion, Shape from Shading, Iterative Solutions|MITV-10]] (notes pp. 52–59)

The notes give a practical way to avoid demanding a hand-measured boundary curve in one special initialization strategy. First find stationary points of brightness; second use second derivatives to estimate local shape; third create a small nonplanar cap around the point; finally start characteristic solutions from the cap boundary. The nonplanar cap matters because a planar seed at a degenerate stationary configuration can leave the initial-value problem underconstrained. This is a deliberately local construction: it gives a place to launch the characteristic integration, not a guarantee that the resulting global surface is unique or correct. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-10 - Characteristic Strip Expansion, Shape from Shading, Iterative Solutions|MITV-10]] (notes p. 59)

### Why the Moon’s full phase can look flat, and what profiles still reveal

The course uses lunar brightness to show why the Lambertian shortcut can fail dramatically. For Lambertian material, equal brightness on a sphere corresponds to equal incidence angle; those loci are circles around the light direction, projected as nested ellipses. A rocky lunar surface is better approximated by the Hapke-type model introduced in the lecture. In its simplified form, outgoing radiance depends on the ratio of cosines of the incidence and viewing angles, approximately $L\propto\sqrt{\cos\theta_i/\cos\theta_e}$. Equal radiance then requires $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s=C(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v)$, so the normal $\mathbf n$ lies in a plane through the origin. Intersecting that plane with the unit sphere gives a great circle; the isophotes follow constant longitude in the Sun–Earth coordinate frame. At full Moon, the Sun and viewer directions nearly coincide, making $\theta_i=\theta_e$ and the disk nearly uniform except for albedo markings. This explains why the full Moon looks like a flat disk even though its circular outline establishes that it is round. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-08 - Shading, Special Cases, Lunar Surface, Scanning Electron Microscope, Green's Theorem|MITV-08]] (video 00:27:23–00:42:20)

The same model gives a useful local shape cue. Convert the normal to height slopes with $\mathbf n=(-p,-q,1)/\sqrt{1+p^2+q^2}$. For the chosen solar/viewing geometry, the square root terms cancel in the ratio, leaving an isophote equation linear in $p$ and $q$. In slope space, each brightness is therefore a straight line. Two lighting conditions give two lines whose intersection yields a unique slope pair; unlike the Lambertian conics, the course’s specific Hapke construction avoids the two-solution ambiguity. Rotate the surface-coordinate axes until one isophote direction aligns with one axis. Then a single brightness measurement tells the slope along that axis even though it says nothing about the perpendicular slope. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-08 - Shading, Special Cases, Lunar Surface, Scanning Electron Microscope, Green's Theorem|MITV-08]] (video 00:44:23–00:57:40)

That one directional slope supports profile integration. Starting from a point with known relative height, take a small step $\Delta x$ along the informative direction and update $z$ by $\Delta z\approx p\Delta x$ (or use the corresponding rotated slope). Repeating the process traces a profile; on the Moon the lecture’s coordinate choice makes these profiles run along lines of latitude, so repeated starting points map local terrain such as a landing-site crater. The image gives no absolute height: adding a constant to every value on a profile leaves its slopes and brightness unchanged. Worse, independent profiles have unknown relative vertical offsets because the method supplies no cross-profile slope. They can be stitched only by additional assumptions, such as smooth neighboring profiles or rotational symmetry of a crater. Material variations and brightness noise also accumulate as integration error. ### A real non-Lambertian example: scanning electron microscope shape cues

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) pictures are legible because their brightness carries local orientation. A focused electron beam raster-scans the specimen; high-energy backscatter is possible, but the shading image in this lecture uses secondary electrons generated as the beam loses energy inside the material. Electrons created near the surface can escape and reach a collector. On a nearly front-facing facet, most secondary electrons are buried and the pixel is dark; on a steeply inclined facet, more are near an exposed edge and escape, so the pixel brightens. Summing the collector current makes the reflectance map approximately radially symmetric in slope space: brightness grows with slope magnitude but says nothing about gradient direction. One pixel still leaves an entire circle of possible $(p,q)$ pairs. The sample must also conduct; the lecture notes that specimens are commonly gold-coated because the microscope operates in vacuum and charging otherwise builds up. This reverses the Lambertian cue at the silhouette: SEM images can get brighter near steep contours rather than darker. Reversing their contrast does not necessarily make them easier to interpret, even though an observer expects a Lambertian-like cue. ### From a noisy needle field to one surface

Photometric stereo or another orientation method produces a needle field: estimated slopes $p(x,y)$ and $q(x,y)$ at every location, not height itself. In a perfect differentiable surface these are $p=\partial z/\partial x$ and $q=\partial z/\partial y$. Integrating along a chosen path gives

$$
\Delta z=\int_\gamma p\,dx+q\,dy.
$$

The height should be path-independent. For a small rectangular loop with sides $\Delta x,\Delta y$, summing the four signed changes and expanding slopes around the cell center gives $(p_y-q_x)\Delta x\Delta y\approx0$. As the cell shrinks, integrability requires $p_y=q_x$, the equality of mixed partials. Measurement noise breaks this condition, so different paths produce different heights. Green’s theorem makes the same point elegantly: if every closed-loop integral vanishes, the area integrand $q_x-p_y$ must vanish everywhere. The boundary-versus-area conversion is also useful in vision itself: area, centroid, and higher shape moments can be computed from a contour rather than accumulating over every interior pixel. A mechanical planimeter once performed the area version by tracing the boundary with a wheel. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-09 - Shape from Shading, General Case - From First Order Nonlinear PDE to Five ODEs|MITV-09]] (video 00:21:19–00:40:50)

Rather than trust one arbitrary integration path, choose the height field $z$ that best fits the noisy needle field:

$$
\mathcal E[z]=\iint\left[(z_x-p)^2+(z_y-q)^2\right]dx\,dy.
$$

This is a least-squares problem over a function, so the continuous derivation belongs to calculus of variations. The lecture first discretizes it to make every unknown explicit. On a grid with spacing $\epsilon$, each pixel height $z_{ij}$ contributes forward differences in both directions. Differentiate the total squared residual with respect to one height, taking care to rename the dummy summation indices before differentiating; confusing a summed index with the selected pixel index produces an incorrect equation. Only the local horizontal and vertical neighbors survive, which yields a sparse linear system. Its continuous limit is the Poisson equation

$$
\nabla^2 z=p_x+q_y.
$$

The left side is the Laplacian, the sum of second derivatives. Its five-point stencil has $4z_{ij}-z_{i-1,j}-z_{i+1,j}-z_{i,j-1}-z_{i,j+1}$, scaled by $1/\epsilon^2$. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-09 - Shape from Shading, General Case - From First Order Nonlinear PDE to Five ODEs|MITV-09]] (video 00:40:52–01:01:16)

Millions of pixels make dense Gaussian elimination impractical, but each equation has only a handful of nonzero entries. The lecture turns the sparse equation into a local iterative update: replace one height by the average of its four previous neighbors plus the image-derived correction involving $p_x+q_y$. This is a relaxation method related to heat diffusion. Neighboring pixels can be updated in parallel as long as one update does not read another pixel while it is being changed in the same step; a checkerboard or partitioned schedule provides that separation. Iterate until the residual is small enough. The solution has a gauge freedom: adding a constant to all heights leaves gradients and the Laplacian unchanged, so one height or a boundary reference must be fixed. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-09 - Shape from Shading, General Case - From First Order Nonlinear PDE to Five ODEs|MITV-09]] (video 01:01:18–01:09:12)

### Shape from one shading image when isophotes are straight

The Hapke-type reflectance map from the preceding section has parallel straight isophotes in slope space. Rotate image coordinates so the direction perpendicular to those lines is the $x'$ axis. The slopes transform by the same rotation:

$$
p'=p\cos\theta+q\sin\theta,\qquad q'=-p\sin\theta+q\cos\theta.
$$

Then brightness determines $p'$ directly, while $q'$ remains unconstrained by that pixel. This makes one image into a set of independent one-dimensional integration problems along rows of the rotated coordinate system. Accumulate $z$ along each row from an initial value and interpolate brightness into a slope at every step. An initial curve, not merely a single starting point, is needed to seed all the rows. Brightness still cannot determine absolute height; indeed, the rows may each have their own unknown vertical offset. Profiles can only be stitched together using external measurements or assumptions about cross-row smoothness or crater geometry. For this special material, the integration direction is determined by the known light geometry. The next general method will address reflectance maps without this straight-isophote property. ### Characteristics: tracing the full nonlinear shading equation

The single-image general case cannot usually read a complete gradient from one brightness. Write the image irradiance law as $E(x,y)=R(p,q)$, where $R$ is the calibrated reflectance map. To move to a neighboring point, the height increment is $dz=p\,dx+q\,dy$. The slopes also change, by $dp=p_xdx+p_ydy$ and $dq=q_xdx+q_ydy$. These derivatives are second derivatives of height, the entries of the Hessian

$$
H=\begin{bmatrix}r&s\\s&t\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}p_x&p_y\\q_x&q_y\end{bmatrix},
$$

with $s=p_y=q_x$ for a smooth surface. At first this seems to create three new unknown curvatures. The image supplies the missing relation: differentiate brightness, so $E_x=R_p r+R_qs$ and $E_y=R_ps+R_qt$. These are two equations for the three entries of $H$, and therefore do not determine curvature outright. The key is to avoid solving for all of $H$: choose the direction of travel to be proportional to the reflectance-map gradient $(R_p,R_q)$. For $F(x,y,z,p,q)=R(p,q)-E(x,y)=0$, substitution into Charpit’s equations gives $x'=R_p$, $y'=R_q$, $z'=pR_p+qR_q$, $p'=E_x$, and $q'=E_y$, where primes denote derivatives with respect to the characteristic parameter. Thus image derivatives supply the slope rates while reflectance-map derivatives choose the image direction. Their solution is a characteristic strip: a path in the image and surface that carries height and orientation together. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-10 - Characteristic Strip Expansion, Shape from Shading, Iterative Solutions|MITV-10]] (video 00:19:32–00:39:20)

This is a method for a first-order nonlinear PDE, even though its numerical implementation is a family of ordinary differential equations. The direction is selected adaptively from the local reflectance map, so its image projection (the base characteristic) can bend as the measured brightness changes. Given an initial curve, the strip family fills the surface. Each curve’s integration is independent after initialization, making the computation parallelizable, while the base-characteristic layout still needs monitoring: add curves where they spread too far apart and merge or discard duplicates where they crowd together. The course keeps numerical integration at forward Euler, because pixel brightness and finite differences are already noisy; a high-order ODE solver would spend effort resolving precision the measurements do not contain. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-10 - Characteristic Strip Expansion, Shape from Shading, Iterative Solutions|MITV-10]] (video 00:39:22–00:53:14)

The step parameter can be chosen to control how the characteristic family samples the surface. Divide the ODEs by the height rate to take equal height increments, which advances from one constant-$z$ contour to another. Normalize the image velocity to take equal image-plane steps, but this may let neighboring curves advance at different heights; the normalization fails if the image velocity vanishes. Normalize the full $(x,y,z)$ velocity for equal 3-D steps, with its own zero-speed singularity. A fourth choice moves between image isophotes at a controlled brightness change, using the image brightness gradient and reflectance-map gradient. These are reparameterizations of the same geometric curves: they alter sampling speed, not the ideal characteristic path. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-10 - Characteristic Strip Expansion, Shape from Shading, Iterative Solutions|MITV-10]] (video 00:53:14–01:00:50)

An initial curve need not come with a measured normal at every point. Along a known 3-D curve $(x(\eta),y(\eta),z(\eta))$, the chain rule gives $z_\eta=p x_\eta+q y_\eta$. The measured brightness adds $E=R(p,q)$. Together these are two constraints for the two unknown slopes. The brightness equation may be nonlinear, but the curve-tangent equation is linear, so the number of candidate orientations is finite for ordinary reflectance maps. A silhouette also gives a normal direction: under orthographic projection, the normal at the occluding boundary lies in the image plane and is perpendicular to the silhouette tangent. But its height slopes diverge at that boundary, so it is not a stable starting point for the ODEs. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-10 - Characteristic Strip Expansion, Shape from Shading, Iterative Solutions|MITV-10]] (video 01:05:28–01:12:56)

A more useful seed is a unique, global, isolated extremum of the reflectance map. For a white Lambertian ball under a source behind the viewer, the brightest point has its normal aligned with the source; in the SEM reflectance map, the corresponding known orientation is instead a minimum. The method calls either one a stationary point. At such a point, $R_p=R_q=0$, and the image brightness gradient is also zero. That makes it a poor literal starting state: all characteristic rates vanish. Instead estimate the local cap around it from the image’s second derivatives, construct a tiny curved initial curve consistent with that curvature, and launch the characteristics from the cap. A flat plane would fail because all its points would preserve the same stationary orientation. This seed matters perceptually as well: removing the highlight from a vase image makes its shape more ambiguous, and cropping away a stationary highlight from a blob can change perceived shape. ### Shape from shading: a local equation is not a global surface

Let a distant point light have direction $\hat{\mathbf s}=(s_x,s_y,s_z)$ and let a Lambertian albedo be $\rho$. With height-field normal proportional to $(-p,-q,1)$, predicted irradiance is

$$
E=\rho\frac{-s_xp-s_yq+s_z}{\sqrt{1+p^2+q^2}}
$$

for a visible, illuminated patch under this sign convention. Given $E$, the equation constrains $(p,q)$ to a locus. At each image coordinate, there may be multiple slopes on that locus. A smooth surface field must also satisfy $p_y=q_x$. This integrability relation couples neighboring pixels, but does not generally collapse all possible solutions to one. Boundary values, known points, convexity assumptions, or additional lighting/views may be needed. Shadows and attached-shadow boundaries introduce inequalities or discontinuities that the smooth PDE does not capture directly. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-07 - Gradient Space, Reflectance Map, Image Irradiance Equation, Gnomonic Projection|MITV-07]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-09 - Shape from Shading, General Case - From First Order Nonlinear PDE to Five ODEs|MITV-09]]

The characteristic method rewrites the PDE $F(x,y,z,p,q)=0$ as a system along a curve parameterized by $s$. The standard Charpit form is $dx/ds=F_p$, $dy/ds=F_q$, $dz/ds=pF_p+qF_q$, $dp/ds=-F_x-pF_z$, and $dq/ds=-F_y-qF_z$, where subscripts denote partial derivatives. These are the five ODEs named in the lecture title. A boundary curve supplies initial values for $x,y,z,p,q$, then the ODEs propagate them. This form also clarifies failure: when the characteristic direction becomes tangent or singular relative to the desired marching direction, numerical propagation can become unstable; when characteristics intersect, a single-valued smooth height function may cease to represent the solution. The characteristic strip method computes a local neighboring strip and must be monitored for such breakdowns. ## Surface descriptions and Gaussian images

Gaussian images and related distributions describe shape through normal directions, connecting local curvature to global geometry.

### Shape descriptions, surface normals, and curvature distributions

Recognition requires a representation that can tolerate pose changes while preserving useful shape distinctions. A raw mesh stores vertices and adjacency, but direct matching of vertex-to-vertex distances is brittle: tessellation density and sampling can differ even when two objects have the same shape. Generalized cylinders describe an object by sweeping a cross-section along a medial axis; they are useful for articulated forms but can be nonunique, as a sphere admits infinitely many axis/cross-section descriptions. Polyhedral representations retain vertices, edges, faces, and their adjacency, giving a combinatorial structure for matching. The lecture motivates a representation invariant to translation and insensitive to rotation, so pose can be handled separately during model alignment. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]] (notes pp. 166–170; video 00:25:02–00:39:18)

The Gaussian image maps a surface point to its unit normal direction on the unit sphere. Translation leaves it unchanged; rotating the object rotates the whole spherical pattern, so object attitude becomes a rotation-search problem in normal space. For a smooth surface, nearby surface area maps to a patch of normal directions. Where normals change rapidly, the Gaussian image spreads area over a larger spherical patch; high curvature therefore corresponds to lower area density on the unit sphere. Flat regions concentrate normals near one direction. The [[Surface orientation from image brightness|extended Gaussian image]] records this normal-direction distribution weighted by surface area, separating pose from location while retaining curvature information. It loses some spatial arrangement, so different shapes may still share similar distributions; the representation is especially useful for convex objects and needs additional constraints for nonconvex parts, occlusion, or missing surfaces. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]] (video 00:46:10–00:53:40, 01:25:42–01:29:36)

In two dimensions, the analogous map sends each boundary point to its outward unit normal on the unit circle. If arc length is $s$ and tangent angle is $\theta$, curvature is $\kappa=d\theta/ds$, or the reciprocal of local radius of curvature. A line segment has constant normal and contributes concentrated mass at one circle direction; a tightly curved arc sweeps a large range of normal directions over a short arc length. Thus curvature is inversely related to the density of the normal-angle distribution. For an ellipse $x=a\cos\theta$, $y=b\sin\theta$, the tangent and normal directions are not uniformly spaced in the ellipse parameter $\theta$; the corresponding normal-angle density reflects the semiaxes. A circle provides a check: its curvature is $1/r$ and its normal-direction density is uniform. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]] (video 00:59:01–01:22:39)

In three dimensions the Gaussian curvature $K$ is the product of the two principal curvatures, and the local area ratio under the normal map satisfies $d\Omega=|K|,dA$ away from singularities. Equivalently, the extended Gaussian image’s area density per unit solid angle is proportional to $1/|K|$. For a sphere of radius $r$, $K=1/r^2$ and the area ratio is $4\pi/(4\pi r^2)=1/r^2$, matching the formula. A saddle has negative Gaussian curvature because its principal curvatures have opposite signs; the signed normal-map orientation must therefore retain sign information rather than treating every surface as convex. The lecture closes by pointing toward integrated curvature: flat faces have zero ordinary curvature, while edges and vertices of a polyhedron concentrate curvature, requiring an integrated or distributional treatment. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]] (notes pp. 166–170; video 01:25:42–01:32:22)

### Gaussian images: representing shape by normal direction

The Gaussian image maps each visible surface point to its unit normal on the unit sphere. A flat face maps to one spherical point; curved regions spread normals over a spherical area. An extended Gaussian image weights each normal direction by the amount of surface area mapped there, connecting surface curvature to a directional distribution. In 2-D, the analogous construction maps boundary tangent/normal directions onto a circle; changes in direction encode curvature. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (notes pp. 171–184)

For a surface of revolution, rotational symmetry around an axis creates a structured distribution of normals. Direction histograms discretize the sphere or circle into bins and count or weight surface normals. This supports recognition from orientation statistics, especially when exact point-to-point correspondence is unavailable. But a histogram discards location and some topology: distinct shapes can share similar normal distributions. The representation is informative when paired with known symmetry or other geometric cues, not as a complete shape description by itself.

Regular polyhedra and tessellations divide the sphere into cells for discrete directional sampling. Desirable partitions keep cells reasonably equal in area and shape and avoid privileged directions. Poor tessellations bias the histogram because some orientations receive finer or more numerous bins. The notes connect these sampling choices to regular and semi-regular polyhedra and to the space of rotations: both problems require discretizing a curved domain without introducing avoidable directional bias.

### From surface area to a directional mass distribution

Lecture 23 makes the measure behind this representation explicit. The Gaussian map sends a surface point to the unit sphere point with the same outward normal. For a sufficiently small surface patch, let $dA$ be its object-space area and $d\Omega$ the area of the corresponding normal patch on the unit sphere. Gaussian curvature is the signed local area ratio

$$
K=\frac{d\Omega}{dA},\qquad G=\frac{1}{K}=\frac{dA}{d\Omega}.
$$

The extended Gaussian image (EGI) is therefore a density over normal directions: integrate $G$ over a region of the unit sphere and one obtains the area of the object whose normals map into that region. A highly curved patch sends neighboring normals across a relatively large spherical area and has low EGI density; a nearly flat patch concentrates substantial object area into a small range of directions and has high density. For a sphere of radius $r$, the whole object has area $4\pi r^2$ and the unit sphere has area $4\pi$, so $K=1/r^2$ and $G=r^2$ everywhere. The corresponding 2-D construction has curvature $k=d\eta/ds$ and circular-image density $g=ds/d\eta=1/k$; for a circle of radius $r$, $g=r$. These examples fix the direction of the ratio and prevent confusing curvature with its inverse density. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (video 01:25:42–01:32:17; ; video 00:00:33–00:08:33)

The 2-D ellipse calculation shows how a continuous signature can expose orientation without preserving boundary point identities. Start from the ellipse parameterization $x=a\cos\theta$, $y=b\sin\theta$. Its tangent is $(-a\sin\theta,b\cos\theta)$, so a normal direction is proportional to $(b\cos\theta,a\sin\theta)$. Normalizing this vector gives the location on the unit circle, while the rate at which that normal turns per unit boundary arc length gives curvature. The resulting $g(\eta)=1/k(\eta)$ is continuous and has alternating extrema aligned with the major and minor axes. Rotating the ellipse rotates its circular signature by the same angle; comparing a measured signature with library signatures can jointly test shape and recover its in-plane orientation. A circle is the limiting caution: its signature is constant, so its rotation cannot be recovered because the object has no distinguished in-plane direction. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]] (video 01:12:34–01:25:40)

### Corners, closed surfaces, and the zero-centroid condition

Pointwise curvature is not a useful description at a sharp edge. A cube face is flat, while the normal changes abruptly across an edge and at a corner. Integral curvature handles this by measuring the spherical area swept by the normals over a finite object patch. Smooth the corner, map its changing normals onto the unit sphere, and then shrink the smoothing radius: at a cube vertex the normal directions fill one octant, whose unit-sphere area is $4\pi/8=\pi/2$. That is the vertex’s integrated curvature even though curvature is undefined or singular at the unsmoothed corner. Along an edge, normals sweep a great-circle arc. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (video 00:00:33–00:08:33)

There is also a global validity test. For a closed polyhedron with face areas $A_i$ and outward unit normals $\mathbf n_i$, projected areas viewed from opposite directions balance. Since this holds for every viewing direction, the vector sum must vanish:

$$
\sum_i A_i\mathbf n_i=\mathbf 0.
$$

The EGI treats each face area as mass at its normal direction, so this identity says the spherical mass distribution has its centroid at the sphere’s center. The continuous counterpart is $\int_{S^2}G(\mathbf n)\mathbf n\,d\Omega=\mathbf 0$. A distribution whose first directional moment is nonzero cannot represent a closed surface; a missing back face is a simple way to violate the condition. This is a structural constraint on valid shape descriptors, not a normalization convention. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (video 00:09:00–00:18:22, 01:07:02–01:09:14)

### Solids of revolution and the torus counterexample

For a surface generated by revolving a curve around an axis, symmetry reduces the area-ratio calculation to a narrow belt. If the generating curve is parameterized by arc length $s$, its radius is $r(s)$ and its tangent-normal angle is $\alpha$. The object belt has area approximately $2\pi r\,ds$; the corresponding unit-sphere belt has area $2\pi\cos\alpha\,d\alpha$. Hence $K=(\cos\alpha/r)(d\alpha/ds)$. With the lecture’s sign convention $dr/ds=-\sin\alpha$, differentiation gives $K=-r''(s)/r(s)$. If the profile is given as $r(z)$ instead, the equivalent graph formula is

$$
K=-\frac{r''(z)}{r(z)\left(1+r'(z)^2\right)^2}.
$$

For a sphere, its profile is $r(s)=R\cos(s/R)$, so $-r''/r=1/R^2$, recovering the known result. This derivation is useful because the profile’s 2-D curvature and the circumferential radius together explain the 3-D Gaussian curvature, and the formula can be checked against a sphere before using a more complicated profile. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (video 00:30:31–00:46:34)

The torus tests what happens when a normal direction occurs at multiple surface points. Let $R$ be its major radius, $\rho$ its tube radius, and $\eta$ the angle around the tube. Its distance from the axis is $r=R+\rho\cos\eta$, giving

$$
K(\eta)=\frac{\cos\eta}{\rho(R+\rho\cos\eta)}.
$$

The outer tube has positive curvature; the inner saddle band has negative curvature; at the transition $\cos\eta=0$, the Gaussian map’s local area change degenerates. Two different torus patches can share a normal, so simply summing signed inverse curvatures can cancel contributions and erase useful shape information. The lecture instead motivates a discrete EGI operationally: each mesh facet or photometric-stereo pixel contributes its positive surface area as mass at its normal. A triangular mesh patch with edge vectors $\mathbf a,\mathbf b$ contributes area $\tfrac12\|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\|$ at normalized direction $(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)/\|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\|$. For a torus, contributions from both points with a shared normal are added as area masses. That choice keeps an orientation histogram meaningful, but it loses uniqueness: the EGI can depend on the product $R\rho$, so differently proportioned tori with the same product can share the same descriptor. Nonconvexity also creates occlusion: a single photometric-stereo view may miss some front-facing patches in a cavity, whereas the analytic model includes the complete surface. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (video 00:46:38–01:09:23, 01:10:32–01:15:10)

### From directional masses to matching and sampling

The EGI deliberately supports recognition and alignment without reconstructing the object. Translation changes no surface normals, so its signature is translation invariant. Rotation sends every normal through the same rotation and therefore rotates the spherical distribution in the same simple way: the representation is rotation equivariant. Align a measured and model distribution by rotating one sphere until their mass patterns agree; compare their residual distribution to decide whether they describe the same shape. For a body of revolution, the remaining spin about its symmetry axis is unobservable and should remain an explicit pose ambiguity. The lecture also cautions that uniqueness results for convex shapes do not make inversion an easy algorithm: classical existence/uniqueness results can be nonconstructive, and iterative polyhedral reconstruction by adjusting plane offsets is computationally expensive and can diverge when a face-area response reverses sign. Recognition can stay in descriptor space and avoid that reconstruction problem. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-22 - Exterior Orientation, Recovering Position & Orientation, Bundle Adjustment, Object Shape|MITV-22]] · [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (video 00:31:04–00:33:08, 00:39:19–00:50:59; ; video 00:58:19–01:02:19)

To build the descriptor from a mesh, compute each triangle’s area and oriented normal, then accumulate its area in the spherical cell containing that normal. A faceted approximation becomes a directional histogram; refining the mesh changes the number of contributions but not the total surface area represented. Histograms also connect this geometry to measurements beyond machine vision: parallel muscle or nerve fibers create concentrated orientation peaks, while the chaotic blood-vessel directions described for tumor tissue create a more diffuse distribution. In the camera pipeline, however, perspective, visibility and missing cavity surfaces can bias the observed histogram relative to the complete model. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (video 01:10:32–01:18:06)

Discretizing the sphere is not a trivial detail. Equal-area cells avoid giving some directions more statistical weight; similar, rounded cells reduce variation in quantization error with orientation; regularity makes indexing and storage manageable. Latitude-longitude bins over-concentrate cells near the poles. A brute-force nearest-cell assignment uses the maximum dot product between the measured unit normal and every cell-center unit vector, but that scans all bins for each sample. A projected regular polyhedron offers a repeatable partition and permits some rotations to permute bins exactly, avoiding repeated interpolation and the progressive blurring of resampling. Arbitrary rotations still cross cell boundaries, so matching has to decide how to interpolate or tolerate quantization noise. These are design tradeoffs between equal area, shape, indexing cost and rotation alignment; the final lecture introduces the regular and semi-regular polyhedra as candidate spherical grids. ### Gaussian image refinements and the meaning of a directional histogram

For a smooth surface, the Gauss map sends each surface point to its outward unit normal on the unit sphere. Gaussian curvature $K$ measures the local area distortion of this map: infinitesimal spherical area is $|K|$ times surface area, with orientation sign distinguishing convex from saddle behavior. An extended Gaussian image accumulates area associated with each normal direction; discretely, each surface patch contributes its area to a bin around its normal. A direction histogram without area weights records how many sampled points face each direction instead, which depends on mesh sampling density. These are different estimators and should not be conflated. [[Chapter Edition/02 - Graphics Vision and XR/36 - Source Atlas and Reconstruction Checkpoints#MITV-23 - Gaussian Image, Solids of Revolution, Direction Histograms, Regular Polyhedra|MITV-23]] (notes pp. 171–184)

For a sphere of radius $R$, normals cover the full sphere uniformly in area because Gaussian curvature is constant $1/R^2$ and each spherical patch corresponds proportionally to a surface patch. A plane has a single normal direction, so its Gaussian image collapses to one point; a cylinder concentrates normals around its equator, with zero Gaussian curvature along the axial direction. A solid of revolution has rotational structure in its normal distribution. These examples show why the Gaussian representation can recognize broad shape families, while also discarding position and some distinctions between objects. A direction histogram is stable under translation, but it cannot tell whether two matching normal populations were arranged in the same spatial order.

Regular and semi-regular polyhedra provide partitions of the sphere for directional sampling. The design goal is not simply “many bins”: equal-area coverage, bounded distortion, neighborhood structure, and rotational bias matter. A grid that overrepresents pole directions makes a recognition score depend on how the object is oriented relative to the binning. Refinement should subdivide cells in a controlled way. The rotational tessellation topic and the earlier rotation-space representation meet here: both discretize orientations on curved manifolds, and both need awareness that uniform-looking Euclidean bins are not uniform on the sphere. ## Finite-field projections turn image size into collision counts

Here “projection” means mapping a point set onto a line, such as $\pi_\theta(x,y)=x+\theta y$. This is a different use of the word from camera projection, which maps 3D coordinates to an image plane. The number-theoretic results below do not describe camera matrices.

### Projection as a collision problem

For a finite set $X\subset\mathbb R^2$ and a line $V$ through the origin, orthogonal projection is $\pi_V(x)=x-\langle x,v^\perp\rangle v^\perp$, where $v$ is a unit vector along $V$. A fiber is a set of points with the same projection; its direction is parallel to the kernel $V^\perp$. A small image means many collisions in the fibers. The subject asks how many directions can produce a small image and what structure makes those directions exceptional.

For the slope parameterization, set $\pi_\theta(x,y)=x+\theta y$ and $\pi_\infty(x,y)=y$. In the grid $X=[N]\times[N]\cap\mathbb Z^2$, a rational direction $\theta=a/b$ gives $b\pi_\theta(x,y)=bx+ay$ (up to sign convention for the projected axis), so the image has only $O(N(|a|+|b|))$ possible values rather than $N^2$. The resonance comes from integer arithmetic. This example motivates a structure-versus-rarity principle: a structured set can have small projections, but the same compression cannot generally persist over many sufficiently varied directions.

For a finite map $f:X\to Y$, write $r(y)=|f^{-1}(y)|$. Then $\sum_y r(y)=|X|$ and
$$\sum_y r(y)^2=\#\{(x,x')\in X^2:f(x)=f(x')\}.$$
Cauchy–Schwarz gives $|f(X)|\sum_y r(y)^2\ge |X|^2$. Thus a good upper bound for collisions forces a large image. This same move appears later as point–tube incidence counting and [[Additive energy and approximate structure|additive energy]].

For measures, replace cardinality by covering number $|E|_\delta$, the minimum number of radius-$\delta$ balls needed to cover $E$. A scale-$\delta$ set may have $|E|_\delta\approx\delta^{-s}$; local nonconcentration requires $|E\cap B(x,r)|_\delta\le Cr^s|E|_\delta$ at every $\delta\le r\le1$. The energy viewpoint and its projection integral are derived in the proof supplement below.

### Finite fields: exact tests and arithmetic obstruction

Over $\mathbb F_q$, define $\pi_\theta(x,y)=x+\theta y$ for $\theta\in\mathbb F_q$. If $S=\max_{\theta\in D}|\pi_\theta(X)|$ for $X\subset\mathbb F_q^2$, elementary coincidence counting proves $|D|\lesssim S$ when $S\le|X|/2$. The mean-zero line-indicator argument proves the independent estimate $|D|\lesssim Sq/|X|$ when $S\le q/2$. Both count all ordered pairs, with the diagonal separated explicitly; see the complete derivations below.

The prime-field grid example suggests the sharper inequality $|D|\lesssim S^2/|X|$, but it fails over extension fields: if $q=p^2$, $X=\mathbb F_p^2$ and $D=\mathbb F_p$, then $|X|=q$, $|D|=q^{1/2}$ and all projected images lie in $\mathbb F_p$, so $S=q^{1/2}$. The subfield makes many directions preserve a lower-dimensional arithmetic world. Bourgain–Katz–Tao replace a bare size condition with a sum-product expansion argument that excludes approximate subfields in $\mathbb F_p$.

### Exact finite-field collision and orthogonality bounds

For $X\subset\mathbb F_q^2$ and $D\subset\mathbb F_q$, let $\pi_\theta(x,y)=x+\theta y$ and $S=\max_{\theta\in D}|\pi_\theta(X)|$. The collision count for one direction is

$$C_\theta=\#\{(x,x')\in X^2:x\ne x',\ \pi_\theta(x)=\pi_\theta(x')\}.$$

If the fibers contain $n_z$ points, then $\sum_z n_z=|X|$ and $\sum_z n_z^2=C_\theta+|X|$. Cauchy–Schwarz gives $\sum_z n_z^2\ge |X|^2/|\pi_\theta(X)|\ge |X|^2/S$. Thus $C_\theta\ge |X|^2/S-|X|\ge |X|^2/(2S)$ whenever $S\le |X|/2$. Summing over $D$ gives at least $|D||X|^2/(2S)$ triples $(\theta,x,x')$. But for fixed distinct $x-x'=(a,b)$, the equation $a+\theta b=0$ has at most one solution $\theta$ unless $b=0$, in which case it has none because then $a\ne0$. So there are at most $|X|(|X|-1)$ triples. Comparing proves $|D|\le 2S$ (with harmless integer-rounding constants). The hypothesis $S\le |X|/2$ is what lets the diagonal term be absorbed; no prime-field assumption is used.

The second finite-field bound assumes $S\le q/2$. For each direction choose exactly $S$ affine fibers, adding unused lines if necessary, whose union covers $X$. Let $\mathcal L$ be this multiset of lines, so $|\mathcal L|=S|D|$, and let $F=\sum_{L\in\mathcal L}1_L$. On $X$, $F(x)=|D|$. Each line has $q$ points, so the normalized average of $1_L$ on $\mathbb F_q^2$ is $q/q^2=1/q$. Write $1_L=1/q+g_L$, where $g_L$ has mean zero. Then $F=F_0+F_H$ with $F_0=|\mathcal L|/q=S|D|/q$. The condition $S\le q/2$ gives $F_0\le |D|/2$, hence $|F_H(x)|\ge |D|/2$ for every $x\in X$. Using the **unnormalized** squared norm $\|g\|_2^2=\sum_x|g(x)|^2$,

$$|X||D|^2/4\le \|F_H\|_2^2.$$

For distinct lines, direct counting gives $\langle g_{L_1},g_{L_2}\rangle=0$ when they are nonparallel and $-q$ when they are distinct parallel lines. For the latter, they are disjoint and expansion gives $0-q-q+q=-q$. A line’s own mean-zero norm is $q-q^2/q=q(1-1/q)\le q$. Therefore, summing all diagonal and off-diagonal terms,

$$\|F_H\|_2^2=\sum_{L_1,L_2}\langle g_{L_1},g_{L_2}\rangle\le q|\mathcal L|=qS|D|.$$

The two inequalities imply $|D|\le 4Sq/|X|$. Constants are not optimized. If instead the norm is the normalized average $q^{-2}\sum_x|g(x)|^2$, both sides above must be divided by $q^2$; mixing the conventions would lose a factor of $q^2$.

With the unnormalized transform $\widehat f(\xi)=\sum_x f(x)e(-x\cdot\xi)$, the matching Plancherel identity is $\sum_x f(x)\overline{g(x)}=q^{-2}\sum_\xi\widehat f(\xi)\overline{\widehat g(\xi)}$. The factor $q^{-2}$ is the reciprocal size of the ambient group, not an optional convention. For a line $L=\{x:x\cdot v=c\}$, direct character summation gives $\widehat{1_L}(\xi)=q e(-c\lambda)$ when $\xi=\lambda v$, and zero otherwise: parametrize the line by one free coordinate; the resulting nontrivial character sums to zero unless its coefficient vanishes. At $\lambda=0$ this is $q$, the line’s cardinality. Subtracting the mean removes exactly this zero-frequency value. Why does orthogonality appear? If $L$ is the affine line $x\cdot v=c$, its indicator has Fourier support on the one-dimensional normal subspace $\{\lambda v:\lambda\in\mathbb F_q\}$. Subtracting its mean removes the zero frequency. Two nonparallel lines have distinct normal directions, whose frequency supports then meet only at zero; the mean-zero supports are disjoint. Plancherel makes their inner product zero. Parallel disjoint lines have the same nonzero frequency support and a negative correlation, which helps the upper bound. In $\mathbb F_q^3$, a line’s normal frequency support is a plane, and two such planes intersect in a line containing nonzero frequencies. Thus the exact orthogonality argument fails; the lecture’s repair groups lines by direction and bounds the overlap multiplicity rather than pretending the planes are disjoint. This is a useful example of checking the dimension before importing an orthogonality claim.

### Fourier slice identity and the low-frequency local-constancy step

Let $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^2)$ and project onto a unit vector $v$. The projected density along that line is the Radon pushforward

$$R_vf(s)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(sv+tw)\,dt,$$

where $w$ is a perpendicular unit vector. Its one-dimensional Fourier transform at frequency $\sigma$ equals

$$\widehat{R_vf}(\sigma)=\int_{\mathbb R}R_vf(s)e^{-2\pi i s\sigma}\,ds=\int_{\mathbb R^2}f(x)e^{-2\pi i (\sigma v)\cdot x}\,dx=\widehat f(\sigma v).$$

The change of variables $x=sv+tw$ has Jacobian one; Fubini is justified by $f\in L^1$. Thus a projection does not arbitrarily scramble Fourier data: it restricts the two-dimensional transform to the frequency line parallel to the projection direction. This identity is also called the Fourier-slice theorem.

The low-frequency estimate in the real tube method uses local constancy, not a pointwise bandlimited assertion without decay. If $\widehat G$ is supported in the ball $B(0,1/r)$, choose a smooth cutoff $\chi$ that equals one on this ball and is supported in a slightly larger ball. Then $G=G*K_r$, where $K_r=\check\chi_r$ is a rapidly decaying kernel at spatial scale $r$. Integration by parts in the inverse Fourier integral gives $|K_r(x)|\le C_Nr^{-2}(1+|x|/r)^{-N}$ in two dimensions. Majorize $|K_r|$ by a nonnegative integrable rapidly decaying kernel $\Psi_r$ and use weighted Cauchy–Schwarz:

$$|G(x)|^2=\left|\int G(x-y)K_r(y)\,dy\right|^2\le C\int |G(x-y)|^2\Psi_r(y)\,dy=(C|G|^2*\Psi_r)(x).$$

If $G$ is large on a set $X$ of unit balls, integrating this inequality over those balls converts the pointwise lower bound into an average of $|G|^2$ over their radius-$r$ neighborhoods. The maximum occupancy $N_X(r)$ controls how often any location is counted. This is the bridge from frequency support to spatial clustering; it is why the real theorem contains a local-density factor instead of only $|X|$.

For the tube decomposition, smooth each $1\times R$ tube at unit scale so its Fourier transform lies in the dual rectangle, of dimensions about $1$ in the tube-normal frequency and $1/R$ in its long-axis frequency. Split frequency into annuli $|\xi|\sim1/r$, dyadically for $1\le r\le R$, plus the lowest-frequency ball. The $r$-piece is locally constant on radius-$r$ spatial balls. Two such pieces are essentially orthogonal unless their tubes fit in one common $O(r)\times O(R)$ container: otherwise the tube neighborhoods are disjoint or their dual frequency supports are essentially disjoint. Each piece has amplitude about $r^{-1}$ on an area about $rR$, so its squared $L^2$ norm is about $r^{-2}\cdot rR=R/r$. If no container holds more than $N_T(r)$ tubes, summing pairwise inner products yields

$$\|f_r\|_2^2\lesssim N_T(r)|T|R/r.$$

This is a dyadic, scale-localized estimate. The logarithmic number of scales and the near-orthogonality errors are absorbed only after explicitly allowing the small power/log losses stated by the theorem.
