# PART 559 — ORTHONORMAL BASES

## 559.1 We already know what a basis is

From Part 512, a basis of an \(n\)-dimensional vector space is a collection of \(n\) vectors that is both:

\[
\text{linearly independent}
\]

and:

\[
\text{spanning}.
\]

For \(\mathbb R^3\), any three independent vectors form a basis.

For example:

\[
b_1=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
b_2=(1,1,0),
\]

\[
b_3=(1,1,1)
\]

form a perfectly valid basis.

But "valid basis" does not imply "convenient basis."

These vectors are neither mutually perpendicular nor all unit length.

That complicates almost every metric operation.

---

# 559.2 Orthogonal basis

A basis:

\[
B=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)
\]

is **orthogonal** when distinct basis vectors satisfy:

\[
\boxed{
b_i\cdot b_j=0
\qquad
i\neq j.
}
\]

They meet at right angles.

Notice this says nothing yet about their lengths.

For example:

\[
(2,0),
\qquad
(0,7)
\]

form an orthogonal basis of \(\mathbb R^2\), because:

\[
(2,0)\cdot(0,7)=0.
\]

But the vectors have lengths:

\[
2,\qquad7.
\]

So orthogonal does **not** mean normalized.

---

# 559.3 Orthonormal basis

An **orthonormal basis** adds unit length:

\[
\boxed{
b_i\cdot b_j
=
\begin{cases}
1,&i=j,\\
0,&i\neq j.
\end{cases}
}
\]

This combines:

\[
b_i\perp b_j
\quad(i\neq j)
\]

with:

\[
\|b_i\|=1.
\]

The word itself tells you:

```text
ortho  → orthogonal
normal → normalized
```

The standard basis:

\[
e_x=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
e_y=(0,1,0),
\]

\[
e_z=(0,0,1)
\]

is orthonormal.

But an orthonormal basis does not need to align with world XYZ.

A rotated frame can be equally orthonormal.

---

# 559.4 Why orthogonality automatically gives independence

Suppose:

\[
b_1,\ldots,b_k
\]

are nonzero and mutually orthogonal.

Assume:

\[
c_1b_1+\cdots+c_kb_k=0.
\]

Dot both sides with \(b_j\):

\[
(c_1b_1+\cdots+c_kb_k)\cdot b_j=0.
\]

By linearity:

\[
c_1(b_1\cdot b_j)+\cdots+c_j(b_j\cdot b_j)+\cdots=0.
\]

Every cross term vanishes:

\[
b_i\cdot b_j=0
\quad i\neq j.
\]

So only:

\[
c_j\|b_j\|^2=0
\]

remains.

Since \(b_j\neq0\):

\[
\|b_j\|^2>0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
c_j=0.
\]

This holds for every \(j\).

Hence:

\[
\boxed{
\text{nonzero mutually orthogonal vectors are automatically linearly independent}.
}
\]

So in \(\mathbb R^3\), three nonzero mutually orthogonal vectors automatically form a basis.

---

# 559.5 Why coordinates become extremely easy

Suppose:

\[
B=(e_1,e_2,e_3)
\]

is orthonormal.

Any vector:

\[
v
\]

can be written uniquely as:

\[
v=c_1e_1+c_2e_2+c_3e_3.
\]

Dot both sides with \(e_1\):

\[
v\cdot e_1
=
c_1(e_1\cdot e_1)
+
c_2(e_2\cdot e_1)
+
c_3(e_3\cdot e_1).
\]

Because the basis is orthonormal:

\[
e_1\cdot e_1=1
\]

and:

\[
e_2\cdot e_1=e_3\cdot e_1=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
c_1=v\cdot e_1.
}
\]

Likewise:

\[
\boxed{
c_2=v\cdot e_2,
\qquad
c_3=v\cdot e_3.
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
v
=
(v\cdot e_1)e_1+
(v\cdot e_2)e_2+
(v\cdot e_3)e_3.
}
\]

This is an extremely powerful formula.

An orthonormal basis lets us recover coordinates using only dot products.

---

# 559.6 Compare with a nonorthogonal basis

Recall:

\[
b_1=(1,0)
\]

and:

\[
b_2=(1,1).
\]

Suppose:

\[
v=(2,1)
=
1b_1+1b_2.
\]

Its basis coordinates are:

\[
(1,1)_B.
\]

But:

\[
v\cdot b_1=2,
\]

not 1.

The other basis vector contaminated the measurement because:

\[
b_1\cdot b_2\neq0.
\]

With orthonormal basis vectors there is no contamination.

Each dot isolates exactly one coordinate.

That is why orthonormal frames are computational gold.

---

# 559.7 The Gram matrix becomes identity

Part 541 gave:

\[
G_{ij}=b_i\cdot b_j.
\]

For an orthonormal basis:

\[
G_{ij}
=
\delta_{ij}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
G=I.
}
\]

This means all the complicated general-basis metric machinery disappears.

Recall:

\[
x\cdot y=c^TGd.
\]

When:

\[
G=I,
\]

this becomes:

\[
x\cdot y=c^Td.
\]

In coordinates:

\[
\boxed{
x\cdot y
=
\sum_i c_i d_i.
}
\]

Likewise:

\[
\|x\|^2=c^TGc
\]

reduces to:

\[
\boxed{
\|x\|^2
=
\sum_i c_i^2.
}
\]

The familiar Cartesian formulas are easy precisely because the standard basis is orthonormal.

---

# 559.8 Pythagoras extends to the whole basis

If:

\[
v=\sum_i c_i e_i
\]

in an orthonormal basis, then:

\[
\|v\|^2
=
v\cdot v.
\]

Expand:

\[
\left(\sum_i c_ie_i\right)
\cdot
\left(\sum_j c_je_j\right).
\]

Every \(i\neq j\) cross term vanishes.

Only:

\[
c_i^2(e_i\cdot e_i)
\]

remains.

Since each self-dot equals one:

\[
\boxed{
\|v\|^2
=
\sum_i c_i^2.
}
\]

This is the generalized Pythagorean theorem.

It is also a finite-dimensional version of **Parseval's identity**.

You do not need the functional-analysis version. The key idea is:

> orthogonal coordinates separate energy/length cleanly.

---

# 559.9 Dot products are preserved by orthonormal coordinates

Let:

\[
u=\sum_i a_i e_i
\]

and:

\[
v=\sum_i b_i e_i.
\]

Then:

\[
u\cdot v
=
\sum_i a_ib_i.
\]

So moving from one orthonormal frame to another changes coordinate numbers but preserves:

\[
\boxed{
\text{lengths}
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
\text{angles}.
}
\]

Why?

Because both are determined by dot products.

This is why rigid orientation frames in graphics, cameras and physics are expected to remain orthonormal.

---

# 559.10 What does an orthonormal frame mean geometrically?

In 3D suppose:

\[
R,U,F
\]

represent right, up and forward.

For an ideal camera orientation:

\[
R\cdot U=0,
\]

\[
R\cdot F=0,
\]

\[
U\cdot F=0,
\]

while:

\[
\|R\|=\|U\|=\|F\|=1.
\]

Then a vector can be converted into camera-relative components through:

\[
v_R=v\cdot R,
\]

\[
v_U=v\cdot U,
\]

\[
v_F=v\cdot F.
\]

That is the geometric meaning behind many later view-matrix constructions.

---

# 559.11 TBN frame connection

From Parts 556–558:

\[
T
\]

is tangent,

\[
B
\]

bitangent,

\[
N
\]

normal.

A clean tangent frame wants:

\[
T\cdot B=0,
\]

\[
T\cdot N=0,
\]

\[
B\cdot N=0,
\]

and:

\[
\|T\|=\|B\|=\|N\|=1.
\]

Then tangent-space normal:

\[
n_t=(x,y,z)
\]

corresponds to:

\[
\boxed{
n_w=xT+yB+zN.
}
\]

And inverse coordinate extraction is just:

\[
\boxed{
x=n_w\cdot T,
\quad
y=n_w\cdot B,
\quad
z=n_w\cdot N.
}
\]

Orthonormality makes both directions simple.

---

# 559.12 Numerical reality

A mathematically orthonormal frame can drift in floating point.

Suppose:

\[
R\cdot U
\]

should be:

\[
0.
\]

After thousands of incremental rotations you might get:

\[
2\times10^{-5}.
\]

And:

\[
\|R\|
\]

might become:

\[
1.00003.
\]

Now the frame contains slight scale/skew contamination.

Repeatedly treating it as perfectly orthonormal can gradually produce errors.

That motivates the next three Parts: construction and re-orthogonalization.

---

# PART 560 — CONSTRUCTING AN ORTHONORMAL BASIS FROM ONE DIRECTION

This sounds impossible at first.

One vector contains only one direction.

A 3D basis needs three directions.

So what exactly are we solving?

---

# 560.1 The input does not uniquely determine the answer

Suppose we are given unit vector:

\[
N=(0,0,1).
\]

Any pair of unit XY vectors rotated by any angle around Z can complete the basis.

For example:

\[
T=(1,0,0),\quad B=(0,1,0)
\]

works.

But so does:

\[
T=
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right),
\]

\[
B=
\left(
-\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right).
\]

Infinitely many completions exist.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{one direction fixes two rotational degrees of freedom but leaves free rotation around itself}.
}
\]

That remaining freedom is often called **roll**.

The construction algorithm therefore needs an arbitrary but deterministic convention.

---

# 560.2 Desired output

Given nonzero:

\[
v,
\]

we want:

\[
e_1,e_2,e_3
\]

such that:

\[
e_1=\frac v{\|v\|}
\]

and:

\[
e_i\cdot e_j=0
\quad i\neq j,
\]

with:

\[
\|e_i\|=1.
\]

We also want a chosen handed orientation.

---

# 560.3 Naive approach

Normalize input:

\[
e_1=\hat v.
\]

Choose some helper axis:

\[
h.
\]

Cross:

\[
t=h\times e_1.
\]

Then normalize:

\[
e_2=\frac t{\|t\|}.
\]

Finally:

\[
e_3=e_1\times e_2.
\]

Why does this work?

Because:

\[
h\times e_1
\]

is perpendicular to \(e_1\).

After normalization:

\[
e_2\perp e_1
\]

and:

\[
\|e_2\|=1.
\]

Then:

\[
e_1\times e_2
\]

is perpendicular to both.

Because \(e_1,e_2\) are unit and perpendicular:

\[
\|e_1\times e_2\|
=
1\cdot1\cdot\sin90^\circ
=
1.
\]

So:

\[
e_3
\]

is already unit.

Hence the basis is orthonormal.

---

# 560.4 Why `helper = X` is not robust

Suppose:

\[
e_1=(1,0,0)
\]

and:

\[
h=(1,0,0).
\]

Then:

\[
h\times e_1=0.
\]

Cannot normalize.

Even if:

\[
e_1
\]

is merely *almost* parallel to \(h\), the cross product becomes tiny:

\[
\|h\times e_1\|
=
\sin\theta.
\]

A tiny cross product amplifies floating-point noise when normalized.

Therefore helper selection must deliberately avoid alignment.

---

# 560.5 A robust axis-selection principle

Suppose normalized:

\[
n=(n_x,n_y,n_z).
\]

Choose the standard axis associated with the **smallest absolute component** of \(n\).

Why?

If:

\[
|n_x|
\]

is smallest, then \(n\) cannot be strongly aligned with:

\[
e_x=(1,0,0).
\]

Alignment with \(e_x\) is:

\[
|n\cdot e_x|
=
|n_x|.
\]

So picking the smallest component picks the coordinate axis least aligned with \(n\).

This maximizes safety against near-parallel helper vectors.

---

# 560.6 Example

Let:

\[
n=(0.99,0.1,0.05)
\]

after normalization approximately.

Absolute components:

\[
0.99,\quad0.1,\quad0.05.
\]

Smallest is Z.

Choose:

\[
h=e_z=(0,0,1).
\]

This is much safer than using \(e_x\), which would be almost parallel to \(n\).

---

# 560.7 Complete construction

Given nonzero input \(v\):

\[
n=\operatorname{normalize}(v).
\]

Choose helper \(h\) least aligned with \(n\).

Then:

\[
\boxed{
t=
\operatorname{normalize}(h\times n).
}
\]

Then:

\[
\boxed{
b=n\times t.
}
\]

We now have:

\[
t\perp n,
\]

\[
b\perp n,
\]

\[
b\perp t,
\]

with all three unit.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
(t,b,n)
}
\]

is an orthonormal basis.

---

# 560.8 Verify algebraically

Since:

\[
t=\frac{h\times n}{\|h\times n\|},
\]

cross-product perpendicularity gives:

\[
t\cdot n=0.
\]

Then:

\[
b=n\times t.
\]

So:

\[
b\cdot n=0,
\]

\[
b\cdot t=0.
\]

Magnitude:

\[
\|b\|
=
\|n\|\|t\|\sin90^\circ
=
1.
\]

Therefore all orthonormal conditions hold.

---

# 560.9 C++ implementation

```cpp
struct OrthonormalBasis3 {
    Vec3 x;
    Vec3 y;
    Vec3 z;
};

[[nodiscard]]
std::optional<OrthonormalBasis3>
basisFromDirection(Vec3 direction) noexcept
{
    auto nOpt = tryNormalize(direction);

    if (!nOpt) {
        return std::nullopt;
    }

    const Vec3 n = *nOpt;

    Vec3 helper;

    const float ax = std::abs(n.x);
    const float ay = std::abs(n.y);
    const float az = std::abs(n.z);

    if (ax <= ay && ax <= az) {
        helper = {1.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f};
    }
    else if (ay <= az) {
        helper = {0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f};
    }
    else {
        helper = {0.0f, 0.0f, 1.0f};
    }

    auto tOpt = tryNormalize(
        cross(helper, n));

    if (!tOpt) {
        return std::nullopt;
    }

    const Vec3 t = *tOpt;
    const Vec3 b = cross(n, t);

    return OrthonormalBasis3{
        t,
        b,
        n
    };
}
```

For valid finite input, the helper strategy should prevent the second normalization from degenerating.

---

# 560.10 What arbitrary choice did we make?

The input direction fixed:

\[
n.
\]

But the helper axis determined the orientation of \(t\) around \(n\).

So if the input direction moves smoothly and the selected helper suddenly switches from X to Y, the generated tangent can rotate discontinuously.

That can matter for:

- procedural shading;
- particle orientation;
- animation frames;
- camera rigs.

Therefore "robust against division by zero" and "temporally smooth" are **different requirements**.

A helper-axis method solves the first but does not necessarily solve the second.

More specialized basis-construction algorithms can improve continuity.

The canonical concept here is the construction principle; later systems may choose more sophisticated methods.

---

# PART 561 — GRAM-SCHMIDT ORTHOGONALIZATION

Part 560 solved one special 3D problem.

Now suppose we already have several linearly independent vectors:

\[
v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k.
\]

They span some subspace but are not orthogonal.

We want to transform them into an orthonormal set:

\[
e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_k
\]

that spans the **same subspace**.

This is the Gram-Schmidt process.

---

# 561.1 The core idea

Take the first vector.

Nothing comes before it, so just normalize:

\[
u_1=v_1.
\]

\[
\boxed{
e_1=
\frac{u_1}{\|u_1\|}.
}
\]

Now look at \(v_2\).

It may contain some component along \(e_1\).

Remove that component:

\[
u_2
=
v_2-
\operatorname{proj}_{e_1}v_2.
\]

Because \(e_1\) is unit:

\[
\operatorname{proj}_{e_1}v_2
=
(v_2\cdot e_1)e_1.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
u_2
=
v_2-(v_2\cdot e_1)e_1.
}
\]

Then normalize:

\[
\boxed{
e_2=\frac{u_2}{\|u_2\|}.
}
\]

Now \(e_2\) is perpendicular to \(e_1\).

---

# 561.2 Third vector

For:

\[
v_3,
\]

remove its component along **both** existing orthonormal directions:

\[
u_3
=
v_3
-
(v_3\cdot e_1)e_1
-
(v_3\cdot e_2)e_2.
\]

Then:

\[
\boxed{
e_3=
\frac{u_3}{\|u_3\|}.
}
\]

General pattern:

\[
\boxed{
u_i
=
v_i
-
\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}
(v_i\cdot e_j)e_j
}
\]

followed by:

\[
\boxed{
e_i=\frac{u_i}{\|u_i\|}.
}
\]

That is classical Gram-Schmidt.

---

# 561.3 Why does subtracting all projections work?

Take:

\[
u_i
=
v_i
-
\sum_{j<i}
(v_i\cdot e_j)e_j.
\]

Choose some previous basis vector \(e_k\), where:

\[
k<i.
\]

Dot:

\[
u_i\cdot e_k.
\]

Substitute:

\[
v_i\cdot e_k
-
\sum_{j<i}
(v_i\cdot e_j)
(e_j\cdot e_k).
\]

Because previous \(e_j\) are orthonormal:

\[
e_j\cdot e_k
=
\begin{cases}
1,&j=k\\
0,&j\neq k.
\end{cases}
\]

Therefore the entire sum collapses to:

\[
v_i\cdot e_k.
\]

So:

\[
u_i\cdot e_k
=
v_i\cdot e_k-v_i\cdot e_k
=
0.
\]

Hence:

\[
\boxed{
u_i\perp e_k
\quad
\text{for every }k<i.
}
\]

Normalization does not change orthogonality.

Therefore \(e_i\) is orthogonal to every previous vector.

This gives an induction proof that the entire output is orthonormal.

---

# 561.4 Why span is preserved

This is just as important as orthogonality.

We do not want to invent an unrelated basis.

We want the same subspace.

For \(u_i\):

\[
u_i
=
v_i
-
\sum_{j<i}(v_i\cdot e_j)e_j.
\]

Every earlier \(e_j\) lies in:

\[
\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_j\}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
u_i
\]

is a linear combination of:

\[
v_1,\ldots,v_i.
\]

So:

\[
\operatorname{span}\{e_1,\ldots,e_i\}
\subseteq
\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_i\}.
\]

Conversely, rearrange:

\[
v_i
=
u_i+
\sum_{j<i}(v_i\cdot e_j)e_j.
\]

Thus each original \(v_i\) lies in the span of the new vectors.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{span}\{e_1,\ldots,e_k\}
=
\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_k\}.
}
\]

Gram-Schmidt changes the **basis**, not the represented subspace.

---

# 561.5 Full worked example

Take:

\[
v_1=(1,1,0),
\]

\[
v_2=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
v_3=(1,1,1).
\]

These are independent but not orthogonal.

Start:

\[
u_1=v_1=(1,1,0).
\]

Magnitude:

\[
\|u_1\|=\sqrt2.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
e_1=
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right).
}
\]

Now \(v_2\).

Projection coefficient:

\[
v_2\cdot e_1
=
(1,0,0)\cdot
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right)
=
\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]

Projection:

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2}e_1
=
\left(
\frac12,
\frac12,
0
\right).
\]

Subtract:

\[
u_2
=
(1,0,0)
-
\left(
\frac12,\frac12,0
\right).
\]

So:

\[
u_2=
\left(
\frac12,-\frac12,0
\right).
\]

Its magnitude:

\[
\|u_2\|
=
\sqrt{
\frac14+\frac14
}
=
\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
e_2=
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
-\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right).
}
\]

Check:

\[
e_1\cdot e_2
=
\frac12-\frac12
=
0.
\]

Now:

\[
v_3=(1,1,1).
\]

First projection:

\[
v_3\cdot e_1
=
\frac1{\sqrt2}+
\frac1{\sqrt2}
=
\sqrt2.
\]

Thus:

\[
(v_3\cdot e_1)e_1
=
\sqrt2
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right)
=
(1,1,0).
\]

Second projection:

\[
v_3\cdot e_2
=
\frac1{\sqrt2}
-
\frac1{\sqrt2}
=
0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
u_3
=
(1,1,1)
-
(1,1,0)
-
0.
\]

So:

\[
u_3=(0,0,1).
\]

Already unit.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
e_3=(0,0,1).
}
\]

Final orthonormal basis:

\[
\boxed{
e_1=
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right)
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
e_2=
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
-\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right)
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
e_3=(0,0,1).
}
\]

---

# 561.6 What if input vectors are dependent?

Suppose:

\[
v_2=2v_1.
\]

After producing:

\[
e_1,
\]

Gram-Schmidt computes:

\[
u_2
=
v_2-(v_2\cdot e_1)e_1.
\]

But the entirety of \(v_2\) lies along \(e_1\).

So:

\[
u_2=0.
\]

Then normalization fails.

That is exactly correct.

The original vectors did not contain another independent direction.

Gram-Schmidt cannot manufacture dimension that was not present.

---

# 561.7 Near dependence is the dangerous case

Exact dependence gives a clean zero.

Near dependence gives a tiny residual.

Suppose:

\[
v_2=v_1+\epsilon.
\]

After removing the large component along \(v_1\), only a tiny difference remains.

Then normalization multiplies that residual approximately by:

\[
\frac1{\|\epsilon\|}.
\]

Any rounding error inside the residual gets amplified.

This is why Gram-Schmidt becomes numerically interesting rather than merely algebraically interesting.

That is Part 562.

---

# 561.8 Input order matters

Suppose you feed:

\[
v_1,v_2,v_3.
\]

Gram-Schmidt gives one orthonormal basis.

Change the order:

\[
v_3,v_1,v_2.
\]

You may get another.

Both span the same full subspace if inputs are independent.

Why?

The first vector is preserved up to normalization, so whatever vector appears first heavily determines the resulting orientation.

Therefore Gram-Schmidt is not a unique canonical map from a set to a basis.

The input is an **ordered sequence**.

---

# 561.9 Complexity

For \(k\) vectors each with \(n\) components, vector \(i\) is projected against approximately \(i-1\) previous vectors.

Total projection count is:

\[
0+1+\cdots+(k-1)
=
\frac{k(k-1)}2.
\]

Each dot/update costs:

\[
O(n).
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
O(nk^2)
}
\]

time.

Storage is roughly:

\[
O(nk)
\]

for the vectors themselves.

For a tiny 3D frame this is irrelevant.

For large numerical matrices it matters.

---

# 561.10 Classical C++ skeleton

```cpp
bool gramSchmidt3(
    Vec3 const input[3],
    Vec3 output[3],
    float minResidualSquared)
{
    for (int i = 0; i < 3; ++i) {
        Vec3 u = input[i];

        for (int j = 0; j < i; ++j) {
            // Classical mathematical expression:
            // subtract projection onto each prior unit vector.
            u -= output[j]
               * dot(input[i], output[j]);
        }

        const float u2 = dot(u, u);

        if (u2 <= minResidualSquared) {
            return false;
        }

        output[i] =
            u * (1.0f / std::sqrt(u2));
    }

    return true;
}
```

Notice something subtle:

inside the projections we used:

```cpp
dot(input[i], output[j])
```

rather than:

```cpp
dot(u, output[j])
```

That distinction leads directly to Part 562.

---

# PART 562 — MODIFIED GRAM-SCHMIDT AND NUMERICAL STABILITY

In exact real arithmetic, classical Gram-Schmidt works perfectly.

Computers do not use exact real arithmetic.

Repeated projection subtraction introduces rounding.

The original textbook formula and its algebraically equivalent rearrangement can behave very differently in floating point.

---

# 562.1 Classical Gram-Schmidt

For input \(v_i\):

\[
u_i
=
v_i
-
\sum_{j<i}
(v_i\cdot e_j)e_j.
\]

Notice every coefficient is computed against the **original** \(v_i\):

\[
v_i\cdot e_1,
\]

\[
v_i\cdot e_2,
\]

etc.

Conceptually:

```text
start with original v_i

measure all its components
against existing basis axes

subtract all those components
```

In exact arithmetic this is correct.

---

# 562.2 What floating point changes

Suppose:

\[
v_i
\]

is almost contained in the span of previous basis vectors.

The desired residual:

\[
u_i
\]

is tiny.

But the formula forms it by subtracting large nearly cancelling vectors:

\[
v_i
-
\text{large projection}
-
\text{large projection}.
\]

Each subtraction incurs rounding.

So the final tiny residual may contain substantial contamination along directions we thought we removed.

Then normalization amplifies the contamination.

Output vectors lose orthogonality.

---

# 562.3 Modified Gram-Schmidt

Instead of calculating all coefficients against the original vector, maintain a working residual:

\[
w=v_i.
\]

Then process one previous basis direction at a time.

First:

\[
r_{1i}=e_1\cdot w.
\]

Update:

\[
w\leftarrow w-r_{1i}e_1.
\]

Now \(w\) should have less component along \(e_1\).

Then:

\[
r_{2i}=e_2\cdot w.
\]

Update:

\[
w\leftarrow w-r_{2i}e_2.
\]

Continue.

Finally normalize the remaining \(w\).

So:

\[
\boxed{
w_0=v_i
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
w_j
=
w_{j-1}
-
(e_j\cdot w_{j-1})e_j.
}
\]

This is **Modified Gram-Schmidt**, MGS.

---

# 562.4 Why are CGS and MGS identical in exact arithmetic?

Suppose previous basis vectors are perfectly orthonormal.

After subtracting \(e_1\)'s component:

\[
w_1
=
v-(v\cdot e_1)e_1.
\]

Now compute:

\[
w_1\cdot e_2.
\]

Substitute:

\[
=
v\cdot e_2
-
(v\cdot e_1)(e_1\cdot e_2).
\]

But:

\[
e_1\cdot e_2=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
w_1\cdot e_2
=
v\cdot e_2.
\]

So in exact arithmetic it makes no difference whether the second coefficient is measured from \(v\) or the updated residual.

This is why both algorithms produce the same mathematical answer.

---

# 562.5 Why floating point breaks that equivalence

On a computer, we may have:

\[
\operatorname{fl}(e_1\cdot e_2)
\neq0
\]

even if mathematically they should be orthogonal.

Or subtraction may leave:

\[
w_1
\]

with a small residual component along \(e_1\).

Modified Gram-Schmidt measures later components from the **actual residual the machine currently has**, not from the ideal mathematical original.

This tends to remove errors as the computation progresses.

It is not magically exact.

But it is usually more numerically stable.

---

# 562.6 Code difference is tiny

Classical-style inner computation:

```cpp
Vec3 u = input[i];

for (int j = 0; j < i; ++j) {
    const float coeff =
        dot(input[i], output[j]);

    u -= output[j] * coeff;
}
```

Modified:

```cpp
Vec3 u = input[i];

for (int j = 0; j < i; ++j) {
    const float coeff =
        dot(u, output[j]);

    u -= output[j] * coeff;
}
```

One tiny source-code difference:

```text
dot(input[i], ...)
```

versus:

```text
dot(u, ...)
```

can create a major numerical difference for difficult inputs.

This is exactly why implementation-level understanding matters.

---

# 562.7 Illustrative float32 example

Consider three almost dependent vectors:

\[
v_1=(1,\epsilon,0),
\]

\[
v_2=(1,0,\epsilon),
\]

\[
v_3=(1,-\epsilon,0)
\]

with:

\[
\epsilon=10^{-4}.
\]

Mathematically they are still independent.

But they are extremely close to sharing the same first direction.

A representative binary32 calculation can produce classical Gram-Schmidt output whose worst off-diagonal dot product is on the order of:

\[
\boxed{4.5\times10^{-1}}
\]

in this adversarial example—a severe loss of orthogonality.

Running the sequential Modified Gram-Schmidt form on the same values can keep the corresponding orthogonality error around:

\[
\boxed{7\times10^{-5}}
\]

for the same style of float32 evaluation.

The exact numbers depend on compiler, instruction ordering, FMA usage and implementation, so do not memorize the values.

The important point is the magnitude of the difference:

\[
\boxed{
\text{algebraically equivalent does not mean numerically equivalent}.
}
\]

---

# 562.8 Why near-dependent systems are inherently difficult

Do not conclude:

> CGS is bad, MGS fixes everything.

Suppose:

\[
v_2
\]

differs from \(v_1\) by only a few representable bits.

The information describing the second independent direction is genuinely tiny.

No rearrangement can recover information that floating-point representation never stored.

MGS improves stability.

It cannot abolish conditioning.

That distinction is crucial:

\[
\boxed{
\text{algorithmic stability}
\neq
\text{problem conditioning}.
}
\]

---

# 562.9 Reorthogonalization

For demanding numerical work, even MGS may lose too much orthogonality.

One strategy is to perform another orthogonalization pass.

After obtaining residual \(w\), project it again against existing basis vectors and subtract remaining components.

Conceptually:

\[
\text{MGS pass}
\rightarrow
\text{residual}
\rightarrow
\text{MGS again}.
\]

This costs more arithmetic but can recover substantially better orthogonality.

In high-quality numerical linear algebra, other methods such as Householder transformations are often preferred for QR factorization because of stronger stability properties.

Those belong to later advanced-linear-algebra Parts.

Here the lesson is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Gram-Schmidt is not only an algebraic process; its computational arrangement matters}.
}
\]

---

# 562.10 What should an engine do?

For a 3D frame, full numerical-linear-algebra machinery may be excessive.

Suppose you have a somewhat dirty normal \(N\) and tangent \(T\).

Often you can normalize \(N\), reject \(T\) from \(N\):

\[
T'
=
T-(T\cdot N)N,
\]

normalize \(T'\), then construct:

\[
B=N\times T'.
\]

That is effectively a small specialized Gram-Schmidt step.

For arbitrary high-dimensional bases or decompositions, general MGS/Householder-type methods become more appropriate.

The engineering solution should match the problem scale.

---

# 562.11 Numerical validation

After constructing a 3D orthonormal frame \(e_1,e_2,e_3\), useful diagnostic quantities include:

\[
|e_i\cdot e_i-1|
\]

for unit-length error, and:

\[
|e_i\cdot e_j|
\]

for orthogonality error.

Rather than only asking:

```cpp
isFrameValid(frame)
```

during debugging, log the actual residuals.

Example:

```text
|x·x - 1| = 2.3e-6
|y·y - 1| = 9.0e-7
|z·z - 1| = 1.7e-6

|x·y| = 3.1e-5
|x·z| = 7.2e-6
|y·z| = 1.4e-5
```

That tells you whether the problem is scale drift, nonorthogonality, or both.

# PART 563 — HANDEDNESS OF COORDINATE BASES

Now we reach a concept that causes an enormous number of graphics bugs because people learn it as:

> some engines are left-handed and OpenGL/DirectX do different things.

That is too shallow.

Handedness is fundamentally a property of an **ordered basis orientation**.

---

# 563.1 Basis order matters

Suppose we have the standard basis vectors:

\[
e_x,
e_y,
e_z.
\]

The ordered basis:

\[
(e_x,e_y,e_z)
\]

is not conceptually identical to:

\[
(e_y,e_x,e_z).
\]

They contain the same set of directions.

But the **order** is different.

Handedness captures this orientation distinction.

---

# 563.2 Use the cross product

In a positively oriented conventional 3D basis:

\[
e_x\times e_y=e_z.
\]

This gives one orientation class.

Suppose instead the third basis direction were:

\[
-e_z.
\]

Then:

\[
e_x\times e_y
=
-e_3.
\]

That basis has opposite handedness.

So for orthonormal basis:

\[
(b_1,b_2,b_3),
\]

look at:

\[
b_1\times b_2.
\]

If it points along \(b_3\), the frame has one handed orientation.

If it points opposite \(b_3\), it has the other.

---

# 563.3 Scalar triple product gives the clean test

Take:

\[
\boxed{
h=
(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3.
}
\]

For an orthonormal basis:

\[
b_1\times b_2
\]

is unit and perpendicular to the first two vectors.

Therefore it must equal either:

\[
+b_3
\]

or:

\[
-b_3.
\]

Thus:

\[
h=
+1
\]

or:

\[
h=-1.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
h>0
\Rightarrow
\text{one handed orientation}
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
h<0
\Rightarrow
\text{the opposite orientation}.
}
\]

With the standard right-hand cross-product convention, positive corresponds to a **right-handed basis**.

So:

\[
\boxed{
(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3>0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{right-handed}.
}
\]

Negative:

\[
\boxed{
\text{left-handed}.
}
\]

Parts 564 and 565 will treat each system separately. Here we care about the underlying basis property.

---

# 563.4 General independent bases

The basis does not need to be orthonormal.

Take independent:

\[
b_1,b_2,b_3.
\]

The scalar triple product:

\[
\boxed{
(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3
}
\]

still has a sign.

Its magnitude is the parallelepiped volume.

Its sign gives orientation.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{sign}
[(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3]
}
\]

detects handedness for any independent 3D basis.

If the result is zero:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the vectors do not form a basis at all}.
}
\]

They are linearly dependent.

---

# 563.5 Why swapping two basis vectors flips handedness

Start:

\[
H=(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3.
\]

Swap \(b_1,b_2\):

\[
H'
=
(b_2\times b_1)\cdot b_3.
\]

Cross anti-commutativity:

\[
b_2\times b_1
=
-(b_1\times b_2).
\]

Therefore:

\[
H'=-H.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{swapping any two basis axes reverses handedness}.
}
\]

This is why axis reordering is not innocent when orientation matters.

---

# 563.6 Positive scaling preserves handedness

Scale:

\[
b_1\rightarrow sb_1
\]

with:

\[
s>0.
\]

Then:

\[
(sb_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3
=
s[(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3].
\]

Sign remains unchanged.

So changing axis length positively does not change orientation.

---

# 563.7 Negative scaling flips handedness

Now:

\[
s<0.
\]

Then the triple-product sign reverses.

Geometrically, negative scaling one axis reflects the frame across a plane.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{one-axis reflection flips handedness}.
}
\]

This becomes extremely important later with negative object scales.

---

# 563.8 Rotation preserves handedness

A proper rotation changes all basis directions but preserves their orientation relationships.

If you rotate a right-handed basis rigidly, it remains right-handed.

You did not mirror space.

You only turned the frame.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{rotation preserves handedness}.
}
\]

A reflection reverses it.

This distinction eventually becomes determinant sign:

\[
\det R=+1
\]

for proper rotations, while orientation-reversing transforms have negative determinant.

Full determinant theory is intentionally Part 629.

---

# 563.9 Concrete example

Start:

\[
e_x=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
e_y=(0,1,0),
\]

\[
e_z=(0,0,1).
\]

Then:

\[
e_x\times e_y=e_z.
\]

So:

\[
(e_x\times e_y)\cdot e_z
=
e_z\cdot e_z
=
1.
\]

Right-handed.

Now flip Z:

\[
e_z'=(0,0,-1).
\]

Then:

\[
(e_x\times e_y)\cdot e_z'
=
e_z\cdot(-e_z)
=
-1.
\]

Opposite handedness.

Nothing changed about X/Y perpendicularity or unit length.

Only orientation changed.

This demonstrates that:

\[
\boxed{
\text{orthonormality does not determine handedness}.
}
\]

There are right-handed and left-handed orthonormal bases.

---

# 563.10 TBN handedness revisited

Part 558 gave tangent frame:

\[
(T,B,N).
\]

Suppose:

\[
B=N\times T.
\]

What handedness does the ordered frame \((T,B,N)\) have?

Check:

\[
T\times B
=
T\times(N\times T).
\]

Use vector triple product:

\[
T\times(N\times T)
=
N(T\cdot T)
-
T(T\cdot N).
\]

For unit perpendicular \(T,N\):

\[
T\cdot T=1,
\]

\[
T\cdot N=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
T\times B=N.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
(T,B,N)
}
\]

is positively/right-handed under this convention.

If instead:

\[
B=-N\times T,
\]

the frame becomes left-handed.

This is exactly why mirrored UVs require a handedness sign.

---

# 563.11 Handedness is NOT "which axis is up"

A coordinate system might use:

\[
Y\text{ up}
\]

or:

\[
Z\text{ up}.
\]

Either can be right-handed or left-handed depending on the directions and ordering of the remaining axes.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Y-up vs Z-up is an axis convention, not handedness itself}.
}
\]

---

# 563.12 Handedness is NOT "camera looks down +Z or -Z"

Likewise, a camera convention using:

\[
-Z\text{ forward}
\]

does not by itself tell you the handedness of every coordinate basis involved.

You need to inspect the ordered basis orientations.

Statements such as:

> "-Z forward means right-handed"

without specifying the rest of the basis are incomplete.

---

# 563.13 Handedness is NOT memory layout

These are completely separate issues:

```text
row-major vs column-major
```

describes matrix storage/index interpretation.

```text
row vectors vs column vectors
```

describes algebraic convention.

```text
right-handed vs left-handed
```

describes basis orientation.

They can be combined independently.

Confusing these categories causes endless graphics folklore.

---

# 563.14 Why cross products become tricky across handedness changes

Suppose a transform mirrors X.

Ordinary direction vectors transform accordingly.

But recall from Part 546: a cross product is an orientation-sensitive pseudovector.

If:

\[
n=a\times b,
\]

and you reflect \(a,b\), then blindly comparing the transformed \(n\) against the cross of transformed vectors can produce an extra sign under an orientation reversal.

That is not cross product malfunction.

The cross product encodes orientation.

So converting meshes between opposite handed conventions often requires attention to:

- triangle winding;
- normals;
- tangent-frame handedness;
- cross-product order.

Part 564–565 will make the coordinate-system versions concrete.

---

# 563.15 Detecting a frame's handedness in code

For a general independent basis:

```cpp
enum class Handedness {
    Right,
    Left
};

[[nodiscard]]
std::optional<Handedness>
basisHandedness(
    Vec3 x,
    Vec3 y,
    Vec3 z,
    float degeneracyThreshold)
{
    const double orientedVolume =
        dot(cross(x, y), z);

    if (std::abs(orientedVolume)
        <= degeneracyThreshold)
    {
        return std::nullopt;
    }

    return orientedVolume > 0.0
        ? Handedness::Right
        : Handedness::Left;
}
```

But again:

\[
\boxed{
\text{a raw absolute threshold is scale-dependent}.
}
\]

The triple product has cubic units if all three vectors share a length unit.

For an already approximately orthonormal frame, the ideal value is:

\[
\pm1,
\]

so classification is straightforward.

For arbitrary badly scaled bases, use a scale-aware degeneracy policy.

---

# 563.16 A normalized orientation score

For nonzero basis vectors:

\[
s
=
\frac{
(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3
}{
\|b_1\|\|b_2\|\|b_3\|
}.
\]

Its sign still indicates orientation.

Its magnitude lies in:

\[
[0,1]
\]

for the relevant geometric relationship.

For a perfectly orthogonal frame:

\[
|s|=1.
\]

Near:

\[
s=0,
\]

the three vectors are close to coplanar/degenerate.

So this normalized quantity separates scale from orientation quality.

---

# HOW 559–563 FIT TOGETHER

We started with an arbitrary basis.

An orthonormal basis gives the ideal metric structure:

\[
\boxed{
e_i\cdot e_j=\delta_{ij}.
}
\]

That makes coordinate extraction, lengths, angles, projections, TBN frames and camera frames simple.

Given only one direction, Part 560 showed how to choose additional perpendicular directions and manufacture such a frame.

Given several arbitrary independent vectors, Part 561 generalized the same idea:

\[
\boxed{
\text{subtract earlier projections}
\rightarrow
\text{orthogonal residual}
\rightarrow
\text{normalize}.
}
\]

Part 562 then exposed the machine-level issue:

\[
\boxed{
\text{mathematically equivalent formulas can have very different floating-point stability}.
}
\]

And Part 563 added the final missing property:

Even if a frame is perfectly orthonormal, there are still **two orientation classes**.

\[
\boxed{
\text{orthonormality tells you metric quality;}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{handedness tells you orientation}.
}
\]

Those are independent properties.

---

# ENGINE-LEVEL EXAMPLE: REPAIRING A CAMERA FRAME

Suppose a camera has approximate:

\[
F
\]

forward and:

\[
U
\]

up vectors after numerical drift.

Assume forward must be authoritative.

Normalize it:

\[
F'=\operatorname{normalize}(F).
\]

Remove from \(U\) whatever lies along \(F'\):

\[
U'
=
U-(U\cdot F')F'.
\]

Normalize:

\[
U''=\operatorname{normalize}(U').
\]

Then construct:

\[
R=U''\times F'
\]

or another cross order depending on the engine's desired handedness convention.

You have just performed a small Gram-Schmidt-like orthonormalization.

The final cross order determines orientation.

So three Parts appear in one practical repair:

```text
Gram-Schmidt idea
→ restore perpendicularity

normalization
→ restore axis lengths

cross-product order
→ choose handedness
```

This is the mathematics underneath many camera-frame fixes.

---

# NUMERICAL FAILURE SCENARIO

Suppose:

\[
F=(0,1,0)
\]

and user's nominal up vector is also:

\[
U=(0,1,0).
\]

Then:

\[
U-(U\cdot F)F
\]

becomes:

\[
0.
\]

The desired frame cannot be constructed from those two directions because they carry only one independent direction.

This is not a normalization problem.

It is a **rank/independence problem**.

A robust camera system needs a fallback auxiliary axis.

The same underlying issue appears in:

- tangent-frame creation;
- look-at matrices;
- procedural particle orientation;
- joint frames;
- constraint axes.

---

# IMPLEMENTATION INVARIANTS FOR A 3D ORTHONORMAL BASIS

For:

```cpp
struct Frame3 {
    Vec3 x;
    Vec3 y;
    Vec3 z;
};
```

you want the following approximate invariants:

\[
x\cdot x\approx1,
\]

\[
y\cdot y\approx1,
\]

\[
z\cdot z\approx1,
\]

\[
x\cdot y\approx0,
\]

\[
x\cdot z\approx0,
\]

\[
y\cdot z\approx0.
\]

And if orientation matters:

\[
\boxed{
(x\times y)\cdot z\approx+1
}
\]

for a right-handed orthonormal frame, or approximately:

\[
-1
\]

for left-handed.

Notice how the last invariant cannot be inferred from the first six.

Both handednesses satisfy identical orthonormality tests.

---

# PERFORMANCE REALITY

For a 3D frame, none of these operations is computationally expensive in isolation.

Construction requires a few dots/crosses, one or two normalizations, comparisons and scalar operations.

The engineering concerns are more often:

- whether the frame must be rebuilt every frame;
- whether thousands or millions of entities need it;
- whether orientations can instead be represented by quaternions/matrices;
- whether repeated reconstruction creates temporal discontinuities;
- whether SIMD batching is useful;
- whether an already orthogonal representation can preserve the invariant more cheaply.

For general \(n\times k\) Gram-Schmidt, however:

\[
O(nk^2)
\]

becomes meaningful.

That distinction between fixed graphics `Vec3` math and general numerical linear algebra is important.

---

# DEBUGGING ATTACK TREE

Suppose an allegedly orthonormal camera/TBN frame behaves incorrectly.

First inspect:

\[
\|x\|,\|y\|,\|z\|.
\]

If they differ materially from 1, you have normalization/scale drift.

Then inspect:

\[
x\cdot y,\quad x\cdot z,\quad y\cdot z.
\]

If those are nonzero, you have orthogonality drift.

Then inspect:

\[
(x\times y)\cdot z.
\]

If its sign is opposite the expected convention, you have a handedness problem rather than merely an orthogonality problem.

If its magnitude is close to zero, the frame is degenerating.

If all mathematical invariants pass but rendering still appears wrong, investigate coordinate-space mismatch, matrix convention, or transform semantics rather than repeatedly renormalizing healthy vectors.

That diagnostic separation is important.
