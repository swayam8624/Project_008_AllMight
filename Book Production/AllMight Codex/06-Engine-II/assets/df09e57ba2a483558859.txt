# PART 580 — NORMALIZED DEVICE-COORDINATE INTUITION AS A VECTOR-SPACE MAPPING

---

## 580.1 The real problem NDC solves

Imagine you are writing a renderer.

Your camera might see geometry measured in metres:

\[
P=(3.7,\;1.2,\;-42.0).
\]

Another model might be at:

\[
(7000,\;20,\;-12000).
\]

Your screen might be:

```text
1920 × 1080
```

or:

```text
2560 × 1440
```

or a phone, VR eye buffer, shadow map, off-screen texture, etc.

You do **not** want the core rasterization logic to fundamentally care that this particular framebuffer happens to be 1920 pixels wide.

You want some intermediate coordinate convention saying:

> "After the camera/projection stage, every visible thing lives inside one standardized canonical box."

That canonical coordinate system is **Normalized Device Coordinates**, NDC.

Conceptually:

```text
arbitrary camera-space geometry
             ↓
       camera projection
             ↓
          clip space
             ↓
       perspective divide
             ↓
             NDC
             ↓
       viewport mapping
             ↓
      framebuffer pixels
```

NDC separates:

\[
\boxed{\text{camera/projective geometry}}
\]

from:

\[
\boxed{\text{actual output resolution}}.
\]

That separation is one major reason it exists.

---

# 580.2 What does "normalized" mean?

It does **not** mean:

\[
\|p\|=1.
\]

This is not vector normalization from Part 528.

Here "normalized" means coordinates have been mapped into a standardized range.

For X and Y, a common convention is approximately:

\[
-1\le x_{\text{ndc}}\le1,
\]

\[
-1\le y_{\text{ndc}}\le1.
\]

Depth convention depends on graphics system/projection convention. Common canonical depth intervals include:

\[
[-1,1]
\]

or:

\[
[0,1].
\]

The important conceptual point is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{NDC is a standardized coordinate region independent of framebuffer dimensions.}
}
\]

---

# 580.3 Imagine a camera looking at our spaceship triangle

Suppose after all camera/projection machinery, three vertices eventually correspond to:

\[
A_{\text{ndc}}=(-0.5,-0.5),
\]

\[
B_{\text{ndc}}=(0.5,-0.5),
\]

\[
C_{\text{ndc}}=(0,0.5).
\]

Picture:

```text
NDC

 y = +1
 ┌──────────────────────────┐
 │                          │
 │            C             │
 │           / \            │
 │          /   \           │
 │         /     \          │
 │        A───────B         │
 │                          │
 └──────────────────────────┘
 y = -1

x=-1                    x=+1
```

This triangle is now expressed relative to a canonical square.

It doesn't yet mean "pixel 728."

It means something like:

> halfway between the screen centre and the left edge.

---

# 580.4 NDC to screen space

Suppose framebuffer width:

\[
W=1920.
\]

An NDC X coordinate:

\[
x_{\text{ndc}}\in[-1,1]
\]

must become a screen coordinate in approximately:

\[
[0,W].
\]

First map:

\[
[-1,1]\rightarrow[0,1].
\]

Use:

\[
u=
\frac{x_{\text{ndc}}+1}{2}.
\]

Check:

For:

\[
x=-1,
\]

\[
u=0.
\]

For:

\[
x=0,
\]

\[
u=\frac12.
\]

For:

\[
x=1,
\]

\[
u=1.
\]

Then:

\[
x_{\text{screen}}=uW.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
x_{\text{screen}}
=
\frac{x_{\text{ndc}}+1}{2}W.
}
\]

For:

\[
x_{\text{ndc}}=0.5
\]

and:

\[
W=1920,
\]

we get:

\[
x_{\text{screen}}
=
0.75(1920)
=
1440.
\]

The exact viewport/sample-center convention can modify endpoint interpretation, but the mapping idea is this.

---

# 580.5 Why not project directly into pixels?

You could theoretically design a camera that directly outputs:

```text
x = 732 pixels
y = 418 pixels
```

But then your camera projection becomes coupled to output resolution.

NDC gives a clean architectural split:

```text
camera
   ↓
canonical visual region

viewport
   ↓
actual device region
```

Suppose tomorrow you render the same camera into:

```text
512 × 512 shadow map
```

instead of:

```text
1920 × 1080 screen.
```

The underlying projected geometry can remain conceptually identical in NDC.

Only the viewport mapping changes.

That is why the abstraction matters.

---

# 580.6 Where homogeneous \(w\) returns

From Part 578, a homogeneous point might be:

\[
P_c=(X_c,Y_c,Z_c,W_c)
\]

in **clip space**.

Then NDC is typically obtained by:

\[
\boxed{
x_n=\frac{X_c}{W_c}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
y_n=\frac{Y_c}{W_c}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
z_n=\frac{Z_c}{W_c}.
}
\]

This is the **perspective divide**.

Example:

\[
P_c=(2,1,3,4).
\]

Then:

\[
P_{\text{ndc}}
=
\left(
\frac24,\frac14,\frac34
\right).
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
P_{\text{ndc}}
=
(0.5,0.25,0.75).
}
\]

---

# 580.7 Why Part 578 mattered

Recall homogeneous equivalence:

\[
(X,Y,Z,W)
\sim
(\lambda X,\lambda Y,\lambda Z,\lambda W).
\]

Take:

\[
(2,1,3,4)
\]

and scale by 10:

\[
(20,10,30,40).
\]

Perspective divide:

\[
20/40=0.5,
\]

\[
10/40=0.25,
\]

\[
30/40=0.75.
\]

Same NDC point.

So perspective division removes the arbitrary homogeneous scale.

It selects the canonical representative where:

\[
w=1.
\]

Conceptually:

\[
(X,Y,Z,W)
\]

becomes:

\[
\left(
\frac XW,
\frac YW,
\frac ZW,
1
\right).
\]

This is exactly the projective representative idea from Part 578 becoming graphics machinery.

---

# 580.8 Very important: perspective divide is NOT a linear map

The title says "vector-space mapping intuition," but we must not commit a mathematical lie.

The operation:

\[
(X,Y,Z,W)
\mapsto
\left(
\frac XW,\frac YW,\frac ZW
\right)
\]

is not linear.

Why?

A linear function \(F\) must satisfy:

\[
F(a+b)=F(a)+F(b).
\]

Take:

\[
a=(1,0,0,1),
\]

\[
b=(1,0,0,1).
\]

Each individually gives NDC X:

\[
1.
\]

So:

\[
F(a)+F(b)=2.
\]

But:

\[
a+b=(2,0,0,2).
\]

Perspective divide gives:

\[
F(a+b)=1.
\]

Therefore:

\[
1\neq2.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{perspective division is projective/nonlinear in ordinary vector-space terms.}
}
\]

This distinction will become incredibly important when we later study matrices and perspective interpolation.

---

# 580.9 So what should "vector-space mapping intuition" mean here?

Think of NDC as a **canonical coordinate space** into which transformed projective points are mapped.

Before perspective divide:

\[
\text{clip-space homogeneous representation}.
\]

After divide:

\[
\text{a canonical coordinate representative suitable for clipping/raster-space reasoning}.
\]

The pipeline contains linear and affine transformations around it, but the division itself introduces projective nonlinearity.

Never collapse all of that into:

> "NDC is just another linear vector space."

That is too crude.

---

# 580.10 Real-life analogy: architectural blueprint

Imagine a building is 100 metres wide.

You draw it on A4 paper.

Instead of storing every wall as:

```text
237.4 mm from left paper edge
```

you might first represent it in normalized blueprint coordinates:

```text
left boundary  = -1
centre         =  0
right boundary = +1
```

Then whichever paper, monitor, tablet, or printer you use maps those normalized coordinates to actual device dimensions.

NDC serves a similar role.

---

# 580.11 Coordinate-space debugging

Suppose a vertex is:

```cpp
Vec3 p = {0.4f, 0.2f, 0.8f};
```

Those three numbers alone tell us almost nothing.

They could mean:

```text
object metres
world metres
camera metres
NDC
screen position
RGB colour
normal
```

A graphics engineer should mentally ask:

\[
\boxed{\text{Which space is this value in?}}
\]

If it is NDC:

\[
(0.4,0.2,0.8)
\]

has a completely different meaning than the same tuple in world space.

That space-tracking habit will save you repeatedly later.

---

# 580.12 Common NDC bug

Suppose you accidentally apply viewport mapping to clip-space X before dividing by W.

Wrong conceptual order:

```text
clip coordinates
      ↓
map X to pixels
      ↓
divide somehow later
```

Correct:

```text
clip coordinates
      ↓
divide X,Y,Z by W
      ↓
NDC
      ↓
viewport map
```

Perspective division changes the location nonlinearly, so order matters.

}

# PART 581 — DUAL VECTORS AND COVECTORS

This is probably the first Part here that can feel abstract.

So we will build it from a physical question rather than a definition.

---

# 581.1 You already know vectors

Suppose a displacement is:

\[
v=(3,2).
\]

You might interpret it as:

```text
3 metres east
2 metres north.
```

That is a vector.

It represents:

\[
\boxed{\text{a displacement/direction-like object}.}
\]

Now ask a different question:

> How much altitude does this displacement gain?

Suppose terrain altitude changes by:

```text
2 metres of altitude per metre east
1 metre of altitude per metre north.
```

Then a movement:

\[
v=(3,2)
\]

changes altitude by:

\[
2(3)+1(2)=8.
\]

The thing:

\[
(2,1)
\]

here is not being used as another displacement.

It is being used as a **measurement rule**.

It eats a vector and produces a scalar.

That is the intuition of a **covector**.

---

# 581.2 Vector versus covector

A vector is something like:

\[
v.
\]

A covector is something like:

\[
\omega
\]

that acts on a vector:

\[
\boxed{
\omega(v)\in\mathbb R.
}
\]

Example:

\[
\omega(x,y)=2x+y.
\]

Then:

\[
\omega(3,2)=8.
\]

This is a completely different semantic role.

Vector:

> "Move this way."

Covector:

> "Measure how much this movement changes the quantity I care about."

---

# 581.3 Another real-life analogy: price

Suppose a shopping vector records quantities:

\[
q=
\begin{pmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{pmatrix}
\]

meaning:

```text
3 apples
5 oranges.
```

A price covector might be:

```text
₹20/apple
₹30/orange.
```

Apply it:

\[
20(3)+30(5)=210.
\]

The quantity vector and price rule interact to produce total cost.

The price rule is not "another basket."

It belongs to the **dual role**.

---

# 581.4 What is the dual space?

Given vector space:

\[
V,
\]

the **dual space**:

\[
V^*
\]

is the collection of all linear scalar-valued measurement rules on V.

Formally:

\[
\boxed{
V^*
=
\{\omega:V\rightarrow\mathbb R
\mid
\omega\text{ linear}\}.
}
\]

An element:

\[
\omega\in V^*
\]

is a covector/linear functional.

Part 583 will study linear functionals directly.

For now:

\[
\boxed{
\text{dual space = space of linear measurements of vectors}.
}
\]

---

# 581.5 Why do vectors and covectors often look identical in code?

In ordinary Euclidean \(\mathbb R^3\), a fixed vector:

\[
n
\]

can define measurement:

\[
\omega_n(v)=n\cdot v.
\]

Example:

\[
n=(0,1,0).
\]

Then:

\[
n\cdot v
\]

extracts vertical component.

So a vector has apparently become a measurement rule.

Because Euclidean space has a dot product, we can associate vectors with covectors.

This is why beginners rarely notice there are two different concepts.

But the identification depends on the metric.

Conceptually:

\[
\boxed{
\text{vector}
\neq
\text{covector}
}
\]

even if a Euclidean dot product allows convenient conversion between them.

---

# 581.6 The ruler/grid intuition

Imagine graph paper.

A vector is an arrow moving across the paper.

A covector can be imagined as a family of parallel equally spaced lines:

```text
/////////////////////
/////////////////////
/////////////////////
/////////////////////
```

Crossing these lines increases the measured scalar.

A vector tells you where you move.

The covector tells you:

> how many measurement contours did that movement cross?

If the vector moves parallel to the contours:

\[
\omega(v)=0.
\]

If it crosses many contours:

\[
|\omega(v)|
\]

is large.

This intuition becomes perfect for surface normals.

---

# 581.7 Plane equations are covector-like

Consider plane through origin:

\[
2x+y-z=0.
\]

Define:

\[
\omega(x,y,z)=2x+y-z.
\]

Every vector lying inside the plane satisfies:

\[
\omega(v)=0.
\]

Vectors on one side produce positive values.

Other side negative.

So the coefficients:

\[
(2,1,-1)
\]

are naturally acting as a covector defining a plane.

This is why normals and plane equations are deeply connected to dual vectors.

---

# 581.8 Why call it "dual"?

Suppose basis vectors:

\[
e_1,e_2,e_3.
\]

We can construct measurement rules:

\[
e^1,e^2,e^3
\]

such that:

\[
\boxed{
e^i(e_j)=
\delta^i_j.
}
\]

Meaning:

\[
e^1(e_1)=1,
\]

\[
e^1(e_2)=0,
\]

\[
e^1(e_3)=0.
\]

So \(e^1\) extracts the coefficient along \(e_1\).

Likewise for the others.

These are called the **dual basis**.

The superscript notation is commonly used to distinguish covector basis objects from vector basis objects.

---

# 581.9 In orthonormal Cartesian space the distinction is hidden

For:

\[
e_x=(1,0,0),
\]

the covector extracting X is numerically represented as:

\[
(1,0,0).
\]

Same numbers.

So you start believing:

\[
\text{vector}=\text{covector}.
\]

But this is a special convenience of an orthonormal Euclidean basis.

Once basis vectors are scaled or skewed, their coordinate transformation behavior differs.

---

# 581.10 The scaling example that makes covectors click

Suppose old coordinate basis uses metres.

A vector has X displacement:

\[
v_x=4\text{ m}.
\]

Now choose a new basis where one coordinate unit equals:

\[
2\text{ m}.
\]

The same physical displacement now has coordinate:

\[
v_x'=2.
\]

So vector coordinate went:

\[
4\rightarrow2.
\]

Now consider a covector measuring:

\[
3\text{ units of temperature change per metre}.
\]

In old coordinates:

\[
\omega_x=3.
\]

If one new coordinate unit represents 2 metres, then moving one new coordinate changes temperature by:

\[
6.
\]

So covector coordinate goes:

\[
3\rightarrow6.
\]

Notice:

```text
vector coordinate:
4 → 2

covector coordinate:
3 → 6
```

They transform **oppositely**.

Why?

Because the physical scalar measurement must stay unchanged:

\[
3\times4=12.
\]

In new coordinates:

\[
6\times2=12.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{vector components and covector components compensate inversely}.
}
\]

This is the seed of why normals use inverse-transpose transforms.

---

# 581.11 Mastery gate 581

You should be able to explain:

\[
\boxed{
\text{vector = geometric displacement/direction}
}
\]

versus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{covector = linear scalar measurement rule}.
}
\]

You should be able to give examples:

\[
\text{velocity}
\]

versus:

\[
\text{measurement of velocity along some axis}.
\]

And you should understand why Euclidean dot products let a vector masquerade as a covector—but why their transformation laws are fundamentally different.

# PART 582 — WHY SURFACE NORMALS TRANSFORM DIFFERENTLY FROM POSITIONS

This Part is one of the most important transform facts in graphics.

If you only memorize:

> normals use inverse transpose

you will forget it or misuse it.

We are deriving exactly **why**.

---

# 582.1 Start from what a surface normal IS

A surface normal is not fundamentally defined as:

> "a vector sticking out of a polygon."

It is defined through a constraint.

If:

\[
t
\]

is any tangent vector of a surface and:

\[
n
\]

is a normal, then:

\[
\boxed{
n\cdot t=0.
}
\]

That orthogonality condition is the meaning we must preserve after transforming the surface.

---

# 582.2 Our spaceship triangle

Take a surface tangent:

\[
t=(1,1).
\]

A perpendicular normal is:

\[
n=(1,-1).
\]

Check:

\[
t\cdot n
=
1(1)+1(-1)
=
0.
\]

Picture:

```text
     n
    ↗
   /
  /
 ●────────? actually tangent slopes upward
  \
   \
    → t
```

Now stretch the spaceship model horizontally by factor 2.

Transformation:

\[
(x,y)\rightarrow(2x,y).
\]

The tangent is an ordinary direction/displacement, so:

\[
t=(1,1)
\]

becomes:

\[
\boxed{
t'=(2,1).
}
\]

---

# 582.3 Naively transform normal the same way

If we do the same to:

\[
n=(1,-1),
\]

we get:

\[
n'_{\text{naive}}=(2,-1).
\]

Check perpendicularity:

\[
(2,1)\cdot(2,-1)
=
4-1
=
3.
\]

Not zero.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{using the same nonuniform scale on a normal breaks its defining property}.
}
\]

This is the entire reason special normal transformation exists.

---

# 582.4 What should the correct new normal be?

We need some:

\[
n'=(a,b)
\]

satisfying:

\[
n'\cdot t'=0.
\]

Since:

\[
t'=(2,1),
\]

we require:

\[
2a+b=0.
\]

Choose:

\[
a=1.
\]

Then:

\[
b=-2.
\]

So a valid transformed normal direction is:

\[
\boxed{
n'=(1,-2).
}
\]

Compare scaling:

Original normal:

\[
(1,-1).
\]

Transformation effectively changed:

\[
x\text{ coefficient by }1/2,
\]

while Y coefficient remained unchanged, up to overall normalization/scale.

That reciprocal relationship is the clue.

---

# 582.5 Why reciprocal scale?

Surface stretch:

\[
x\rightarrow2x.
\]

Tangent X component doubles.

To keep:

\[
n\cdot t=0,
\]

the normal's X measurement coefficient must halve.

Exactly like the vector/covector scaling example in Part 581.

This is why a normal behaves covector-like.

---

# 582.6 General derivation

Let the surface transformation's linear part be:

\[
A.
\]

A tangent transforms:

\[
\boxed{
t'=At.
}
\]

We need transformed normal \(n'\) such that:

\[
\boxed{
n'\cdot t'=0
}
\]

whenever:

\[
n\cdot t=0.
\]

Substitute:

\[
n'\cdot At=0.
\]

Writing dot-product pairing algebraically, we want to move A from the tangent side to the normal side.

The transformation that accomplishes this is the **inverse transpose**:

\[
\boxed{
n'=A^{-T}n.
}
\]

Check:

\[
n'^T t'
=
(A^{-T}n)^T(At).
\]

Transpose reverses order:

\[
=
n^T A^{-1}At.
\]

Since:

\[
A^{-1}A=I,
\]

we get:

\[
=
n^Tt.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
n'^Tt'=n^Tt.
}
\]

So if original was zero:

\[
n^Tt=0,
\]

then transformed remains zero.

That is the proof.

---

# 582.7 Why inverse?

Because tangents get stretched by A.

Normal measurement coefficients must compensate for that distortion.

If X stretches by 2:

\[
A_x=2,
\]

normal X coefficient effectively scales by:

\[
1/2.
\]

That is the inverse part.

---

# 582.8 Why transpose?

Because normals are acting as linear measurement rules—covectors.

The transpose is the coordinate machinery that moves a linear map across the vector/covector pairing.

We will make that idea more explicit in Part 583.

---

# 582.9 Translation does not apply to normals

Suppose spaceship moves 100 metres right.

Triangle point:

\[
P
\]

must become:

\[
P+t.
\]

Normal orientation should not become:

\[
N+t.
\]

That makes no geometric sense.

Normals represent orientation/constraints, not locations.

This is already reflected by homogeneous semantics:

```text
point:
w = 1

ordinary direction:
w = 0
```

Normals are also translation-insensitive, although under general linear transforms they are **not simply ordinary directions**.

---

# 582.10 Why rotation looks deceptively easy

For a pure rotation \(R\):

\[
R^{-1}=R^T
\]

for an orthogonal rotation.

Therefore:

\[
R^{-T}=R.
\]

So:

\[
n'=Rn.
\]

Normals and ordinary vectors transform identically under pure rotation.

That is why the bug often stays hidden.

Likewise uniform scaling changes only normal magnitude after reciprocal scaling, and if you normalize afterward the direction looks correct.

The failure becomes obvious under:

\[
\boxed{\text{nonuniform scale}}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{\text{shear}}.
\]

---

# 582.11 Real engine failure

Imagine a sphere mesh.

Scale object:

```text
X × 5
Y × 1
Z × 1
```

Now it becomes an ellipsoid.

If you transform normals like positions/directions using the same scale, the normals no longer point perpendicular to the ellipsoid.

Lighting becomes visibly wrong:

```text
highlights slide strangely
surface looks dented
specular lobes distort
```

Geometry itself can look correct while lighting is broken.

That is the classic symptom of incorrect normal transformation.

---

# 582.12 Why normalize afterward?

Even correct:

\[
A^{-T}n
\]

generally does not preserve unit magnitude.

But shading formulas commonly expect:

\[
\|n\|=1.
\]

So typical conceptual flow:

\[
\boxed{
n'
=
\operatorname{normalize}
(A^{-T}n).
}
\]

The inverse-transpose preserves the correct perpendicular direction.

Normalization restores the unit-length invariant.

They solve different problems.

---

# 582.13 Singular transformation problem

Suppose scale X is zero:

\[
(x,y,z)\rightarrow(0,y,z).
\]

The transformation crushes 3D space into a lower-dimensional shape.

There is no inverse.

So:

\[
A^{-T}
\]

does not exist.

This is not just a library failure.

The geometry itself lost dimension.

A surface may collapse.

Its old normal information cannot in general be transformed uniquely.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{singular transform}
\rightarrow
\text{normal transform may be undefined}.
}
\]

---

# 582.14 Mastery gate 582

You must be able to recreate the simple example:

\[
t=(1,1),
\quad
n=(1,-1).
\]

Scale X by 2:

\[
t'=(2,1).
\]

Naive normal:

\[
(2,-1)
\]

fails:

\[
3\neq0.
\]

Correct direction:

\[
(1,-2)
\]

satisfies:

\[
2-2=0.
\]

Then explain that the general law is:

\[
\boxed{
n'=A^{-T}n.
}
\]

If you forget the formula, you should be able to rederive it from:

\[
\boxed{
n\cdot t=0.
}
\]

---

# PART 583 — LINEAR FUNCTIONALS

Part 581 introduced covectors.

Now we formalize the actual thing they do.

---

# 583.1 What is a function?

A function takes input and returns output:

\[
f:X\rightarrow Y.
\]

Example:

\[
f(x)=x^2.
\]

A **functional** is a function whose output is a scalar.

For vector space V:

\[
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb R.
\]

A **linear functional** additionally obeys linearity.

---

# 583.2 Formal definition

A function:

\[
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb R
\]

is linear if:

\[
\boxed{
f(av+bw)
=
af(v)+bf(w)
}
\]

for every vectors:

\[
v,w
\]

and scalars:

\[
a,b.
\]

This combines additivity:

\[
f(v+w)=f(v)+f(w)
\]

and homogeneity:

\[
f(av)=af(v).
\]

---

# 583.3 Example: coordinate extraction

In:

\[
\mathbb R^3,
\]

define:

\[
f_x(x,y,z)=x.
\]

Then:

\[
f_x(av+bw)
\]

extracts X of the combination.

Because X coordinates combine linearly:

\[
=
a f_x(v)+b f_x(w).
\]

So coordinate extraction is a linear functional.

This is the dual basis idea from 581.

---

# 583.4 Example: dot with fixed vector

Fix:

\[
n.
\]

Define:

\[
f(v)=n\cdot v.
\]

Then:

\[
f(av+bw)
=
n\cdot(av+bw).
\]

Dot-product distributivity:

\[
=
a(n\cdot v)+b(n\cdot w).
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
v\mapsto n\cdot v
}
\]

is a linear functional.

This is why every fixed normal can be used as a linear measuring rule.

---

# 583.5 Plane as a functional's kernel

Define:

\[
f(v)=n\cdot v.
\]

Now consider all vectors satisfying:

\[
f(v)=0.
\]

That is:

\[
n\cdot v=0.
\]

These are exactly vectors perpendicular to n.

In 3D they form a plane through the origin.

This set is called the **kernel**:

\[
\boxed{
\ker f=
\{v\mid f(v)=0\}.
}
\]

So one nonzero linear functional naturally defines a plane of vectors it annihilates.

---

# 583.6 "Annihilate" is not mystical vocabulary

If:

\[
f(v)=0,
\]

we say f annihilates v.

For a surface normal/covector n:

\[
n(t)=0
\]

for tangent t.

So the normal covector **annihilates tangent directions**.

This is an extremely clean way to define a surface normal:

\[
\boxed{
\text{normal covector = measurement rule that returns zero on every tangent direction}.
}
\]

Now the inverse-transpose law from Part 582 becomes conceptually natural.

---

# 583.7 Real-life example: elevation function

Suppose coordinates represent displacement:

\[
v=(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z).
\]

Define:

\[
f(v)=\Delta z.
\]

Then all horizontal displacements satisfy:

\[
f(v)=0.
\]

The kernel is the horizontal plane.

If:

\[
f(v)>0,
\]

movement goes upward.

If:

\[
f(v)<0,
\]

downward.

A linear functional divides vector space into:

```text
positive side
zero hyperplane
negative side
```

This is exactly how plane equations and half-space tests work.

---

# 583.8 Every linear functional in finite-dimensional coordinates

In ordinary finite-dimensional coordinates, a linear functional on:

\[
\mathbb R^n
\]

can be represented:

\[
\boxed{
f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
=
a_1x_1+\cdots+a_nx_n.
}
\]

For 3D:

\[
\boxed{
f(x,y,z)
=
ax+by+cz.
}
\]

The coefficients:

\[
(a,b,c)
\]

are covector components.

This looks exactly like a dot product, but conceptually the object is a scalar-producing map.

---

# 583.9 Linear versus affine functional

Plane not through the origin:

\[
ax+by+cz+d=0.
\]

The map:

\[
f(x)=ax+by+cz+d
\]

is not linear if:

\[
d\neq0,
\]

because:

\[
f(0)=d\neq0.
\]

A linear map must send zero to zero.

Instead this is an **affine functional**.

This distinction matters in plane equations.

The normal part:

\[
ax+by+cz
\]

is linear.

The offset:

\[
d
\]

moves the plane away from the origin.

---

# 583.10 Why transformations of covectors are inverse

Suppose:

\[
v'=Av.
\]

We want the same physical scalar measurement:

\[
f(v)
\]

after changing vector coordinates.

Call new functional:

\[
f'.
\]

We require:

\[
f'(v')=f(v).
\]

Since:

\[
v=A^{-1}v',
\]

we get:

\[
f'(v')
=
f(A^{-1}v').
\]

So the new functional must compose with the **inverse** transformation.

That is the conceptual reason covectors transform contragrediently/oppositely to vectors.

In coordinate notation this becomes inverse transpose.

So Part 582 wasn't a weird graphics exception.

It is a manifestation of general dual-space mathematics.

---

# 583.11 Mastery gate 583

You need to own:

\[
\boxed{
f(av+bw)=af(v)+bf(w).
}
\]

You should be able to identify:

\[
f(x,y,z)=2x-y+5z
\]

as linear.

But:

\[
g(x,y,z)=2x-y+5z+3
\]

is affine, not linear.

You should understand:

\[
\ker f
\]

as the set of directions the functional measures as zero.

And you should connect:

\[
\boxed{
\text{surface tangent space}
=
\ker(\text{normal covector})
}
\]

at least conceptually.

# PART 584 — EXTERIOR-PRODUCT INTUITION

Now we return to a problem you first saw around the cross product.

---

# 584.1 Cross product gave us something useful—but suspicious

In 3D:

\[
a\times b
\]

produces a vector perpendicular to both.

Its magnitude equals parallelogram area.

Its sign/orientation comes from handedness.

That works beautifully in 3D.

But remember Part 547:

In 4D, two independent vectors do **not** determine a unique perpendicular direction.

So what did the pair \(a,b\) fundamentally represent?

Not necessarily a normal vector.

The truly intrinsic geometric object is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the oriented parallelogram / plane element spanned by }a,b.
}
\]

Exterior algebra keeps that object directly instead of converting it into a perpendicular vector.

---

# 584.2 Think of two sticks holding a piece of cloth

Take two vectors:

\[
a,b.
\]

Place them tail-to-tail.

They span an oriented parallelogram:

```text
        a+b
         ●
        /|
       / |
      /  |
     /   |
    ●────●
   O      a

other side follows b
```

What information matters?

The spanned plane.

The area.

The orientation.

If the vectors become parallel, area collapses to zero.

This is precisely the information we want an algebraic object to encode.

That object will be a **bivector**.

---

# 584.3 A bivector is not "two vectors stored together"

This distinction matters.

The pair:

\[
(a,b)
\]

contains more information than their oriented area element.

For example, replace:

\[
a,b
\]

with another pair:

\[
c,d
\]

that spans the same oriented parallelogram.

Exterior algebra can represent those as the same bivector.

So the bivector represents the **oriented plane element**, not the specific generating pair.

---

# 584.4 Why not always use cross product?

Because cross product turns oriented area into a normal vector only due to special 3D structure.

Exterior product works naturally in:

\[
2D,\ 3D,\ 4D,\ \ldots
\]

without pretending every plane has one unique perpendicular vector.

In 4D, an oriented plane remains an oriented plane.

No fake `Vec4` normal is required.

---

# 584.5 Exterior algebra adds grades

Ordinary scalars are grade 0.

Vectors are grade 1.

Oriented plane elements/bivectors are grade 2.

Oriented volume elements/trivectors are grade 3.

Conceptually:

```text
grade 0 → scalar
grade 1 → directed line element
grade 2 → oriented area element
grade 3 → oriented volume element
...
```

This is extremely powerful because geometry itself naturally has dimensions.

Instead of forcing everything to be "another vector," exterior algebra lets each geometric object retain its dimensional grade.

---

# 584.6 Why this is useful in graphics thinking

A triangle patch naturally supplies two tangent directions:

\[
T_u,\ T_v.
\]

What do they fundamentally define?

Not merely a normal.

They define an oriented surface element.

Cross product in 3D gives:

\[
T_u\times T_v.
\]

Exterior algebra would instead retain:

\[
T_u\wedge T_v.
\]

The latter directly means:

\[
\boxed{
\text{oriented tangent-plane area element}.
}
\]

A normal can be derived from that later if the ambient dimension, metric, and orientation make such a conversion meaningful.

This is conceptually cleaner.

---

# 584.7 Degeneracy becomes natural

If:

\[
b=\lambda a,
\]

the two vectors span no area.

So their exterior product should be:

\[
0.
\]

That means:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b=0
}
\]

when they are linearly dependent.

Compare cross product:

\[
a\times b=0
\]

for parallel 3D vectors.

Exterior product generalizes that fact to arbitrary dimension.

---

# 584.8 Why Part 584 is before Part 585

Part 584's point is conceptual:

> The cross product was hiding a more fundamental object: oriented area.

Part 585 now gives the actual algebraic operation that constructs it.

---

# PART 585 — WEDGE PRODUCT AS ORIENTED AREA CONCEPT

The wedge product is written:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b.
}
\]

It takes two vectors and returns a bivector.

---

# 585.1 First defining property: antisymmetry

Swapping the vectors reverses orientation:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b
=
-(b\wedge a).
}
\]

This matches everything we learned about signed area.

If:

\[
a=b,
\]

then:

\[
a\wedge a
=
-(a\wedge a).
\]

Therefore:

\[
2(a\wedge a)=0.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge a=0.
}
\]

That immediately captures zero area from repeated direction.

---

# 585.2 Bilinearity

The wedge distributes:

\[
\boxed{
(a+b)\wedge c
=
a\wedge c+b\wedge c
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge(b+c)
=
a\wedge b+a\wedge c.
}
\]

Scalars pull out:

\[
(\lambda a)\wedge b
=
\lambda(a\wedge b).
\]

This means oriented area responds linearly to each edge vector.

Double one side of a parallelogram:

\[
\text{area doubles}.
\]

Correct.

---

# 585.3 Derive coordinate form in 2D

Let:

\[
a=a_xe_x+a_ye_y,
\]

\[
b=b_xe_x+b_ye_y.
\]

Then:

\[
a\wedge b
=
(a_xe_x+a_ye_y)
\wedge
(b_xe_x+b_ye_y).
\]

Expand:

\[
=
a_xb_x(e_x\wedge e_x)
+
a_xb_y(e_x\wedge e_y)
\]

\[
+
a_yb_x(e_y\wedge e_x)
+
a_yb_y(e_y\wedge e_y).
\]

But:

\[
e_x\wedge e_x=0,
\]

\[
e_y\wedge e_y=0,
\]

and:

\[
e_y\wedge e_x
=
-(e_x\wedge e_y).
\]

Therefore:

\[
a\wedge b
=
(a_xb_y-a_yb_x)
(e_x\wedge e_y).
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b
=
(a_xb_y-a_yb_x)e_x\wedge e_y.
}
\]

Recognize the coefficient?

It is exactly the signed 2D area determinant from Part 566.

So orientation tests were already computing the coefficient of a wedge product.

---

# 585.4 3D wedge product

Let:

\[
a=(a_x,a_y,a_z)
\]

and:

\[
b=(b_x,b_y,b_z).
\]

There are three independent coordinate planes:

\[
e_x\wedge e_y,
\]

\[
e_x\wedge e_z,
\]

\[
e_y\wedge e_z.
\]

Expanding gives:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b
=
(a_xb_y-a_yb_x)e_x\wedge e_y
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
+
(a_xb_z-a_zb_x)e_x\wedge e_z
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
+
(a_yb_z-a_zb_y)e_y\wedge e_z.
}
\]

These three coefficients describe how much oriented area lies in each coordinate plane.

---

# 585.5 Compare cross product components

Cross product:

\[
a\times b
=
(
a_yb_z-a_zb_y,\;
a_zb_x-a_xb_z,\;
a_xb_y-a_yb_x
).
\]

Wedge components are roughly:

```text
XY area
XZ area
YZ area
```

Cross components correspond to perpendicular axes:

```text
X normal ↔ YZ area
Y normal ↔ ZX area
Z normal ↔ XY area
```

This is not coincidence.

In 3D, an oriented plane element can be mapped to its perpendicular pseudovector.

This mapping depends on the metric and orientation and is related to the **Hodge dual**.

You don't need Hodge-star machinery yet.

The important thing is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{cross product is essentially a 3D dual representation of the wedge product}.
}
\]

---

# 585.6 Why wedge product is more fundamental than cross product

In 4D, wedge of two vectors still works.

How many independent plane components exist?

Choose two distinct coordinate axes:

\[
\binom42=6.
\]

They are:

\[
e_1\wedge e_2,
\]

\[
e_1\wedge e_3,
\]

\[
e_1\wedge e_4,
\]

\[
e_2\wedge e_3,
\]

\[
e_2\wedge e_4,
\]

\[
e_3\wedge e_4.
\]

So a general 4D bivector has six components.

That exactly matches our dimensional argument from Part 547.

There is no reason to squash those six independent oriented-area components into four coordinates.

Exterior algebra simply stores the correct geometric object.

---

# 585.7 Magnitude

In Euclidean geometry, the magnitude of a simple bivector:

\[
a\wedge b
\]

corresponds to parallelogram area:

\[
\boxed{
\|a\wedge b\|
=
\|a\|\|b\|\sin\theta.
}
\]

Same area magnitude as:

\[
\|a\times b\|
\]

in 3D.

But the bivector represents the oriented plane directly.

---

# 585.8 Real surface example

Our spaceship triangle has edges:

\[
e_1=B-A,
\]

\[
e_2=C-A.
\]

Then:

\[
\boxed{
e_1\wedge e_2
}
\]

represents its oriented double-triangle-area plane element.

Its magnitude:

\[
\|e_1\wedge e_2\|
\]

is the parallelogram area.

Triangle area:

\[
\boxed{
A_\triangle
=
\frac12\|e_1\wedge e_2\|.
}
\]

If the triangle collapses to a line:

\[
e_1\wedge e_2=0.
\]

This is a dimension-independent way of expressing triangle degeneracy.

---

# 585.9 Wedge builds higher grades

Take three vectors:

\[
a,b,c.
\]

You can form:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b\wedge c.
}
\]

This represents an oriented volume element.

If the three vectors are linearly dependent:

\[
a\wedge b\wedge c=0.
\]

In 3D, its scalar magnitude corresponds to parallelepiped volume.

So:

```text
wedge 2 vectors → oriented area

wedge 3 vectors → oriented volume

wedge k vectors → oriented k-dimensional volume
```

This is a remarkably clean generalization of determinants, cross products and triple products.

---

# 585.10 Linear independence detector

A powerful fact:

\[
\boxed{
v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_k=0
}
\]

iff the vectors are linearly dependent, assuming the usual exterior-algebra setting.

Why?

If one vector is a combination of the others, bilinearity plus antisymmetry causes repeated-direction terms, all of which vanish.

So wedge products naturally encode independence and oriented measure.

---

# 585.11 Mastery gate 585

You should be able to derive:

\[
a\wedge b
=
(a_xb_y-a_yb_x)e_x\wedge e_y
\]

in 2D from bilinearity and antisymmetry.

Then explain:

\[
a\wedge a=0
\]

and why:

\[
a\wedge b=0
\]

means no 2D area is spanned.

And you should be able to explain the relationship:

\[
\boxed{
\text{cross product}
\approx
\text{3D dual encoding of wedge-product area}.
}
\]

# PART 586 — GEOMETRIC-ALGEBRA MOTIVATION FOR GRAPHICS

Now: why invent an even larger algebra?

Because dot products, wedge products, complex numbers, cross products, reflections and rotations look like separate tricks—but they are related pieces of one geometric multiplication system.

That system is **geometric algebra**.

This Part is motivation, not a full geometric-algebra course.

---

# 586.1 The fragmentation problem

Look at what we have learned:

\[
a\cdot b
\]

gives scalar alignment.

\[
a\wedge b
\]

gives oriented area.

\[
a\times b
\]

gives a special 3D pseudovector.

Complex numbers rotate 2D.

Quaternions will rotate 3D.

Normals/covectors encode constraints.

Matrices transform coordinates.

This works.

But the toolbox feels fragmented.

Geometric algebra asks:

> Can we construct one algebraic product in which geometric relations such as length, angle, oriented plane, reflection and rotation appear naturally?

---

# 586.2 Geometric product

For vectors \(a,b\), geometric algebra defines a product:

\[
\boxed{
ab
=
a\cdot b
+
a\wedge b.
}
\]

Look carefully.

The result is not necessarily another vector.

It contains:

\[
\boxed{
\text{scalar part}
}
\]

plus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{bivector part}.
}
\]

So multiplying two vectors captures both:

\[
\cos\theta
\]

and:

\[
\sin\theta
\]

style geometric relationships simultaneously.

---

# 586.3 Parallel vectors

If \(a,b\) are parallel:

\[
a\wedge b=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
ab=a\cdot b.
\]

Product is purely scalar.

That makes sense: two parallel vectors span no area.

---

# 586.4 Perpendicular vectors

If:

\[
a\cdot b=0,
\]

then:

\[
ab=a\wedge b.
\]

Product is purely bivector.

Two perpendicular vectors have no scalar alignment and span maximal area for their lengths.

Again the algebra matches geometry.

---

# 586.5 Recover dot and wedge from geometric product

Swap order:

\[
ba
=
b\cdot a+b\wedge a.
\]

Dot is symmetric:

\[
b\cdot a=a\cdot b.
\]

Wedge is antisymmetric:

\[
b\wedge a=-a\wedge b.
\]

Therefore:

\[
ab+ba
=
2a\cdot b.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
a\cdot b
=
\frac12(ab+ba).
}
\]

Subtract:

\[
ab-ba
=
2a\wedge b.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b
=
\frac12(ab-ba).
}
\]

So dot and wedge aren't unrelated operations inside geometric algebra.

They are the symmetric and antisymmetric portions of one product.

That is a beautiful structural insight.

---

# 586.6 Vector squared

Take:

\[
a^2=aa.
\]

Then:

\[
a\wedge a=0.
\]

Thus:

\[
a^2=a\cdot a.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
a^2=\|a\|^2
}
\]

in ordinary Euclidean signature.

A vector's square is its squared magnitude.

That gives vector inversion naturally:

For nonzero \(a\):

\[
a^{-1}
=
\frac{a}{a^2}
=
\frac{a}{\|a\|^2}.
\]

Because:

\[
a a^{-1}=1.
\]

This starts to make geometry algebraically compact.

---

# 586.7 The startling 2D connection to complex numbers

Let orthonormal basis:

\[
e_1,e_2.
\]

Define bivector:

\[
I=e_1e_2.
\]

Because they are perpendicular:

\[
e_1e_2=-e_2e_1.
\]

Now compute:

\[
I^2
=
e_1e_2e_1e_2.
\]

Swap middle:

\[
e_2e_1=-e_1e_2.
\]

Therefore:

\[
I^2
=
-e_1e_1e_2e_2.
\]

Since unit basis vectors square to 1:

\[
e_1^2=e_2^2=1.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
I^2=-1.
}
\]

That is exactly the defining algebraic behavior of:

\[
i^2=-1
\]

from complex numbers.

So the imaginary unit in 2D rotations can be interpreted geometrically as the **oriented unit plane element**.

That is a powerful connection to Part 503.

---

# 586.8 Rotations emerge from plane elements

Because:

\[
I^2=-1,
\]

the exponential:

\[
e^{I\theta}
\]

behaves like:

\[
\cos\theta+I\sin\theta.
\]

This is the same structure as complex rotation.

In geometric algebra, rotations are represented by objects called **rotors**.

Conceptually a rotor contains:

\[
\cos\frac\theta2
\]

plus an oriented-plane term involving:

\[
\sin\frac\theta2.
\]

And vectors are rotated through a sandwich-like operation.

This mirrors what quaternions later do in 3D.

Indeed, 3D rotation quaternions are deeply related to the even subalgebra of 3D geometric algebra.

That is why quaternion formulas can feel less arbitrary once geometric algebra is understood.

We will not replace the dedicated quaternion Parts 667+ here.

---

# 586.9 Reflections become fundamental

Reflection across a plane can also be expressed elegantly using geometric products.

Recall our earlier reflection formula:

\[
r
=
v-2(v\cdot n)n.
\]

Geometric algebra can encode reflection through compact sandwich products involving a unit normal.

Why is that important conceptually?

Because rotations can be understood as compositions of reflections.

This gives one algebraic framework for both transformations rather than unrelated formulas.

---

# 586.10 Why graphics engineers may care

Geometric algebra offers a unified language for:

\[
\text{projections},
\]

\[
\text{reflections},
\]

\[
\text{rotations},
\]

\[
\text{oriented subspaces},
\]

\[
\text{intersections in extended formulations},
\]

and other geometry.

It is especially conceptually attractive when a system constantly manipulates directions, planes, rotations, and geometric incidence relations.

---

# 586.11 Why most production graphics code still uses vectors/matrices/quaternions

A powerful abstraction does not automatically win ecosystem adoption.

Traditional graphics stacks have enormous investment in:

```text
Vec3
Mat4
Quat
dot
cross
```

GPUs, shading languages, file formats and APIs are built around them.

Matrices are universally understood.

Quaternions are compact and efficient for rotation.

So geometric algebra may offer conceptual unification but introduce:

- less familiar notation;
- different library ecosystems;
- more complex multivector representation;
- potentially unnecessary generality for routine tasks.

A graphics engineer should understand the idea without assuming every engine should be rewritten around GA.

---

# 586.12 Real benefit even if you never use a GA library

After this Part you should see:

\[
\boxed{
\text{dot product = scalar interaction}
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
\text{wedge product = oriented subspace interaction}.
}
\]

Cross products, complex rotations and quaternions stop looking like completely disconnected mathematical hacks.

That conceptual unification alone is useful.

---

# 586.13 Mastery gate 586

You should be able to derive:

\[
\boxed{
ab=a\cdot b+a\wedge b
}
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
a\cdot b=\frac{ab+ba}{2}
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b=\frac{ab-ba}{2}.
}
\]

You should understand why:

\[
a^2=\|a\|^2.
\]

And derive for an orthonormal 2D basis:

\[
(e_1e_2)^2=-1.
\]

If you can explain why this links geometric plane orientation to complex-number rotation, you've understood the motivation.

# 587.1 Mathematics says

\[
v=(x,y,z).
\]

Computer memory does not store parentheses.

It stores bits.

A typical C++ representation might be:

```cpp
struct RawVec3
{
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
};
```

Conceptually:

```text
RawVec3 object

+-----------+
| x float   |
+-----------+
| y float   |
+-----------+
| z float   |
+-----------+
```

But we need to understand what "float" and those boxes actually mean.

---

# 587.2 What is `float`?

C++ defines a floating-point type named:

```cpp
float
```

but C++ does not universally require it to be IEEE-754 binary32 on every imaginable implementation.

On mainstream desktop/mobile/GPU-oriented platforms it is overwhelmingly commonly:

\[
32\text{-bit IEEE-754 binary32}.
\]

An engine that depends on that can verify assumptions:

```cpp
#include <limits>

static_assert(sizeof(float) == 4);

static_assert(
    std::numeric_limits<float>::is_iec559
);
```

`is_iec559` is commonly used to detect IEC 60559/IEEE-style semantics, though platform-specific guarantees may still matter.

Never confuse:

\[
\boxed{
\text{common ABI fact}
}
\]

with:

\[
\boxed{
\text{universal C++ language guarantee}.
}
\]

---

# 587.3 Typical binary32 structure

A common IEEE binary32 value occupies:

\[
32\text{ bits}.
\]

Conceptually:

```text
1 sign bit
8 exponent bits
23 stored fraction bits
```

With normalized implicit leading significand bit, effective precision is roughly:

\[
24\text{ binary significant bits}.
\]

This is why a `Vec3` does not hold exact real numbers.

It holds three finite floating-point approximations.

So mathematical vector:

\[
(0.1,0.2,0.3)
\]

becomes:

```text
nearest representable binary float to 0.1
nearest representable binary float to 0.2
nearest representable binary float to 0.3
```

Every vector operation later inherits this finite representation.

---

# 587.4 Typical `Vec3` memory footprint

If:

```cpp
sizeof(float) == 4
```

then three raw floats require 12 bytes of member payload:

\[
3\times4=12.
\]

On many conventional ABIs:

```cpp
struct RawVec3
{
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
};
```

will have:

```text
sizeof(RawVec3) = 12
alignof(RawVec3) = 4
```

But do not turn "often" into a language-law assumption.

C++ implementations may insert padding to satisfy object layout/alignment rules.

The object representation can include padding.

This is why later Parts 593–594 explicitly distinguish alignment and packed/aligned Vec3 designs.

---

# 587.5 Arrays give stronger contiguity intuition

For:

```cpp
float v[3];
```

array elements are contiguous.

Memory conceptually:

```text
address +0   → v[0]
address +4   → v[1]
address +8   → v[2]
```

assuming 4-byte floats.

There is no padding **between array elements**.

This fact makes arrays useful when you need guaranteed regular scalar strides.

---

# 587.6 Struct representation

For:

```cpp
struct Vec3
{
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
};
```

the compiler lays out the members according to the type's object-layout rules and alignment requirements.

On ordinary platforms you might see:

```text
offset 0  x
offset 4  y
offset 8  z
sizeof 12
```

But later we may deliberately request stronger alignment:

```cpp
struct alignas(16) Vec3Aligned
{
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
};
```

Now a likely layout is:

```text
offset 0   x
offset 4   y
offset 8   z
offset 12  padding
sizeof 16
```

Why intentionally waste 4 bytes?

Potentially to make loads, arrays, ABI compatibility or SIMD operations simpler.

Full trade-off belongs to Parts 593–595.

---

# 587.7 Arrays of Vec3 make padding matter more

Suppose:

```cpp
Vec3 positions[4];
```

If:

\[
sizeof(Vec3)=12,
\]

layout might be:

```text
position 0: bytes  0..11
position 1: bytes 12..23
position 2: bytes 24..35
position 3: bytes 36..47
```

Stride:

\[
12.
\]

If aligned Vec3 has:

\[
sizeof=16,
\]

then:

```text
position 0: bytes  0..15
position 1: bytes 16..31
position 2: bytes 32..47
position 3: bytes 48..63
```

Stride:

\[
16.
\]

This affects:

- total memory;
- cache usage;
- vectorized loads;
- GPU buffer layouts;
- serialization;
- binary interoperability.

So `sizeof(Vec3)` is not trivia.

It affects entire array architecture.

---

# 587.8 One million positions

Suppose one million vertices.

Packed 12-byte positions:

\[
12,000,000\text{ bytes}
\]

roughly 12 MB decimal.

16-byte stride:

\[
16,000,000\text{ bytes}.
\]

Difference:

\[
4,000,000\text{ bytes}.
\]

Four megabytes for just one million vectors.

Now add:

- normals;
- tangents;
- velocities;
- particle positions;
- instance data.

Alignment choices can materially affect memory bandwidth.

But 16-byte layout may also simplify SIMD/GPU paths.

This is why there is no universal "best Vec3 layout."

---

# 587.9 AoS versus SoA preview

Suppose four particle positions.

Array of structures:

```cpp
struct Vec3 { float x,y,z; };

Vec3 p[4];
```

Memory:

```text
x0 y0 z0 | x1 y1 z1 | x2 y2 z2 | x3 y3 z3
```

This is **Array of Structures**, AoS.

Alternative:

```cpp
float x[4];
float y[4];
float z[4];
```

Memory:

```text
x0 x1 x2 x3
y0 y1 y2 y3
z0 z1 z2 z3
```

This is **Structure of Arrays**, SoA.

If computation needs:

```text
all X components at once
```

SoA may map naturally to vectorized processing.

If code repeatedly processes:

```text
one complete position
```

AoS can be convenient.

We are not choosing yet.

The point is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{mathematical vector representation does not dictate physical memory layout}.
}
\]

---

# 587.10 CPU register representation need not match memory representation

Memory might store:

```text
12 bytes
```

for x,y,z.

CPU calculation may load them into:

```text
128-bit SIMD register
```

with a fourth unused lane.

For example conceptually:

```text
[x y z ?]
```

or:

```text
[x y z 0]
```

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{storage layout}
\neq
\text{execution register layout}.
}
\]

This distinction matters when people claim:

> Vec3 must be 16 bytes because SIMD.

Not necessarily.

You can have packed memory and widen during computation.

Whether that is beneficial depends on workload and architecture.

Part 595 will study SIMD explicitly.

---

# 587.11 Endianness still exists

Suppose one float uses four bytes.

The order of those bytes in memory depends on machine endianness.

Earlier Parts taught endianness.

A `Vec3` is therefore not merely:

```text
12 meaningful bytes
```

in some universally portable serialization format.

If you write raw object bytes to disk and read them on another platform, questions include:

- float representation;
- byte order;
- padding;
- alignment;
- ABI;
- versioning.

So robust serialization normally defines a binary format rather than dumping arbitrary C++ object representations blindly.

---

# 587.12 `memcpy` versus reinterpret-casting

Suppose you need the bits of a float.

Dangerous thinking:

```cpp
uint32_t bits =
    *reinterpret_cast<uint32_t*>(&f);
```

That can violate C++ aliasing/object-model rules.

Safer modern approaches include:

```cpp
#include <bit>

std::uint32_t bits =
    std::bit_cast<std::uint32_t>(f);
```

assuming equal sizes.

Or `std::memcpy` for byte transfers.

This matters in graphics engines because low-level math code often sits close to:

- GPU buffers;
- serialization;
- SIMD;
- hashing;
- binary formats.

Low-level speed does not excuse undefined behavior.

---

# 587.13 Why raw `memcmp` is not vector equality

Suppose:

\[
+0.0f
\]

and:

\[
-0.0f.
\]

They compare numerically equal:

```cpp
+0.0f == -0.0f
```

is true under normal IEEE behavior.

But their bit patterns differ.

So raw byte equality can say:

```text
different
```

while floating-point comparison says:

```text
equal.
```

NaNs create even stranger semantics:

\[
NaN\neq NaN.
\]

Thus:

```cpp
memcmp(&a, &b, sizeof(Vec3))
```

is not generally the same semantic operation as:

```cpp
a == b
```

for floating vectors.

And approximate geometric equality is yet another operation, coming in Part 596.

---

# 587.14 Padding makes byte comparison worse

If a struct contains padding bytes, those bytes may hold unspecified values depending on how the object was created/assigned.

Two vectors with equal member values could potentially have different padding bit contents.

Therefore comparing entire object representations can produce false differences.

Likewise hashing raw struct bytes can accidentally hash meaningless padding.

This is why low-level object representation must be understood before binary tricks.

---

# 587.15 Ownership and lifetime

A simple vector value:

```cpp
struct Vec3
{
    float x, y, z;
};
```

typically owns its three scalar members directly.

No heap allocation.

No pointer ownership.

Construction creates one value object.

Copying copies component values.

Destruction usually has nothing to do.

This is ideal for a tiny math type.

You generally want:

```text
cheap copy
cheap move
trivial lifetime
no hidden allocation
```

unless there is a very unusual requirement.

That is why vector math types are typically value types.

---

# 587.16 Why no heap allocation?

Imagine:

```cpp
class Vec3
{
    float* data;
};
```

where every vector allocates three floats dynamically.

Now every position may require:

```text
allocation
pointer indirection
fragmentation
lifetime management
cache misses
```

for twelve bytes of actual numeric payload.

That would be disastrous for normal engine workloads.

Vectors are small fixed-size data.

They should normally live inline.

---

# 587.17 Thread safety

A `Vec3` value has no magical concurrency concerns if each thread owns its own object.

But shared mutation still races.

Example:

```cpp
sharedPosition.x += 1.0f;
```

from multiple threads without synchronization can be a data race.

"Vec3 is simple" does not make unsynchronized shared writes legal.

Math types should preferably be easy to use as immutable values or thread-local/data-partitioned values in parallel systems.

---

# 587.18 CPU cache intuition

Suppose cache line is commonly around 64 bytes on a particular CPU.

With packed 12-byte Vec3s, one cache line can contain portions of several vectors.

With 16-byte stride, exactly four vectors fit in 64 bytes.

Neither fact alone says which is faster.

Consider workload.

If you process:

```text
position + velocity + normal
```

stored in separate arrays, different cache patterns arise than one monolithic vertex struct.

Performance emerges from access patterns, not merely individual `sizeof`.

---

# 587.19 GPU buffer mismatch

Suppose CPU believes:

```text
Vec3 stride = 12
```

but shader/buffer layout expects:

```text
stride = 16
```

Then GPU might read:

```text
vertex 0 xyz correctly

vertex 1 x from previous padding/data

vertex 1 y/z shifted

everything after first element corrupted
```

This is a classic data-layout bug.

The math is perfect.

The memory contract is wrong.

Graphics engineers must debug both.

---

# 587.20 Real memory trace

Suppose:

```cpp
RawVec3 v{1.0f, 2.0f, 3.0f};
```

On a conventional platform with 32-bit IEEE little-endian floats, conceptual byte representation may resemble:

```text
1.0f bits = 0x3F800000
bytes     = 00 00 80 3F

2.0f bits = 0x40000000
bytes     = 00 00 00 40

3.0f bits = 0x40400000
bytes     = 00 00 40 40
```

So contiguous memory might look:

```text
00 00 80 3F | 00 00 00 40 | 00 00 40 40
     x              y              z
```

Again, that concrete byte sequence assumes a specific conventional platform and should not be treated as universal C++ behavior.

But this is what "the vector exists in memory" actually means.

---

# 587.21 Float precision propagates into vector geometry

Suppose position:

\[
P=(10^8,10^8,10^8)
\]

stored in 32-bit floats.

At that magnitude, the spacing between adjacent representable floats is much larger than around zero.

Adding:

\[
0.001
\]

may change nothing.

Then two nearby points:

\[
P
\]

and:

\[
P+(0.001,0,0)
\]

may collapse to identical stored values.

Now:

\[
B-A
\]

becomes zero.

That can destroy:

- normals;
- triangle areas;
- collision distances;
- tangent frames.

So vector storage precision is not only a memory issue.

It changes what geometry can exist numerically.

---

# 587.22 Why large-world engines care

Imagine a world coordinate measured in metres.

At origin:

\[
x\approx0,
\]

float gives extremely fine absolute spacing.

At:

\[
x=10^7,
\]

spacing becomes much coarser.

Your character can start jittering because centimetre or millimetre movement can no longer be represented accurately.

Solutions later may include:

```text
double-precision world positions
floating origin
camera-relative rendering
hierarchical coordinates
local cells
```

Again, representation affects architecture.

---

# 587.23 Float versus double vector storage

A `double` commonly uses 8 bytes.

So:

```cpp
struct DVec3
{
    double x, y, z;
};
```

has 24 bytes of scalar payload versus 12 for three 4-byte floats.

Double offers substantially more precision and exponent range but doubles bandwidth/storage for raw components.

Graphics often uses:

```text
double on CPU world simulation
float on GPU relative rendering
```

in large-world scenarios.

Whether this is appropriate depends on engine requirements.

---

# 587.24 `float` is usually enough for local graphics vectors

Normals:

\[
[-1,1]
\]

directions:

\[
[-1,1]
\]

UVs often modest ranges.

Camera-relative positions perhaps within kilometres rather than astronomical coordinates.

For these, binary32 may be entirely adequate.

The problem isn't:

> float is bad.

The engineering question is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Does the numeric range and required resolution fit the chosen representation?}
}
\]

---

# 587.25 A baseline value type

Before Parts 588–590 design specialized vector APIs, the fundamental storage might look as simple as:

```cpp
struct Float3
{
    float x{};
    float y{};
    float z{};
};
```

This gives us three named component values.

No normalization invariant.

No semantic coordinate-space information.

No distinction between:

```text
position
normal
velocity
RGB colour
```

That's deliberately low-level.

Later Parts add API meaning.

---

# 587.26 Do not confuse storage type with semantic type

These may all physically contain:

```text
3 × float
```

but mean wildly different things:

```cpp
WorldPosition
WorldDirection
SurfaceNormal
Velocity
RGB
Scale3
AngularVelocity
```

The fact that they have identical memory representation does not mean every operation between them is valid.

For example:

```cpp
WorldPosition + SurfaceNormal
```

may compile with a generic raw Vec3 API yet have no sensible domain meaning.

Part 598–599 will address stronger invariants and coordinate-space distinction.

# 587.27 Mastery gate 587

You should be able to answer this chain without guessing:

A mathematical Vec3 is:

\[
(x,y,z).
\]

A typical machine Vec3 may be:

```text
three binary floating-point values
stored inline in a C++ object
```

with a common payload of:

\[
12\text{ bytes}
\]

when `float` is 4 bytes.

But actual `sizeof` and alignment are object-layout/ABI properties.

Padding may exist.

Array elements have regular contiguous layout.

Object memory representation is not automatically a portable file format.

\[
\boxed{
\text{representation}
\rightarrow
\text{memory footprint}
\rightarrow
\text{bandwidth/cache behavior}
\rightarrow
\text{numeric precision}

\text{actual engine behavior}.
}
\]

# THE DEEP CONNECTION ACROSS 580–587

Now connect the entire block using our spaceship triangle.

We start with a triangle point.

In homogeneous clip coordinates:

\[
P_c=(X,Y,Z,W).
\]

Part 580 says:

\[
\boxed{
P_{\text{ndc}}
=
(X/W,Y/W,Z/W)
}
\]

gives a canonical projective coordinate suitable for later viewport/raster mapping.

Now consider the triangle surface.

Its tangent vectors transform as ordinary vectors.

Its surface normal is something different.

Part 581 says:

\[
\boxed{
\text{a normal behaves naturally as a covector-like measurement rule}
}
\]

because it measures tangent motion and returns zero:

\[
n(t)=0.
\]

Part 583 formalizes such measurement rules as linear functionals.

That lets Part 582 make complete sense:

\[
\boxed{
t'=At
}
\]

but:

\[
\boxed{
n'=A^{-T}n
}
\]

so:

\[
n'(t')=n(t).
\]

The normal still annihilates the transformed tangent plane.

Then Parts 584–585 ask something even deeper:

What was the surface itself?

Two tangent vectors:

\[
T_u,T_v
\]

define an oriented surface element:

\[
\boxed{
T_u\wedge T_v.
}
\]

The cross-product normal was merely a convenient 3D dual encoding of that oriented area.

Part 586 then shows that:

\[
\boxed{
a\cdot b
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
a\wedge b
}
\]

can be seen as complementary portions of one geometric product:

\[
\boxed{
ab=a\cdot b+a\wedge b.
}
\]

So alignment, area, reflections and rotations begin to fit inside one algebraic picture.

Finally Part 587 asks:

> Fine—but where does any of this actually live?

And the answer is:

```text
float x
float y
float z
```

or some related layout in real memory, with finite precision, alignment, cache and ABI constraints.

That transition is fundamental:

```text
projective geometry
      ↓
dual-space geometry
      ↓
surface orientation
      ↓
exterior algebra
      ↓
geometric algebra
      ↓
actual bits in RAM
```

That is what this curriculum is supposed to do: connect abstract math all the way down to the machine instead of leaving them as separate subjects.
