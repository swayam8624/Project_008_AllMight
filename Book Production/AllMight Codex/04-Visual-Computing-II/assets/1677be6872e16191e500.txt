# PART 504 — VECTORS AS GEOMETRIC DISPLACEMENTS

## 504.1 Forget `(x, y, z)` for a moment

Before we touch this:

```cpp
Vec3 v{1.0f, 2.0f, 3.0f};
```

we need to answer a more fundamental question:

**What physical/geometrical thing are those three numbers eventually trying to describe?**

The answer is a **displacement**.

Suppose there are two locations:

```text
A ● --------------------> ● B
```

Going from \(A\) to \(B\) requires a certain movement.

That movement has two essential geometric properties:

- how far you move;
- which way you move.

That movement is a geometric vector.

We can denote it

\[
\vec v.
\]

At this stage, do **not** think

\[
\vec v=(3,2).
\]

That will be Part 505.

For now:

\[
\boxed{\vec v=\text{a geometric displacement}}
\]

is the important idea.

---

# 504.2 Position and displacement are different concepts

Imagine standing at some location \(A\).

You walk:

```text
5 m east
```

That is a displacement.

Now suppose somebody else starts 100 metres away and also walks:

```text
5 m east
```

Their starting point differs.

But the displacement is the same.

Graphically:

```text
A ●──────→ B

               C ●──────→ D
```

If both arrows have the same direction and the same length, they represent the same **free vector**.

So the geometric vector is not inherently tied to one location.

This gives an important idea:

\[
\boxed{
\text{A geometric vector can be translated without changing the vector.}
}
\]

The arrow is being used to visualize the displacement.

The location where we draw the arrow is not part of the displacement itself.

---

# 504.3 A more rigorous geometric definition

A directed line segment has:

- a tail;
- a head.

For example:

\[
\overrightarrow{AB}.
\]

Another directed segment

\[
\overrightarrow{CD}
\]

represents the same free vector if it has the same geometric displacement.

Informally:

\[
\overrightarrow{AB}
=
\overrightarrow{CD}
\]

when both have the same length and direction.

So one rigorous way to think about a free vector is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{an equivalence class of directed line segments under translation}
}
\]

That phrase deserves unpacking.

An **equivalence class** means we collect many visually different arrows and declare them equivalent because they describe the same displacement.

For example:

```text
       ─────→

                     ─────→

 ─────→
```

Their absolute locations differ.

Their displacement does not.

All three drawings can represent one vector \(\vec v\).

This is deeper than:

> a vector is an arrow.

The arrow is a **representative drawing** of the underlying displacement.

---

# 504.4 Why did mathematics need this object?

Suppose an object moves through a scene.

At time \(t_0\), it occupies location \(P\).

Later, it occupies location \(Q\).

We want to describe:

> what motion took us from \(P\) to \(Q\)?

The answer should not depend on where the coordinate origin happened to be placed.

Suppose I shift the entire coordinate system ten kilometres east.

The object's coordinates change.

But its actual movement should not suddenly change.

That means we want something that describes **relative movement**, rather than absolute location.

That thing is displacement.

And geometric vectors give us precisely that.

---

# 504.5 Translation invariance

This property is fundamental.

Suppose an arrow goes from \(A\) to \(B\).

Now translate both endpoints by the same amount.

You obtain:

\[
A'\rightarrow B'.
\]

The absolute locations changed.

But the relative movement from tail to head did not.

Therefore:

\[
\overrightarrow{AB}
=
\overrightarrow{A'B'}.
\]

This is called **translation invariance** of free vectors.

It is why vectors are so useful for:

- velocities;
- accelerations;
- forces;
- surface directions;
- ray directions;
- geometric offsets;
- movement commands.

Those quantities describe directional relationships rather than absolute places.

---

# 504.6 Vector addition comes naturally from displacement

Suppose you first move using displacement \(\vec u\).

Then, from your new location, you move by \(\vec v\).

```text
A ●────u────>● B
             \
              \
               v
                \
                 ▼
                 ● C
```

There must be one displacement that moves directly from \(A\) to \(C\).

Call it:

\[
\vec u+\vec v.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\vec u+\vec v
=
\text{the displacement produced by performing }\vec u\text{ followed by }\vec v
}
\]

Graphically:

```text
A ●────u────>● B
 \            |
  \           |
   \ u + v    | v
    \         |
     \        ▼
      ───────>● C
```

This is the **head-to-tail rule**.

It is not an arbitrary algebra rule someone invented.

It arises directly from composing motions.

---

# 504.7 Why vector addition is commutative geometrically

Interestingly,

\[
\vec u+\vec v
=
\vec v+\vec u.
\]

We can see this using the parallelogram construction.

```text
          ●
         /|
        / |
      v/  |u
      /   |
     ●----●
        u
```

Starting from the same point:

- move by \(\vec u\), then \(\vec v\);
- or move by \(\vec v\), then \(\vec u\).

Both reach the opposite corner of the same parallelogram.

Thus the total displacement is identical.

This eventually becomes one of the vector-space axioms in Part 507:

\[
\vec u+\vec v=\vec v+\vec u.
\]

Notice the teaching direction:

```text
geometry
   ↓
observed structural rule
   ↓
later abstracted into an axiom
```

rather than starting by memorizing axioms.

---

# 504.8 Associativity also comes from composing movement

Consider three movements:

\[
\vec u,\quad\vec v,\quad\vec w.
\]

Whether we conceptually group the first two:

\[
(\vec u+\vec v)+\vec w
\]

or the last two:

\[
\vec u+(\vec v+\vec w)
\]

the physical final displacement is unchanged.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
(\vec u+\vec v)+\vec w
=
\vec u+(\vec v+\vec w)
}
\]

Again, geometry motivates a vector-space axiom.

---

# 504.9 The zero vector

What displacement means:

> do not move at all?

That is the **zero vector**:

\[
\vec 0.
\]

It has zero displacement.

Therefore:

\[
\vec v+\vec 0=\vec v.
\]

Geometrically:

```text
perform v
then perform no movement
```

obviously gives the same total displacement as performing \(\vec v\).

The zero vector is not merely "the vector whose components are zero."

That will be its coordinate representation later.

Its deeper meaning is:

\[
\boxed{\vec0=\text{identity displacement}}
\]

---

# 504.10 Opposite vectors

Suppose \(\vec v\) takes you from \(A\) to \(B\).

There must be a displacement that takes you directly back.

Call it

\[
-\vec v.
\]

Then:

\[
\vec v+(-\vec v)=\vec0.
\]

Geometrically:

```text
A ●────v────>● B
A ●<───-v────● B
```

Same size of displacement.

Opposite direction.

This is the **additive inverse**.

Again, Part 507 will abstract this requirement.

---

# 504.11 Scalar multiplication geometrically

Part 519 will give scalar multiplication its own dedicated treatment, so we will not steal that Part.

But Part 504 cannot be understood without at least seeing its meaning.

Suppose:

\[
2\vec v.
\]

That means a displacement in the same direction but twice as large.

Similarly:

\[
\frac12\vec v
\]

is half the displacement.

And:

\[
-\vec v
\]

reverses it.

Conceptually:

```text
v     : ────→

2v    : ────────→

0.5v  : ──→

-v    : ←────
```

So the scalar changes the **amount and potentially orientation sign** of the displacement.

The exact quantitative ideas of vector magnitude and normalization come later in Parts 526–529.

---

# 504.12 Direction is not the same thing as vector

Suppose:

```text
v1 = 1 metre east
v2 = 100 metres east
```

They point in the same direction.

But:

\[
\vec v_1\neq\vec v_2.
\]

Direction is only one property of a vector.

A displacement also carries an amount.

This is why saying:

> a vector is a direction

is incomplete.

A better statement is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{A geometric vector represents directed displacement.}
}
\]

Later we can normalize a nonzero vector to isolate direction, but that is a separate operation.

---

# 504.13 A vector is also not a position

Imagine a game character is at:

```text
position = 10 metres east of world origin
```

and its velocity corresponds to:

```text
movement direction = 10 metres per second east
```

Both might eventually be stored using three floating-point values.

But mathematically they are not the same type of object.

A position tells you **where something is**.

A displacement tells you **how to move from one position to another**.

Full treatment of this distinction is intentionally deferred to Parts 521–522:

- affine points versus free vectors;
- position vectors and why points are not mathematically vectors.

For now retain:

\[
\boxed{
\text{same storage shape does not imply same geometric meaning}
}
\]

That principle becomes extremely important in engine math.

---

# 504.14 The arrow's tail is irrelevant for a free vector

Suppose:

```text
arrow A:
(near a tree) ───→

arrow B:
(inside a spaceship) ───→
```

If they represent equal displacements, then:

\[
\vec a=\vec b.
\]

This means a vector does **not** remember:

```text
where it started
```

unless we explicitly attach it to a location.

A normal vector at a surface is sometimes conceptually attached to that surface point, for example, but the vector's algebraic identity is still the displacement/direction object.

Later geometry makes distinctions between free, bound, tangent and other vector roles.

---

# 504.15 First implementation glimpse

The mathematical object exists independently of implementation.

But an engine needs bytes.

A semantic API might eventually contain:

```cpp
struct Displacement3 {
    float dx;
    float dy;
    float dz;
};
```

Notice the names:

```text
dx
dy
dz
```

rather than merely

```text
x
y
z
```

They emphasize that these are changes along coordinate directions.

For example:

```cpp
Displacement3 walkForward {
    0.0f,
    0.0f,
    -5.0f
};
```

But those numbers already assume we have chosen coordinate directions.

That transition is precisely Part 505.

The key architecture is:

```text
geometric displacement
        ↓
choose coordinate system
        ↓
obtain scalar components
        ↓
store components in memory
```

Never reverse that chain mentally into:

```text
three floats
therefore vector
```

because three floats could represent almost anything.

---

# 504.16 Complexity

A geometric concept does not inherently have computational complexity.

But once represented in fixed-dimensional coordinates, operations like addition become constant-sized arithmetic.

For 3D:

\[
\vec u+\vec v
\]

requires three component additions.

So for fixed dimension 3:

\[
O(1).
\]

For a general \(n\)-dimensional representation:

\[
O(n).
\]

That distinction matters.

"Vector addition is \(O(1)\)" is only true when the dimension is a compile-time/fixed constant such as 2, 3 or 4.

---

# 504.17 What goes wrong if you don't internalize this Part?

You end up thinking of vectors merely as structs.

Then engine code becomes:

```cpp
Vec3 thing;
```

and you stop asking:

> What does `thing` mean?

That leads to bugs such as:

```cpp
worldPosition += cameraPosition;
```

when the operation makes no geometric sense.

Or:

```cpp
velocity = normalize(position);
```

because both happen to be `Vec3`.

A good graphics engineer asks:

```text
What geometric object does this value represent?
```

before asking:

```text
What C++ type contains it?
```

---

# PART 505 — VECTORS AS COORDINATE TUPLES

Now we solve the computational problem.

A geometric displacement is abstract.

A computer cannot directly store:

> move somewhat northeast and upward.

It stores numbers.

We therefore need a numerical representation.

---

# 505.1 What is a tuple?

A **tuple** is an ordered collection of elements.

For example:

\[
(3,5)
\]

is a 2-tuple.

\[
(3,5,7)
\]

is a 3-tuple.

The word **ordered** matters.

\[
(3,5)\neq(5,3)
\]

in general.

The first position and second position have different roles.

This differs from an unordered mathematical set such as

\[
\{3,5\},
\]

where ordering is not the defining feature.

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{tuple = ordered sequence of components}}
\]

---

# 505.2 Components

Consider:

\[
\vec v=(v_x,v_y,v_z).
\]

The individual scalars

\[
v_x,\quad v_y,\quad v_z
\]

are called **components** or **coordinates**.

Each tells us how much of the displacement occurs along one chosen reference direction.

For conventional Cartesian 3D coordinates:

```text
x component → amount along X direction
y component → amount along Y direction
z component → amount along Z direction
```

Suppose:

\[
\vec v=(3,2).
\]

This can mean:

```text
3 units along the chosen X direction
+
2 units along the chosen Y direction
```

Geometrically:

```text
         ● destination
        /|
       / |
      /  | 2
     /   |
    /    |
   ●-----●
      3
```

The diagonal displacement is the vector.

The numbers \(3\) and \(2\) describe that vector relative to our chosen axes.

---

# 505.3 Why order matters

Compare:

\[
(3,2)
\]

and

\[
(2,3).
\]

The first means:

```text
3 along X
2 along Y
```

The second means:

```text
2 along X
3 along Y
```

Those generally describe different displacements.

So coordinate tuples are not bags of numbers.

Component position carries semantic meaning.

In memory:

```text
index 0 → X
index 1 → Y
index 2 → Z
```

must remain a stable contract.

If some code interprets:

```text
[x, y, z]
```

while another interprets:

```text
[z, y, x]
```

you have changed the mathematical representation.

---

# 505.4 \(\mathbb R^2\) and \(\mathbb R^3\)

Because graphics usually uses real scalars, a 2D coordinate tuple is often an element of:

\[
\mathbb R^2.
\]

Meaning:

\[
\mathbb R^2
=
\{(x,y)\mid x,y\in\mathbb R\}.
\]

Likewise:

\[
\mathbb R^3
=
\{(x,y,z)\mid x,y,z\in\mathbb R\}.
\]

And in general:

\[
\mathbb R^n
=
\{
(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)
\mid
x_i\in\mathbb R
\}.
\]

The superscript does **not** mean exponentiation here.

\[
\mathbb R^3
\]

means roughly:

> ordered triples of real numbers.

---

# 505.5 Coordinate-wise vector addition

Suppose:

\[
\vec u=(u_x,u_y)
\]

and

\[
\vec v=(v_x,v_y).
\]

Geometrically, we know vector addition means composing displacements.

If the first movement contributes \(u_x\) horizontally and the second contributes \(v_x\), then total horizontal displacement must be:

\[
u_x+v_x.
\]

Likewise vertically:

\[
u_y+v_y.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
(u_x,u_y)+(v_x,v_y)
=
(u_x+v_x,\;u_y+v_y)
}
\]

This is why vector addition becomes **component-wise addition**.

It is not merely a programming convention.

It follows from the fact that each coordinate records displacement along one independent reference direction.

---

# 505.6 Worked addition

Take:

\[
\vec u=(3,2)
\]

and:

\[
\vec v=(-1,4).
\]

Then:

\[
\vec u+\vec v
=
(3+(-1),\;2+4)
\]

so:

\[
\boxed{(2,6)}.
\]

Interpretation:

First movement:

```text
+3 X
+2 Y
```

Second:

```text
-1 X
+4 Y
```

Total:

```text
+2 X
+6 Y
```

The machine operation is precisely the geometric bookkeeping.

---

# 505.7 Scalar multiplication in coordinates

Suppose:

\[
\vec v=(x,y)
\]

and scalar:

\[
s\in\mathbb R.
\]

Scaling the entire displacement by \(s\) must scale every directional contribution by \(s\).

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
s(x,y)
=
(sx,sy)
}
\]

and in 3D:

\[
s(x,y,z)
=
(sx,sy,sz).
\]

Example:

\[
3(2,-1)
=
(6,-3).
\]

Again, Part 519 later focuses specifically on this operation; we are establishing only the coordinate rule necessary here.

---

# 505.8 Negation in coordinates

Set:

\[
s=-1.
\]

Then:

\[
-\vec v
=
(-x,-y,-z).
\]

Example:

\[
-(3,-2,5)
=
(-3,2,-5).
\]

Every component reverses.

That corresponds geometrically to reversing the whole displacement.

---

# 505.9 The zero vector in coordinates

The identity displacement from Part 504 becomes:

\[
\boxed{
\vec0=(0,0,\ldots,0)
}
\]

because it must contribute zero displacement along every coordinate direction.

In 3D:

\[
\vec0=(0,0,0).
\]

Then:

\[
(x,y,z)+(0,0,0)
=
(x,y,z).
\]

So the coordinate representation matches the geometric identity.

---

# 505.10 Actual C++ representation

A canonical 3D implementation might be:

```cpp
struct Vec3 {
    float x {};
    float y {};
    float z {};
};
```

Conceptual memory layout:

```text
Vec3
address +0
┌───────────────┐
│ x : float     │ 4 bytes
├───────────────┤
│ y : float     │ 4 bytes
├───────────────┤
│ z : float     │ 4 bytes
└───────────────┘
```

Logically this contains:

\[
3\times32=96\text{ bits}
\]

of payload when `float` is IEEE-754 binary32.

A plain, naturally aligned `struct` of three `float`s is commonly 12 bytes on many C++ ABIs.

But do **not** turn that into a universal law.

Alignment annotations, SIMD wrappers, GPU layout rules, or library-specific types can produce a 16-byte physical representation.

The dedicated packed-versus-aligned `Vec3` issue belongs later in Parts 593–594.

For now:

\[
\boxed{
\text{logical components and physical struct size are related but not identical concepts}
}
\]

---

# 505.11 Implementing arithmetic

```cpp
struct Vec3 {
    float x {};
    float y {};
    float z {};

    constexpr Vec3 operator+(const Vec3& rhs) const noexcept {
        return {
            x + rhs.x,
            y + rhs.y,
            z + rhs.z
        };
    }

    constexpr Vec3 operator-() const noexcept {
        return {
            -x,
            -y,
            -z
        };
    }

    constexpr Vec3 operator-(const Vec3& rhs) const noexcept {
        return {
            x - rhs.x,
            y - rhs.y,
            z - rhs.z
        };
    }

    constexpr Vec3 operator*(float s) const noexcept {
        return {
            x * s,
            y * s,
            z * s
        };
    }
};
```

Look at:

```cpp
x + rhs.x
```

Mathematically it is adding the two displacements' contributions along the first reference direction.

Likewise:

```cpp
y + rhs.y
```

adds the contributions along the second.

The code is not manipulating arbitrary struct fields.

It is implementing the coordinate form of geometric addition.

---

# 505.12 A generic \(n\)-dimensional representation

For dimensions not known semantically as X/Y/Z, we could use:

```cpp
template<std::size_t N>
struct Vector {
    std::array<float, N> component {};
};
```

Then addition:

```cpp
template<std::size_t N>
Vector<N> operator+(const Vector<N>& a,
                    const Vector<N>& b) noexcept {
    Vector<N> result;

    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < N; ++i) {
        result.component[i] =
            a.component[i] + b.component[i];
    }

    return result;
}
```

This has time cost:

\[
O(N).
\]

Memory:

\[
O(N).
\]

A fixed `Vec3` version executes only three additions, so practical compilers typically unroll it entirely.

---

# 505.13 Why dimensions must match

What would this mean?

\[
(1,2)+(3,4,5)
\]

There is no component-to-component correspondence for the third coordinate.

So ordinary vector addition is only defined between vectors in the same vector space/dimension.

A type system can enforce this:

```cpp
Vector<2> a;
Vector<3> b;

// no matching operator+
```

This is more than convenience.

It encodes a mathematical invariant:

\[
\boxed{
\text{vector addition requires both operands to belong to the same space}
}
\]

---

# 505.14 Memory representation versus mathematical dimension

Consider:

```cpp
struct Vec3 {
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
};
```

Mathematically this models a point in the coordinate set:

\[
\mathbb R^3.
\]

Machine representation, however, contains only finitely many floating-point values.

So the actual representable set is something like:

\[
F^3
\]

where \(F\) is the finite set of IEEE floating-point patterns—not literally \(\mathbb R^3\).

This is the same mathematical-versus-machine distinction from Parts 501–502.

Conceptually:

```text
ideal math
   (x,y,z) ∈ ℝ³
       ↓ approximation
machine
   3 × floating-point values
```

---

# 505.15 Tuple does not automatically mean geometric vector

This is a subtle but crucial software-engineering point.

These all occupy three numbers:

```cpp
Vec3 position;
Vec3 velocity;
Vec3 color;
Vec3 scale;
Vec3 normal;
```

But they represent dramatically different things.

Even:

```cpp
struct RGB {
    float r;
    float g;
    float b;
};
```

is an ordered triple.

That does not automatically make it a geometric displacement.

Similarly:

```text
temperature samples:
(20.1, 21.3, 19.8)
```

is a 3-tuple.

Tuple is representation structure.

Vector is mathematical semantics.

`\mathbb R^n` itself *can* be treated as a vector space under componentwise operations, which Part 507 will show, but not every tuple-valued application should semantically be treated as unrestricted geometric vector algebra.

---

# 505.16 Indexing

A generalized vector might expose:

```cpp
float& operator[](std::size_t i);
```

so:

```cpp
v[0]
v[1]
v[2]
```

map to:

```text
v[0] → x
v[1] → y
v[2] → z
```

The implementation must define behavior for invalid indices.

This is dangerous:

```cpp
v[7]
```

for a 3-component vector.

Possible strategies include assertions, checked accessors, or relying on caller correctness in performance-sensitive code.

A debug implementation might use:

```cpp
float& operator[](std::size_t i) noexcept {
    assert(i < 3);
    return (&x)[i];
}
```

However, pointer arithmetic through separate struct members deserves C++ object-layout care. A safer generic representation is:

```cpp
std::array<float, 3> data;
```

when array-style indexing is a primary requirement.

That illustrates an implementation lesson:

\[
\boxed{
\text{mathematically equivalent representations can have different language-level safety properties}
}
\]

---

# 505.17 AoS versus SoA glimpse

Suppose one million vectors are stored:

```cpp
std::vector<Vec3> positions;
```

Memory conceptually resembles:

```text
x0 y0 z0 | x1 y1 z1 | x2 y2 z2 | ...
```

That is an **Array of Structures** arrangement.

Another representation could store:

```text
x0 x1 x2 ...
y0 y1 y2 ...
z0 z1 z2 ...
```

which is Structure of Arrays.

You learned those memory-layout ideas earlier in Phase 4.

For ordinary object transforms, `Vec3` AoS is convenient.

For wide SIMD processing of only X components, SoA can be superior.

The geometry has not changed.

Only storage has.

Again:

\[
\boxed{\text{mathematical representation }\neq\text{physical memory layout}}
\]

---

# 505.18 Floating-point component failure cases

A `Vec3<float>` can contain:

\[
(+\infty, 3, 4)
\]

or

\[
(\text{NaN},0,1)
\]

because each component is an IEEE-754 value.

Then:

```cpp
a + b
```

may propagate NaNs.

For example:

\[
\text{NaN}+4=\text{NaN}.
\]

So although mathematically vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) have real components, the machine representation can enter states that do not correspond cleanly to ordinary real-valued geometry.

Production math libraries often add debug checks such as:

```cpp
bool isFinite(Vec3 v) noexcept {
    return std::isfinite(v.x)
        && std::isfinite(v.y)
        && std::isfinite(v.z);
}
```

This can be extremely valuable during engine development.

# PART 506 — THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN A VECTOR AND ITS COORDINATES

This is one of the most important conceptual lessons in the entire mathematics sequence.

Many programmers unknowingly collapse these two things:

\[
\boxed{\vec v}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{(v_x,v_y,v_z)}.
\]

They are related.

They are **not identical concepts**.

---

# 506.1 The core statement

A geometric vector exists independently of whatever coordinate system we choose to describe it.

The tuple:

\[
(v_x,v_y,v_z)
\]

is a **representation of that vector relative to chosen reference directions**.

Formally, once basis notation is introduced later, we will write something like:

\[
[\vec v]_{\mathcal B}
\]

meaning:

> coordinates of vector \(\vec v\) relative to basis \(\mathcal B\).

The complete basis theory belongs to Parts 512–515.

For this Part, you only need the minimal concept:

A coordinate system chooses reference directions against which we measure the vector.

---

# 506.2 Physical analogy: an address versus a place

Imagine a physical building.

The building exists.

Its GPS coordinates are a description of where the building is within one coordinate convention.

If we change the geographic coordinate system, the numbers may change.

The building did not move.

Likewise:

```text
vector itself
      ≠
coordinates used to describe it
```

Changing representation does not necessarily change the geometric object.

---

# 506.3 A simple 2D example

Suppose our reference directions are:

```text
X → east
Y → north
```

Consider a displacement:

```text
1 metre east
1 metre north
```

Its coordinates are:

\[
(1,1)
\]

in that coordinate system.

Now imagine choosing a new first axis that points northeast.

Call that direction \(X'\).

The same physical displacement is directly along \(X'\).

Its new coordinates could therefore look like:

\[
(\sqrt2,0)
\]

if the new reference directions are unit length and rotated appropriately.

Compare:

\[
(1,1)
\]

versus

\[
(\sqrt2,0).
\]

Those tuples are different.

But the physical displacement is the same.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\vec v\text{ fixed}
\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
\text{coordinates fixed}
}
\]

---

# 506.4 Let's verify that example geometrically

Original vector has horizontal displacement:

\[
1
\]

and vertical displacement:

\[
1.
\]

Its length will later be derived as:

\[
\sqrt{1^2+1^2}
=
\sqrt2.
\]

It points at \(45^\circ\).

If the new coordinate system's first axis points exactly at \(45^\circ\), then the entire displacement lies along that axis.

Therefore its contribution along the new second axis is zero.

So relative to the rotated coordinates:

\[
(\sqrt2,0).
\]

Nothing happened to the vector.

Only our description changed.

---

# 506.5 Coordinates depend on reference directions

This immediately explains why a tuple such as:

\[
(1,0,0)
\]

does not have universal geometric meaning.

What does the first coordinate direction represent?

It might be:

```text
world east
```

or:

```text
camera right
```

or:

```text
object-local right
```

or:

```text
tangent direction on a surface
```

Thus:

\[
(1,0,0)
\]

could refer to different physical directions depending on the coordinate frame.

This is why graphics spends enormous effort tracking spaces.

Later you will repeatedly see:

```text
object space
world space
view space
tangent space
clip space
```

The bytes:

```text
1.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f
```

do not tell you which one you have.

---

# 506.6 Same coordinates do not imply same geometric vector

Suppose:

\[
\vec a=(1,0,0)_{\text{world}}
\]

and:

\[
\vec b=(1,0,0)_{\text{camera}}.
\]

Numerically:

```text
a bytes: 1 0 0
b bytes: 1 0 0
```

Yet if the camera is rotated, camera-right may not point world-right.

Therefore:

\[
\vec a\neq\vec b
\]

geometrically despite identical numbers.

This is a huge source of graphics bugs.

---

# 506.7 Conversely, same vector can have different coordinates

Suppose one physical world-space direction is being described.

World coordinates:

\[
(1,1,0).
\]

Camera rotates.

In camera coordinates, perhaps the same vector becomes:

\[
(\sqrt2,0,0).
\]

So:

```text
different numbers
same geometric vector
```

and the previous section showed:

```text
same numbers
different geometric vectors
```

This destroys the naive identification:

\[
\vec v=(x,y,z)
\]

as a universal statement.

A more accurate idea is:

\[
\boxed{
\vec v
\;\xrightarrow{\text{choose frame/basis}}\;
(v_1,v_2,v_3)
}
\]

---

# 506.8 Units also affect coordinates

Suppose a physical displacement is:

```text
one metre east
```

If coordinates are expressed in metres:

\[
(1,0).
\]

If expressed in centimetres:

\[
(100,0).
\]

The physical vector did not become 100 times longer.

The numerical representation changed because the measurement unit changed.

Therefore components depend on:

- reference directions;
- scale/unit conventions.

This is another reason numerical tuples should never be interpreted without metadata or conventions.

---

# 506.9 Mathematical object versus encoding

Think of three layers:

```text
LAYER 1
Geometric object
v

        ↓ choose coordinate frame

LAYER 2
Coordinate representation
(vx, vy, vz)

        ↓ choose machine scalar format/layout

LAYER 3
Bytes
float x
float y
float z
```

Each transition throws in additional conventions.

Layer 1 does not care whether your engine uses:

```cpp
float
```

or:

```cpp
double
```

Layer 2 does not necessarily care whether the struct is 12 or 16 bytes.

Layer 3 absolutely does.

A strong engine programmer keeps these layers mentally distinct.

---

# 506.10 Why this matters when debugging transformations

Suppose a normal points correctly in model space but looks wrong after rendering.

A novice may inspect:

```cpp
normal = {0, 1, 0};
```

and think:

> the vector looks correct.

But `0,1,0` is only meaningful relative to a space.

The real debugging questions are:

```text
Which space is this expressed in?

Which space does the consumer expect?

What transformation occurred between them?
```

That debugging discipline begins here.

---

# 506.11 The same C++ type hides coordinate-space semantics

Typical engine code often uses:

```cpp
using Vec3 = ...;
```

for everything:

```cpp
Vec3 localPosition;
Vec3 worldPosition;
Vec3 viewDirection;
Vec3 tangentNormal;
Vec3 worldNormal;
```

The compiler sees all five as identical types.

Therefore this compiles:

```cpp
worldNormal += localPosition;
```

even though the operation is geometrically nonsensical.

This is a **type-system weakness**, not mathematical legitimacy.

---

# 506.12 Strong coordinate-space types

A stricter math API could encode space:

```cpp
struct LocalSpace {};
struct WorldSpace {};
struct ViewSpace {};

template<typename Space>
struct Vec3 {
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
};

using LocalVec3 = Vec3<LocalSpace>;
using WorldVec3 = Vec3<WorldSpace>;
using ViewVec3  = Vec3<ViewSpace>;
```

Then:

```cpp
LocalVec3  a;
WorldVec3  b;
```

cannot automatically be added unless an explicit conversion/transformation exists.

This moves an important invariant:

```text
"These vectors must belong to the same coordinate space"
```

from programmer memory into the type system.

That is excellent API design when complexity permits.

---

# 506.13 A transformation changes coordinates according to a rule

Suppose we have coordinates:

\[
[\vec v]_A.
\]

We want coordinates of the same vector in another coordinate system:

\[
[\vec v]_B.
\]

Some mapping must convert between them:

\[
[\vec v]_A
\longrightarrow
[\vec v]_B.
\]

Later, matrices will perform this job:

\[
[\vec v]_B=M[\vec v]_A.
\]

But matrix transformation is Phase 7.

At this stage, understand only:

\[
\boxed{
\text{coordinate conversion is a representation change governed by the relation between coordinate frames}
}
\]

Do not yet memorize a matrix formula.

---

# 506.14 Active versus passive intuition

There are two very different situations that often produce similar-looking numbers.

### Situation A — the vector actually rotates

The coordinate system stays fixed.

The physical geometric vector changes.

### Situation B — the coordinate system rotates

The physical vector stays fixed.

Its coordinates change.

Those are conceptually different operations.

Later this becomes the distinction between active and passive transformations in Part 654.

For now:

```text
rotate object
≠
rotate measuring axes
```

even though related equations can appear.

This distinction prevents a lot of sign/order confusion.

---

# 506.15 A concrete coordinate-space bug

Imagine:

```cpp
Vec3 lightDirectionWorld;
Vec3 normalTangent;
```

Then somebody writes:

```cpp
float lighting = dot(lightDirectionWorld, normalTangent);
```

The code compiles.

The arithmetic runs.

The GPU happily calculates a number.

But geometrically it is meaningless because the two coordinate representations refer to different bases.

The correct operation requires transforming one into the other's coordinate space first.

Later dot products are Parts 534–536.

The bug is worth seeing now because it reveals the importance of Part 506:

\[
\boxed{
\text{coordinate-compatible arithmetic is a semantic precondition}
}
\]

Hardware cannot infer this from three floats.

---

# 506.16 Representation does not determine semantics

Consider these identical bytes:

```text
3F800000 00000000 00000000
```

Interpreted as three `float`s:

```text
1.0 0.0 0.0
```

They might represent:

```text
local direction
world direction
RGB red
scale factor
angular axis
normal
position
velocity
```

The bits contain numbers.

The surrounding program supplies meaning.

This general principle extends far beyond vectors:

\[
\boxed{
\text{data representation is not equivalent to domain semantics}
}
\]

---

# 506.17 API design consequence

Instead of:

```cpp
void setNormal(Vec3 n);
```

an engine might conceptually prefer:

```cpp
void setWorldNormal(WorldNormal n);
```

Instead of:

```cpp
Vec3 transform(Vec3 v);
```

a stronger API could express:

```cpp
WorldVector transform(LocalVector v,
                      LocalToWorld transform);
```

The more mathematical meaning your types carry, the harder it becomes to accidentally combine incompatible data.

The trade-off is more type complexity and conversion machinery.

Production engines choose different points along that spectrum.

---

# 506.18 Debugging checklist for a suspicious vector

When a vector-looking value appears wrong, a useful reasoning sequence is:

```text
What geometric quantity does this represent?
        ↓
Which coordinate space/frame?
        ↓
Which units?
        ↓
Is it a point, displacement, direction, normal, etc.?
        ↓
Which operation produced it?
        ↓
Did that operation preserve/change space?
        ↓
Does the consumer expect the same representation?
```

Notice we are debugging **meaning before numbers**.

That is the habit Part 506 is intended to establish.

---

# PART 507 — VECTOR SPACES AND THEIR AXIOMS

Parts 504–506 were heavily geometric.

Now we make a surprising leap.

Mathematics asks:

> Which properties of geometric vectors actually mattered?

If we identify those properties, perhaps many completely different objects obey the same rules.

Then every theorem proved using only those rules would apply to **all** such objects.

That abstraction is the **vector space**.

This is one of the conceptual turning points of linear algebra.

---

# 507.1 Vector no longer means arrow

After this Part, the word **vector** becomes broader than geometric arrows.

Examples of things that can form vector spaces include:

\[
(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
\]

ordinary coordinate tuples,

but also:

\[
\text{polynomials}
\]

\[
\text{matrices}
\]

\[
\text{functions}
\]

\[
\text{signals}
\]

under appropriate operations.

This can initially feel strange.

How could a polynomial be a vector?

Because in modern linear algebra:

\[
\boxed{
\text{an object is a vector when it belongs to a vector space}
}
\]

and the vector-space axioms define what structural behavior matters.

The arrow is one important example—not the definition of all vectors.

---

# 507.2 What ingredients do we need?

We need:

\[
V
\]

a set of objects we intend to call vectors.

We also need a scalar field:

\[
F.
\]

From Part 501, a field provides scalars supporting ordinary algebra.

In graphics we usually have:

\[
F=\mathbb R.
\]

We then define two operations.

Vector addition:

\[
+:V\times V\rightarrow V.
\]

Read this as:

> give addition two vectors from \(V\), and it produces another vector in \(V\).

And scalar multiplication:

\[
\cdot:F\times V\rightarrow V.
\]

Read:

> give a scalar from \(F\) and vector from \(V\), and it produces another vector in \(V\).

That is the machinery.

But arbitrary operations are not enough.

They must obey particular laws.

---

# 507.3 Formal definition of a vector space

A vector space \(V\) over field \(F\) is a set equipped with vector addition and scalar multiplication satisfying the following laws for all:

\[
\vec u,\vec v,\vec w\in V
\]

and:

\[
a,b\in F.
\]

| Law | Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Addition commutativity | \(\vec u+\vec v=\vec v+\vec u\) | order of adding displacements does not matter |
| Addition associativity | \((\vec u+\vec v)+\vec w=\vec u+(\vec v+\vec w)\) | grouping does not matter |
| Additive identity | \(\exists\vec0:\vec v+\vec0=\vec v\) | there is a zero vector |
| Additive inverse | \(\forall\vec v,\exists-\vec v:\vec v+(-\vec v)=\vec0\) | every vector can be cancelled |
| Scalar identity | \(1\vec v=\vec v\) | multiplying by field identity changes nothing |
| Scalar compatibility | \(a(b\vec v)=(ab)\vec v\) | nested scaling agrees with scalar multiplication |
| Scalar distributes over vector addition | \(a(\vec u+\vec v)=a\vec u+a\vec v\) | scaling a sum equals summing scaled vectors |
| Vector distributes over scalar addition | \((a+b)\vec v=a\vec v+b\vec v\) | adding scale factors behaves consistently |

Closure is built into the operation signatures:

\[
V\times V\to V
\]

and:

\[
F\times V\to V.
\]

Some textbooks list closure separately and therefore present ten conditions rather than eight axioms plus closure.

The mathematics is the same.

---

# 507.4 Why are these axioms here?

They are not random bureaucracy.

They capture the algebraic behavior we observed from displacement.

For geometric displacements:

\[
\vec u+\vec v
\]

must itself be another displacement.

Reordering two displacements gives the same resulting displacement.

There is a "do nothing" displacement.

Every displacement can be reversed.

Scaling twice by \(b\) then \(a\) should be equivalent to scaling once by \(ab\).

And scaling a composed displacement should be equivalent to scaling the pieces.

The axioms preserve these intuitions while removing dependency on geometry.

That is abstraction.

---

# 507.5 Closure

Suppose:

\[
\vec u,\vec v\in V.
\]

Then:

\[
\vec u+\vec v
\]

must also belong to \(V\).

Similarly if:

\[
a\in F
\]

and:

\[
\vec v\in V,
\]

then:

\[
a\vec v\in V.
\]

Why?

Because otherwise the operation can throw you outside the mathematical object you're trying to study.

For example, consider only positive real numbers:

\[
V=\{x\in\mathbb R:x>0\}.
\]

Take:

\[
v=2.
\]

Scale by:

\[
a=-1.
\]

Then:

\[
av=-2.
\]

But:

\[
-2\notin V.
\]

Therefore positive real numbers are not a real vector space under ordinary scalar multiplication.

Closure fails.

---

# 507.6 Additive commutativity

\[
\vec u+\vec v=\vec v+\vec u.
\]

For coordinate tuples:

\[
(u_1,u_2)+(v_1,v_2)
=
(u_1+v_1,u_2+v_2).
\]

Because real addition is commutative:

\[
u_1+v_1=v_1+u_1
\]

and:

\[
u_2+v_2=v_2+u_2.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\vec u+\vec v=\vec v+\vec u.
\]

The vector property is inherited from scalar arithmetic.

---

# 507.7 Additive associativity

For:

\[
\vec u=(u_1,\ldots,u_n),
\]

\[
\vec v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n),
\]

\[
\vec w=(w_1,\ldots,w_n),
\]

we compare:

\[
(\vec u+\vec v)+\vec w
\]

with:

\[
\vec u+(\vec v+\vec w).
\]

Each component reduces to:

\[
(u_i+v_i)+w_i
\]

versus:

\[
u_i+(v_i+w_i).
\]

Real-number associativity makes them equal mathematically.

Therefore coordinate vector addition is associative.

And here we encounter machine reality again:

IEEE floating-point addition is not perfectly associative.

Therefore a `Vec3<float>` implementation is only an **approximation of real vector-space arithmetic**.

Mathematical:

\[
(\vec u+\vec v)+\vec w
=
\vec u+(\vec v+\vec w).
\]

Machine:

```cpp
(u + v) + w
```

can differ slightly from:

```cpp
u + (v + w);
```

That distinction matters later for deterministic physics, parallel reductions and numerical algorithms.

---

# 507.8 Additive identity

There must exist:

\[
\vec0\in V
\]

such that:

\[
\vec v+\vec0=\vec v
\]

for every:

\[
\vec v\in V.
\]

For:

\[
V=\mathbb R^3,
\]

the zero vector is:

\[
(0,0,0).
\]

Because:

\[
(x,y,z)+(0,0,0)
=
(x,y,z).
\]

---

# 507.9 Additive inverse

For each:

\[
\vec v\in V
\]

there must exist:

\[
-\vec v
\]

with:

\[
\vec v+(-\vec v)=\vec0.
\]

In \(\mathbb R^3\):

\[
-(x,y,z)
=
(-x,-y,-z).
\]

Then:

\[
(x,y,z)+(-x,-y,-z)
=
(0,0,0).
\]

Again, this requirement eliminates many intuitive-looking sets from being vector spaces.

---

# 507.10 Unit-length vectors are NOT a vector space

Suppose:

\[
U=
\{
\vec v\in\mathbb R^3:
\|\vec v\|=1
\}.
\]

You have not formally studied norms yet, but conceptually this means vectors of length one.

Take:

\[
\vec v\in U.
\]

Multiply by:

\[
2.
\]

Then:

\[
2\vec v
\]

has length two, not one.

Therefore:

\[
2\vec v\notin U.
\]

Closure under scalar multiplication fails.

Also the zero vector is not unit length.

So unit directions are not a vector space.

This becomes important later when normals and directions are normalized.

---

# 507.11 Scalar identity

The field \(F\) has multiplicative identity:

\[
1.
\]

Vector-space arithmetic requires:

\[
\boxed{
1\vec v=\vec v
}
\]

because scaling by one should leave the vector unchanged.

For coordinates:

\[
1(x,y,z)
=
(1x,1y,1z)
=
(x,y,z).
\]

---

# 507.12 Compatibility of scalar multiplication

We require:

\[
\boxed{
a(b\vec v)
=
(ab)\vec v
}
\]

Meaning:

```text
scale by b
then scale by a
```

must be equivalent to:

```text
scale once by a × b
```

Example:

\[
2(3\vec v)=6\vec v.
\]

Without this law, scalar multiplication would not behave like coherent scaling.

---

# 507.13 Distributivity over vector addition

We require:

\[
\boxed{
a(\vec u+\vec v)
=
a\vec u+a\vec v
}
\]

Let's verify in 2D.

Let:

\[
\vec u=(u_x,u_y)
\]

and:

\[
\vec v=(v_x,v_y).
\]

Left side:

\[
a(\vec u+\vec v)
\]

becomes:

\[
a(u_x+v_x,\;u_y+v_y)
\]

therefore:

\[
(a(u_x+v_x),\;a(u_y+v_y)).
\]

Using scalar distributivity:

\[
(au_x+av_x,\;au_y+av_y).
\]

Right side:

\[
a\vec u+a\vec v
\]

equals:

\[
(au_x,au_y)+(av_x,av_y)
\]

which becomes:

\[
(au_x+av_x,\;au_y+av_y).
\]

Both match.

So the vector-space law follows from field distributivity.

---

# 507.14 Distributivity over scalar addition

We also require:

\[
\boxed{
(a+b)\vec v
=
a\vec v+b\vec v
}
\]

Example:

\[
(2+3)\vec v=5\vec v.
\]

Other side:

\[
2\vec v+3\vec v.
\]

Both represent five copies' worth of scaling.

For coordinates:

\[
(a+b)(x,y)
\]

equals:

\[
((a+b)x,(a+b)y).
\]

Scalar distributivity gives:

\[
(ax+bx,ay+by).
\]

And:

\[
a(x,y)+b(x,y)
\]

gives exactly the same result.

---

# 507.15 Why these axioms are powerful

Suppose you prove some theorem using only the eight vector-space laws.

Then the theorem is not merely true for arrows in 3D.

It is automatically true for every vector space.

That might include:

```text
R² coordinate vectors
R³ coordinate vectors
Rⁿ vectors
polynomials
matrices
functions
signals
```

This is why abstraction is so powerful.

You prove structure once.

Then reuse the result everywhere the structure exists.

---

# 507.16 Example 1 — \(\mathbb R^n\)

The most familiar example is:

\[
V=\mathbb R^n.
\]

Vectors:

\[
(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
\]

Addition:

\[
(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
+
(y_1,\ldots,y_n)
=
(x_1+y_1,\ldots,x_n+y_n).
\]

Scalar multiplication:

\[
a(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
=
(ax_1,\ldots,ax_n).
\]

All vector-space axioms follow from the corresponding field properties of real numbers.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb R^n
\text{ is a vector space over }\mathbb R.
}
\]

---

# 507.17 Example 2 — complex coordinates as a real vector space

Consider:

\[
\mathbb C.
\]

You already know \(\mathbb C\) is itself a field.

So \(\mathbb C\) is certainly a vector space over \(\mathbb C\).

But interestingly, it can also be considered a vector space over \(\mathbb R\).

A complex number:

\[
a+bi
\]

has two real degrees of freedom.

Over the real scalars, it behaves analogously to a two-dimensional vector space.

This foreshadows an important idea:

\[
\boxed{
\text{dimension depends partly on which scalar field you choose}
}
\]

Dimension is formally Part 513, so we will not develop this further yet.

---

# 507.18 Example 3 — polynomials

Consider all real polynomials such as:

\[
p(x)=2+3x-x^2.
\]

Another:

\[
q(x)=5-7x+4x^2.
\]

We can add them:

\[
(p+q)(x)
=
7-4x+3x^2.
\]

We can scale them:

\[
2p(x)
=
4+6x-2x^2.
\]

There is a zero polynomial:

\[
0.
\]

Every polynomial has an additive inverse:

\[
-p(x).
\]

All vector-space laws hold.

Therefore polynomial sets of appropriate form can be vector spaces.

Notice:

```text
no arrow
no X/Y/Z spatial displacement
```

yet mathematically they are vectors.

This is where "vector" stops meaning merely geometric arrow.

---

# 507.19 Example 4 — matrices

Take all real \(2\times2\) matrices.

Example:

\[
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
3&4
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

We can add matrices component-wise.

We can multiply every entry by a real scalar.

There is a zero matrix.

Every matrix has an additive inverse.

Therefore:

\[
M_{2\times2}(\mathbb R)
\]

forms a vector space over \(\mathbb R\).

Again:

\[
\boxed{\text{matrices themselves can be vectors in an abstract vector space}}
\]

even though later matrices will also act as transformations between vector spaces.

Context determines the role.

---

# 507.20 Example 5 — functions

Consider real-valued functions:

\[
f:\mathbb R\rightarrow\mathbb R.
\]

Define addition pointwise:

\[
(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x).
\]

Define scalar multiplication:

\[
(af)(x)=a\,f(x).
\]

The zero vector is the zero function:

\[
0(x)=0.
\]

The inverse of \(f\) is:

\[
(-f)(x)=-f(x).
\]

Under suitable choice of function set, these form a vector space.

So one "vector" can be an entire function.

This becomes important in advanced graphics and numerical methods because signals, basis functions and approximations are frequently treated vector-space-theoretically.

---

# 507.21 Non-example — points

Geometric points do not naturally form the same vector space as displacements.

Why?

Ask:

> What is twice the location Paris?

Or:

> What is New York + Tokyo?

Those operations do not naturally have the same geometric meaning as doubling or adding displacements.

You can assign coordinates to positions and numerically manipulate them, but that does not mean the underlying geometric points naturally possess vector-space structure.

This leads later to **affine spaces**, Parts 521–522 and 571.

The important preview:

```text
vector + vector      → vector
point + vector       → point
point - point        → vector
point + point        → generally not geometrically meaningful
```

Do not treat this as the full affine-space lesson yet, but remember the distinction.

---

# 507.22 Non-example — positive RGB light values

Suppose physically valid radiance values must satisfy:

\[
r,g,b\ge0.
\]

Take:

\[
(1,0,0).
\]

Multiply by:

\[
-1.
\]

You obtain:

\[
(-1,0,0),
\]

which is outside the physical nonnegative domain.

Therefore the restricted set of physically valid nonnegative RGB values is not a vector space over \(\mathbb R\).

Yet the surrounding unrestricted numerical space:

\[
\mathbb R^3
\]

is.

This distinction appears everywhere:

\[
\boxed{
\text{an underlying vector space may contain a physically valid subset that is not itself a vector space}
}
\]

---

# 507.23 Deriving facts from the axioms

Now comes the important part.

Once we have axioms, we do **not** need to declare every familiar property separately.

We can prove new ones.

This is what it means to own the structure rather than memorize a list.

---

# 507.24 Proof that \(0\vec v=\vec0\)

There are two different zeroes here.

\[
0
\]

on the left is the scalar zero from the field.

\[
\vec0
\]

on the right is the vector zero.

We want to prove:

\[
\boxed{
0\vec v=\vec0.
}
\]

Start with:

\[
0=0+0.
\]

Multiply \(\vec v\):

\[
0\vec v=(0+0)\vec v.
\]

Using distributivity:

\[
0\vec v
=
0\vec v+0\vec v.
\]

Now add the additive inverse of \(0\vec v\) to both sides.

Left:

\[
0\vec v-(0\vec v)=\vec0.
\]

Right:

\[
0\vec v+0\vec v-(0\vec v)
=
0\vec v.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
0\vec v=\vec0.
}
\]

This was **derived**.

It did not need to be an axiom.

---

# 507.25 Proof that \(a\vec0=\vec0\)

Now take the vector zero.

Because:

\[
\vec0+\vec0=\vec0,
\]

multiply by scalar \(a\):

\[
a\vec0
=
a(\vec0+\vec0).
\]

Distributivity gives:

\[
a\vec0
=
a\vec0+a\vec0.
\]

Subtract \(a\vec0\) from both sides:

\[
\boxed{
a\vec0=\vec0.
}
\]

Again, derived from the axioms.

---

# 507.26 Proof that \((-1)\vec v=-\vec v\)

We know:

\[
1+(-1)=0.
\]

Multiply \(\vec v\):

\[
(1+(-1))\vec v
=
0\vec v.
\]

Distribute:

\[
1\vec v+(-1)\vec v
=
\vec0.
\]

Using scalar identity:

\[
\vec v+(-1)\vec v
=
\vec0.
\]

But \(-\vec v\) is defined as the unique additive inverse satisfying:

\[
\vec v+(-\vec v)=\vec0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
(-1)\vec v=-\vec v.
}
\]

So even vector negation follows naturally from scalar multiplication.

---

# 507.27 Why the zero vector is unique

Suppose two zero vectors existed:

\[
\vec0_A
\]

and:

\[
\vec0_B.
\]

Because \(\vec0_A\) is an additive identity:

\[
\vec0_A+\vec0_B=\vec0_B.
\]

Because \(\vec0_B\) is also an additive identity:

\[
\vec0_A+\vec0_B=\vec0_A.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\vec0_A=\vec0_B.
}
\]

So a vector space cannot have two distinct additive identities.

The zero vector is unique.

---

# 507.28 Why additive inverses are unique

Suppose both:

\[
\vec a
\]

and:

\[
\vec b
\]

are additive inverses of \(\vec v\).

Thus:

\[
\vec v+\vec a=\vec0
\]

and:

\[
\vec v+\vec b=\vec0.
\]

Start with:

\[
\vec a.
\]

Insert the identity:

\[
\vec a
=
\vec a+\vec0.
\]

Since:

\[
\vec v+\vec b=\vec0,
\]

replace \(\vec0\):

\[
\vec a
=
\vec a+(\vec v+\vec b).
\]

Associativity:

\[
=
(\vec a+\vec v)+\vec b.
\]

Commutativity:

\[
=
(\vec v+\vec a)+\vec b.
\]

But:

\[
\vec v+\vec a=\vec0.
\]

So:

\[
=
\vec0+\vec b
=
\vec b.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\vec a=\vec b.
}
\]

Every vector has exactly one additive inverse.

---

# 507.29 If \(a\vec v=\vec0\), what can we infer?

Suppose:

\[
a\vec v=\vec0.
\]

If:

\[
a=0,
\]

this is automatically true.

But suppose:

\[
a\neq0.
\]

Because \(F\) is a field, \(a\) has inverse:

\[
a^{-1}.
\]

Multiply both sides by \(a^{-1}\):

\[
a^{-1}(a\vec v)
=
a^{-1}\vec0.
\]

Compatibility gives:

\[
(a^{-1}a)\vec v
=
\vec0.
\]

Since:

\[
a^{-1}a=1,
\]

we get:

\[
1\vec v=\vec0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\vec v=\vec0.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
a\vec v=\vec0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
a=0\text{ or }\vec v=\vec0.
}
\]

That resembles the zero-product property of field scalars and is extremely useful algebraically.

---

# 507.30 Why integers create trouble as scalars

Suppose someone wants a vector space "over integers."

The standard vector-space definition requires the scalars to form a field.

But:

\[
\mathbb Z
\]

is not a field because, for example, \(2\) has no multiplicative inverse in \(\mathbb Z\).

Without inverses, many linear-algebra results stop working.

Structures using integer scalars do exist, but mathematically they are generally studied under other names such as **modules**, not ordinary vector spaces over a field.

That distinction matters later if you use integer coordinate grids.

---

# 507.31 C++ cannot enforce vector-space axioms automatically

We might write:

```cpp
template<typename V, typename S>
concept VectorLike =
    requires(V a, V b, S s) {
        { a + b } -> std::same_as<V>;
        { s * a } -> std::same_as<V>;
    };
```

This checks that the operations exist.

But it cannot prove:

\[
a+b=b+a
\]

for every possible value.

Nor can it prove:

\[
s(a+b)=sa+sb.
\]

These are **semantic laws**.

A type may satisfy the syntactic interface while violating the mathematical contract.

This is an important generic-programming principle:

\[
\boxed{
\text{an API signature can enforce operation availability but not automatically prove algebraic laws}
}
\]

---

# 507.32 Property testing

Although C++ cannot generally prove the laws, tests can sample them.

For exact integer-like toy arithmetic:

```cpp
assert((u + v) == (v + u));
```

For floating-point vectors, exact equality is dangerous.

You might instead test approximately:

```cpp
bool approx(float a, float b, float eps = 1e-5f) {
    return std::abs(a - b) <= eps;
}
```

and component-wise for vectors.

Then sample random values and test properties such as:

```cpp
approxVec(a + b, b + a);
approxVec(s * (a + b), s * a + s * b);
```

But this does **not prove** the axioms.

And floating-point associativity can genuinely fail beyond tolerance for badly scaled inputs.

So property tests verify your implementation's expected numerical behavior, not mathematical truth.

---

# 507.33 Machine vectors are imperfect algebraic models

Mathematically:

\[
\mathbb R^3
\]

is a vector space.

Machine:

```cpp
struct Vec3 {
    float x, y, z;
};
```

is not literally \(\mathbb R^3\).

Why?

Because `float` itself is not \(\mathbb R\).

For example:

\[
(\vec a+\vec b)+\vec c
\]

may not have identical bits to:

\[
\vec a+(\vec b+\vec c).
\]

NaNs also create pathological behavior.

So in engineering language we usually say:

> `Vec3<float>` implements real-vector arithmetic approximately.

This distinction becomes critical in:

- numerical solvers;
- physics determinism;
- large-world rendering;
- geometric predicates;
- optimization;
- parallel reductions.

---

# 507.34 Vector-space laws and optimization

Compilers sometimes want to rewrite:

\[
a(\vec u+\vec v)
\]

into:

\[
a\vec u+a\vec v.
\]

Mathematically these are equal.

Floating-point arithmetic may produce slightly different rounded results.

This is exactly why aggressive `fast-math` optimizations can change numerical behavior.

The compiler may use mathematical algebraic identities that floating-point arithmetic does not satisfy exactly.

This connects directly back to your Phase 1 study of floating-point reassociation.

# A DEEP WORKED EXAMPLE

Suppose a spacecraft issues two movement commands.

First:

```text
3 units east
2 units north
```

Second:

```text
-1 unit east
4 units north
```

At the pure geometric level, these are two displacements:

\[
\vec u,\vec v.
\]

They exist conceptually before coordinates.

Choose coordinate directions:

```text
X = east
Y = north
```

Then:

\[
[\vec u]=(3,2)
\]

and:

\[
[\vec v]=(-1,4).
\]

Their total displacement is:

\[
\vec w=\vec u+\vec v.
\]

Coordinates:

\[
[\vec w]
=
(3,2)+(-1,4)
\]

\[
=
(2,6).
\]

Now rotate our measuring axes so the first axis points northeast.

The spacecraft did not move again.

Therefore:

\[
\vec w
\]

is unchanged.

But its coordinate tuple will change.

This demonstrates Part 506.

Yet whatever coordinates we choose, addition and scalar multiplication continue obeying the vector-space laws.

That demonstrates Part 507.

The same event can therefore be understood at four levels:

```text
physics/geometric meaning
       ↓
displacement

representation
       ↓
tuple

representation theory
       ↓
coordinates depend on frame

algebraic abstraction
       ↓
vector-space element
```

That layered thinking is exactly what later graphics mathematics requires.

# IMPLEMENTATION: A SEMANTICALLY STRONGER VECTOR TYPE

A canonical implementation might begin:

```cpp
#include <cmath>
#include <cassert>

template<typename Space>
struct Vec3 {
    float x {};
    float y {};
    float z {};

    constexpr Vec3 operator+(const Vec3& rhs) const noexcept {
        return {
            x + rhs.x,
            y + rhs.y,
            z + rhs.z
        };
    }

    constexpr Vec3 operator-(const Vec3& rhs) const noexcept {
        return {
            x - rhs.x,
            y - rhs.y,
            z - rhs.z
        };
    }

    constexpr Vec3 operator-() const noexcept {
        return {-x, -y, -z};
    }

    constexpr Vec3 operator*(float s) const noexcept {
        return {
            x * s,
            y * s,
            z * s
        };
    }

    constexpr Vec3& operator+=(const Vec3& rhs) noexcept {
        x += rhs.x;
        y += rhs.y;
        z += rhs.z;
        return *this;
    }
};
```

And spaces:

```cpp
struct LocalSpace {};
struct WorldSpace {};
struct ViewSpace {};

using LocalVec3 = Vec3<LocalSpace>;
using WorldVec3 = Vec3<WorldSpace>;
using ViewVec3  = Vec3<ViewSpace>;
```

Notice what this architecture achieves.

This compiles:

```cpp
WorldVec3 a;
WorldVec3 b;

WorldVec3 c = a + b;
```

because the coordinate representations belong to the same space.

But this does not:

```cpp
WorldVec3 world;
LocalVec3 local;

// error: different types
auto wrong = world + local;
```

The type system now helps preserve Part 506's semantic distinction.

Actual conversion requires an explicit transformation.

That transformation belongs to later Parts.

# HARDWARE VIEW

For millions of `Vec3<float>` values, the mathematics may say:

\[
\vec r_i=\vec a_i+\vec b_i.
\]

At machine level that becomes repeated scalar loads, arithmetic and stores.

Conceptually:

```text
load ax ay az
load bx by bz

add ax + bx
add ay + by
add az + bz

store rx ry rz
```

For \(N\) vectors:

\[
3N
\]

component additions are required mathematically.

A SIMD implementation can process multiple components/vectors simultaneously.

A GPU can assign separate invocations to different vectors.

But the parallel implementation does not alter the vector-space law being computed.

This is the pattern you should increasingly recognize:

\[
\boxed{
\text{same mathematics, many possible physical executions}
}
\]

# NUMERICAL REALITY

Suppose:

\[
\vec a=(10^{20},0,0)
\]

\[
\vec b=(-10^{20},0,0)
\]

\[
\vec c=(3,0,0).
\]

Mathematically:

\[
(\vec a+\vec b)+\vec c
=
(3,0,0).
\]

But with limited floating-point precision:

\[
\vec b+\vec c
\]

may round back to approximately:

\[
(-10^{20},0,0).
\]

Then:

\[
\vec a+(\vec b+\vec c)
\]

may become:

\[
(0,0,0).
\]

So:

\[
(\vec a+\vec b)+\vec c
\neq
\vec a+(\vec b+\vec c)
\]

at the machine level.

This does not invalidate vector-space mathematics.

It tells us:

```text
real vector space
        ↓ modeled approximately by
floating-point vector implementation
```

An engine programmer must know which layer a proof belongs to.

# COMMON FAILURE MODES ACROSS 504–507

The most dangerous conceptual bug is treating any three floats as interchangeable because the machine type is identical. A world-space velocity, local-space normal, position coordinate and RGB color may all occupy twelve bytes yet obey different semantic rules.

Another failure is believing the coordinates *are* the geometric vector. Rotate the coordinate frame and the components change although the vector can remain fixed.

Another is assuming every collection of vector-looking objects is a vector space. Unit vectors, physically positive RGB values, fixed-length normals and many constrained state spaces fail closure or the zero/inverse requirements.

And another is assuming mathematical vector-space identities hold bit-for-bit under floating-point arithmetic. They often hold only approximately because real algebra and IEEE-754 arithmetic are different structures.

# INTERVIEW ATTACK TREE

### Level 1 — What is a vector?

At the geometric level relevant to Part 504, a vector is a free displacement: a directed amount of movement independent of absolute placement.

At the abstract linear-algebra level, a vector is an element of a vector space.

Those are compatible descriptions at different abstraction levels.

---

### Level 2 — Is a vector just `(x,y,z)`?

No.

\[
(x,y,z)
\]

is a coordinate representation of a vector relative to chosen reference directions.

The same vector can have different coordinates in different bases.

---

### Level 3 — Then why can `Vec3` represent a vector?

Because the program has chosen a coordinate system and maps the abstract/geometric vector to three scalar components stored in the struct.

The struct stores the representation, not the geometric object independently of convention.

---

### Level 4 — Can two identical `Vec3` values represent different vectors?

Yes.

For example:

\[
(1,0,0)_{\text{world}}
\]

and:

\[
(1,0,0)_{\text{camera}}
\]

can describe different physical directions if the camera basis is rotated.

---

### Level 5 — Can one vector have two different coordinate tuples?

Yes.

Changing the coordinate basis changes the tuple representing the same underlying vector.

For example, a northeast displacement might be:

\[
(1,1)
\]

in east/north coordinates but:

\[
(\sqrt2,0)
\]

in a coordinate system whose first axis points northeast.

---

### Level 6 — What exactly is a vector space?

A set \(V\) over scalar field \(F\), equipped with vector addition and scalar multiplication satisfying the vector-space axioms: additive commutativity, associativity, identity and inverses; scalar identity and compatibility; and the two distributive laws.

---

### Level 7 — Why must the scalars come from a field?

Because field inverses and algebraic laws support linear algebra's manipulation of scalar coefficients. Using something like integers removes multiplicative inverses and produces a different algebraic structure.

---

### Level 8 — Are unit vectors a vector space?

No.

Scalar closure fails:

\[
2\vec v
\]

is not unit length.

The zero vector is also absent.

---

### Level 9 — Prove \(0\vec v=\vec0\) without assuming it.

Use:

\[
0=0+0.
\]

Then:

\[
0\vec v
=
(0+0)\vec v
=
0\vec v+0\vec v.
\]

Subtract \(0\vec v\) from both sides:

\[
0\vec v=\vec0.
\]

---

### Level 10 — Why isn't `Vec3<float>` literally a vector space over \(\mathbb R\)?

Because its components are finite IEEE-754 values rather than real numbers, and floating-point arithmetic violates exact real-field properties such as associativity.

It is a computational approximation to real-vector arithmetic.
