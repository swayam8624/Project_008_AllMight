Worlds, Images and Inference — Fast Recall

Fast route

Start with a world description and follow it through the physical and computational steps that form an image. Then ask what the measurements let us recover about the world. Each step changes representation and may discard information.

  1. Represent points, curves, surfaces, and meshes with the data each operation needs.
  2. Place and move geometry with coordinate frames, hierarchies, animation, and simulation.
  3. Measure the scene through optics, exposure, sensors, and camera projection.
  4. Render visible surfaces, material response, and light transport; rasterization and ray queries organize the work differently.
  5. Sample the continuous image with filters and finite pixels, then encode color for a device.
  6. Infer hidden motion, orientation, shape, or object pose from measurements, stating ambiguities and assumptions.

Thirty-five retrieval briefs

Each brief gives a mechanism to recall, the condition that matters, and a concrete question to use when reopening that section. The links target the matching chapter in the main note.

  1. A scene description becomes an image (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 8) — Graphics maps a scene description to image measurements; vision works backward from measurements to constraints on the scene. Both depend on camera, geometry, light, and sampling assumptions. Ask: In a ray renderer and a rasterizer, which direction does the visibility query travel?

  2. Mathematical language: points, vectors, and derivatives (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 8) — A point locates; a vector displaces. In a skew basis, geometric squared length is c T G c , with G = B T B . A Jacobian predicts local change; the Hessian records curvature. Ask: Why does summing squared coordinates fail when basis vectors are not orthogonal?

  3. Coordinate frames, transformations, and pose (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 8) — Homogeneous coordinates let one matrix translate points while leaving direction vectors unchanged. For column vectors, T S means scale first, then translate; order matters. Normals use inverse-transpose under nonuniform scale. Ask: What result does S T produce for a point when translation is accidentally scaled too?

  4. Curves as controllable functions (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 8) — A cubic has four degrees of freedom: endpoint positions and endpoint tangents fit Hermite data. Bézier controls expose tangents; de Casteljau evaluates and subdivides exactly. C 1 matches parameter speed as well as direction; G 1 only matches tangent direction. Ask: Why can a visually tangent join still have a speed jump?

  5. Surfaces, patches, topology, and parameterization (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 9) — A chart maps a parameter domain onto a surface; its partial derivatives span the tangent plane. A chart can distort or become singular even if the surface itself is regular. Ask: What condition on the two tangent vectors makes a parameterization lose a local direction?

  6. Meshes, connectivity, and geometry processing (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 9) — A mesh stores geometry plus adjacency. Half-edges encode directed edge pairs and make face and vertex neighborhoods traversable. Edits must preserve required topology and orientation. See Figure U2 (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 9). Ask: After an edge flip, which adjacent face can become inverted if the new diagonal is geometrically invalid?

  7. Lens optics, focus, and image formation (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — A thin lens maps object distance to image distance; defocus spreads a point into a blur circle. Acceptable focus depends on the blur tolerance and output scale, not a universal depth-of-field boundary. The added derivation estimates a focus interval of about 2.34–4.19 m for its stated setup. Ask: Which chosen circle-of-confusion threshold changes that interval?

  8. Exposure, sensors, and dynamic range (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — Exposure controls collected photons through aperture, shutter time, and sensitivity; equivalent brightness does not imply equivalent motion blur, depth of field, noise, or highlight headroom. Sensor readout and saturation add limits. Ask: If shutter time is halved and aperture widened one stop, what changes besides image brightness?

  9. Cameras, projection, and calibration (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — Pinhole projection x = f X / Z couples image scale to depth. Vanishing points constrain parallel directions; calibration estimates internal parameters and pose from known geometry. Orthographic projection is useful when depth variation is negligible or should not affect measured scale. Ask: Why can three orthogonal vanishing directions still leave a mirror-related calibration solution?

  10. Sampling continuous signals and image formation (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — Sampling records finite values of a continuous signal. Frequencies above half the sample rate can alias to a lower pattern; prefilter before reducing resolution. Pixel-area averaging is already a weak low-pass filter. See Figure U3 (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 10). Ask: How can two different sinusoids produce identical samples on one grid?

  11. Image sampling, filtering, and efficient reconstruction (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 11) — Reconstruction filters trade alias suppression, blur, and ringing. A texture Jacobian maps a pixel footprint into texture space; mipmaps suit near-isotropic footprints, anisotropic filters suit elongated ones. Integral images turn any axis-aligned box sum into four table reads after one cumulative pass. Ask: Why does an integral image not make an arbitrary convolution constant-time?

  12. Color perception, encoding, and display (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 11) — Cone responses compress a spectrum into three values, so distinct spectra can be metamers. Display codes are often nonlinear: decode to linear light before averaging or compositing, then encode for display. Gamut bounds which responses a device can reproduce. Ask: Why does encoded midpoint 0.5 represent roughly 0.22 linear intensity under gamma 2.2?

  13. Light measurement and radiometry (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 11) — Flux, irradiance, intensity, and radiance use different measures. For an area-light element, conversion from area density to solid-angle density uses | n y ⋅ ( − ω ) | / r 2 . Use the same measure as the integral. Ask: Why does sampling an area light require both the inverse-square factor and the light-normal cosine?

  14. Reflectance, BRDFs, and shading (Geometry, Cameras, and Image Formation, Part 1, Ch. 11) — A BRDF describes how incoming direction becomes outgoing radiance. Lambertian reflectance is constant at ρ / π ; mirrors are delta-like and glossy materials form lobes. Ask: Why can a smooth directional sampler fail to hit an ideal mirror reflection?

  15. The rendering equation and light transport (Light Transport and Image Synthesis, Part 1, Ch. 2) — Outgoing radiance is emitted light plus visible reflected incoming radiance integrated over a hemisphere. Since incoming radiance comes from other surfaces, transport is coupled. A renderer makes this executable with visibility, BRDF, light, and accumulation. See Figure U5 (Light Transport and Image Synthesis, Part 1, Ch. 9). Ask: Which term becomes zero when geometry blocks a sampled direction?

  16. Numerical integration and Monte Carlo estimation (Mathematics for Visual Computing, Part 2, Ch. 16) — For samples X ∼ p , estimate ∫ f ( x ) d x with the mean of f ( X ) / p ( X ) . Importance sampling reduces estimator variance only when the density covers every contributing region. Ask: Why does quadrupling independent samples usually halve standard error rather than quarter it?

  17. Rasterization, coverage, and depth (Mathematics for Visual Computing, Part 2, Ch. 16) — Rasterization tests projected primitives at pixel sample locations, interpolates attributes, then depth-tests. Exact edge coverage is an area integral; multisampling estimates it with subpixel tests. Perspective-correct interpolation compensates for the projection divide. Ask: What artifact appears if a checkerboard floor is linearly interpolated in screen space?

  18. Textures and surface detail (Light Transport and Image Synthesis, Part 1, Ch. 8) — Texture is a signal over surface coordinates. Charts introduce seams and distortion; filtering must follow how that domain projects into each pixel. Ask: Why can one square mip level overblur a floor receding toward the horizon?

  19. Visibility queries and ray intersections (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 9) — A ray query asks which geometry intersects a path from an origin; hit parameters, barycentric coordinates, and surface orientation answer different parts of the intersection contract. Ask: What additional bound distinguishes a ray from a finite line segment?

  20. Acceleration structures and robust queries (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — A BVH groups geometry in nested bounds. Traversal skips a node only when its bound cannot intersect the query, so bounds must conservatively contain descendants. Robust predicates matter near tangencies and degenerate geometry. Ask: What correctness failure follows from rounding a bound inward?

  21. Scene graphs and articulated geometry (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — A node’s world transform composes parent and local transforms; skinning blends posed bone candidates using per-vertex weights. Linear blends can collapse volume when averaging opposing rotations. Ask: Why does applying the inverse bind transform preserve a vertex when current pose equals rest pose?

  22. Animation curves and motion paths (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — A moving camera needs position and orientation, plus a valid up direction. Smooth position does not guarantee smooth orientation; rotation interpolation and time parameterization are separate choices. Ask: What makes the camera frame singular when view and up vectors align?

  23. Dynamics, forces, and numerical integration (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — Dynamics evolves state from forces or interaction rules; an integrator approximates continuous time with steps. Large steps can inject energy or destabilize springs and cloth. Ask: Why can explicit Euler make a spring oscillation grow even when the physical model conserves energy?

  24. Optimization, constraints, and inverse kinematics (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 10) — Define variables, objective, constraints, and derivatives before solving. IK linearizes forward kinematics with a Jacobian; feasibility and conditioning govern whether a target is reachable and stable. Ask: What changes when the end-effector Jacobian loses rank?

  25. PDEs, fields, and discrete solvers (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 11) — A PDE, spatial discretization, and boundary conditions together define a numerical problem. Choosing a mesh or basis changes the system matrix; boundary data is part of the model. Ask: Why can two solvers using the same interior equation produce different solutions at the boundary?

  26. Optical flow and motion from images (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 11) — Brightness constancy yields one equation, I x u + I y v + I t = 0 , for two flow components. A local aperture therefore leaves motion along an edge ambiguous. Ask: What does a single straight edge constrain well, and which motion component remains unobserved?

  27. Edges, contours, and subpixel localization (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 11) — Blur spreads an ideal edge; fitting a response profile can estimate its center between pixels. Hough voting accumulates weak local evidence into a global line or shape hypothesis. Ask: Why can an edge’s subpixel estimate shift when the blur model is wrong?

  28. Recognition, templates, and industrial matching (Spatial Worlds, Engine State, and Simulation, Part 1, Ch. 11) — Template matching searches over pose and transformation with a score chosen for expected noise and deformation. Coarse-to-fine search reduces cost; thresholds set tolerance, not truth. Ask: Why should an edge alignment score treat normal displacement differently from tangent displacement?

  29. Pose estimation, transforms, and robust fitting (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 3) — Estimate rigid pose from correspondences, enforce a proper rotation, and use robust fitting when outliers contaminate matches. RANSAC trial count depends on inlier rate and sample size. Ask: Why does lowering the assumed inlier fraction sharply increase the number of RANSAC trials?

  30. Stereo, relative orientation, and reconstruction (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 3) — Two views constrain rays and relative pose; triangulation recovers structure up to gauge freedoms such as global scale. Critical surfaces make the geometry ill-conditioned. Ask: Why can perfect image correspondences still leave metric scale unknown?

  31. Surface orientation from illumination (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 3) — Photometric stereo uses several known light directions to solve for scaled normal from brightness measurements. The lighting matrix must span the components being estimated. See Figure U4 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 3). Ask: Which normal component becomes unobservable if every light direction lies in one plane?

  32. Shape from shading and reflectance (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 3) — A single shading image gives a nonlinear relation among surface normal, reflectance, and illumination; integration into height requires boundary conditions and extra assumptions. Ask: Why does a locally valid normal field fail to guarantee one globally consistent surface?

  33. Surface descriptions and Gaussian images (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 3) — A Gaussian image maps surface area to normal directions. Its directional mass distribution summarizes orientation and curvature patterns but can be shared by geometrically different surfaces. Ask: Why does the zero-centroid condition hold for a closed surface, and what does it rule out?

  34. Photographic composition, history, and communication (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 4) — Viewpoint, crop, sequence, and caption shape how a photograph functions as evidence or argument. A recorded scene is selected and framed, not a neutral inventory. Ask: What does a wide frame reveal that a close crop hides in the same documentary image?

  35. Photographic workflow and integrated decisions (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 4) — Trace a capture from scene spectrum through optics, exposure, sampling, processing, and display. Each stage can discard information or add artifacts; choose settings around the intended output. Ask: Which stage caused a moiré pattern, and what evidence distinguishes it from JPEG blocking?
