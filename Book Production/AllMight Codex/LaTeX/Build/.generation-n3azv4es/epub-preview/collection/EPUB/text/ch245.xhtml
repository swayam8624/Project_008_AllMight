Light uses the same decomposition in a new role

Our panel is now a surface, not just a movement plane. A ray approaches it with unit direction i pointing toward the interface. Choose a unit normal n pointing back into the incident medium, so front-side incidence obeys i ⋅ n ≤ 0 .

Reflection reverses the normal component

The tangential part is unchanged and the normal part reverses. Therefore reflection across the surface plane gives

r = i − 2 ( i ⋅ n ) n .

For i = ( 0.6 , 0 , − 0.8 ) and n = ( 0 , 0 , 1 ) , the result is ( 0.6 , 0 , 0.8 ) . The length stays one in exact arithmetic. At normal incidence, i = − n reflects to n .

If the normal is not unit, use the general projection denominator:

r = i − 2 i ⋅ n n ⋅ n n .

Reflection across a line instead preserves the along-line component and reverses the perpendicular component. State which mirror you mean. “Reflect around the normal” is otherwise ambiguous.

Refraction changes the tangent amount

For a transparent material with scalar refractive indices n 1 , n 2 , define η = n 1 / n 2 and c i = − i ⋅ n . Snell’s law is

n 1 sin ⁡ θ i = n 2 sin ⁡ θ t .

The incident tangential vector is i + c i n and has length sin ⁡ θ i . The transmitted tangent must therefore be η ( i + c i n ) . To complete a unit transmitted direction, its normal magnitude must be

c t = k , k = 1 − η 2 ( 1 − c i 2 ) .

Choose its normal component into the second medium, opposite n :

t = η i + ( η c i − k ) n .

For air-to-glass indices 1 and 1.5 at 45 ∘ incidence, the transmitted sine is 2 / 3 and the angle is approximately 28.1 ∘ . The ray bends toward the normal. These indices are a simplified example, not a wavelength-dependent material model.

If k < 0 , no real transmitted direction satisfies Snell’s law: total internal reflection occurs. When n 1 > n 2 , the critical angle obeys

θ c = arcsin ⁡ ( n 2 / n 1 ) .

At the threshold, tiny arithmetic errors can change the sign of k . A physically chosen uncertainty policy may treat a narrow interval specially. Unconditionally clamping every negative k to zero destroys the distinction between rounding and genuine total internal reflection.

When a ray leaves rather than enters, orient the normal against the incident direction and use the correct incident/transmitted index ratio. Snell’s law computes a direction, not Fresnel energy splitting, absorption, dispersion, or full material appearance.

The sign conventions and formulas agree with the GLSL reflection/refraction definitions when their input requirements are met; the shader specification’s zero-return convention for total internal reflection differs from our explicit failure result. GLSL 4.60, Geometric Functions.

A half-vector is a bisector only after its inputs are understood

For shading, let l point from the surface toward the light and v toward the viewer. Both are unit directions, which differs from the incident-travel convention above. The half-vector is

h = l + v ‖ l + v ‖ .

Because l ⋅ ( l + v ) = 1 + l ⋅ v and the same expression holds for v , the normalized sum makes equal angles with both directions. If the directions are opposite, their sum is zero and no unique half-vector exists.

If the inputs have unequal magnitudes, their sum is biased toward the larger one and is not the desired angular bisector. The half-vector supports certain specular-lighting models; it does not by itself define a physically complete reflectance law. The panel’s geometry gives a direction, while material modeling determines what that direction means for brightness.

Immediate footing — gradient

For a differentiable scalar function, the gradient is the vector of partial derivatives. Under the ordinary Euclidean metric it points in the direction of steepest increase. For f ( x , y ) = x 2 + 3 y , ∇ f = ( 2 x , 3 ) , so at ( 1 , 2 ) it is ( 2 , 3 ) . A small displacement δ changes the function approximately by ∇ f ⋅ δ . In a non-orthonormal metric, the gradient vector depends on that metric even though the differential is a covector. Gradient descent moves opposite the gradient, with step size controlling progress and stability.

A derivative measures change; a gradient needs a ruler

At a fixed point, the differential d f is a linear measurement of a small displacement h . If the coordinate derivatives form the column g , then d f [ h ] = g T h . The notation does not require that g itself already be the physically correct steepest-direction arrow. It is first a list of coefficients of a covector.

With inner product ⟨ u , h ⟩ G = u T G h , the gradient is the vector satisfying d f [ h ] = ⟨ grad G f , h ⟩ G for every displacement. Therefore

grad G f = G − 1 g .

In ordinary orthonormal Euclidean coordinates G = I , so the distinction is invisible: the coordinate derivative column and gradient arrow have the same numbers. In slanted coordinates or a deliberately weighted error geometry, the distinction changes the answer.

Take f ( x , y ) = x + y and G = diag ( 1 , 4 ) . Then g = ( 1 , 1 ) but grad G f = ( 1 , 1 / 4 ) . Among directions with h T G h ≤ 1 , the largest first-order increase is

max g T h = g T G − 1 g = 5 2 ,

attained by h = ( 1 , 1 / 4 ) / ( 5 / 2 ) . This is the dual-norm bound applied to a derivative. Moving a Euclidean unit distance along ( 1 , 1 ) answers a different optimization question.

If u = B c is a coordinate change, the derivative coefficients transform as g c = B T g u , while the metric transforms as G c = B T G u B . Combining them gives the correct coordinate transformation of the gradient vector. This explains why normals, derivatives and directions can use related but different matrix formulas. Establish the role of the quantity before choosing the multiplication.

For teaching, trace one small move. In the slanted basis B = [ ( 1 , 0 ) , ( 1 , 1 ) ] , let the world function be f ( u x , u y ) = u y . Then f ( B c ) = c 2 , so g c = ( 0 , 1 ) and G c = ( 1 1 1 2 ) . Solving G c z = g c gives z = ( − 1 , 1 ) . Reconstruct B z = ( 0 , 1 ) : the world gradient points vertically, as expected. Treating ( 0 , 1 ) as slanted-basis vector coefficients would instead produce ( 1 , 1 ) and the wrong direction.

The sensor needs coordinates inside the triangle

Cartesian coordinates tell us where the sensor is in the workcell. Barycentric coordinates tell us how it sits relative to the panel’s three corners.

We seek weights satisfying

P = α A + β B + γ C , α + β + γ = 1.

This is shorthand for an affine construction, not permission for unrestricted point arithmetic. Translate all coordinate origins by a vector t . The weighted expression changes by ( α + β + γ ) t = t , exactly as a point should. Without the sum-to-one constraint, it would depend incorrectly on the origin.

Subtract A to obtain a purely vector equation:

P − A = β ( B − A ) + γ ( C − A ) .

For our panel, P − A = ( 1 , 1 , 0 ) , so 4 β = 1 and 3 γ = 1 . Therefore

β = 1 4 , γ = 1 3 , α = 1 − 1 4 − 1 3 = 5 12 .

All weights are nonnegative, so the sensor lies inside or on the triangle. An edge has one zero weight; a vertex has one weight equal to one and the others zero. The triangle’s centroid, its uniform geometric center, has three equal weights 1 / 3 . A barycenter, also spelled barycentre, is a weighted center. With nonnegative masses at the corners, normalized mass weights describe their center of mass. Barycentric weights in general are not distances, and need not be mass fractions. Do not confuse a triangle’s centroid with an unweighted average of vertices for every possible shape.

Derive the weights in a tilted triangle

Let e 1 = B − A , e 2 = C − A , and q = P − A . Dot the reconstruction equation with each edge:

( e 1 ⋅ e 1 e 1 ⋅ e 2 e 1 ⋅ e 2 e 2 ⋅ e 2 ) ( β γ ) = ( e 1 ⋅ q e 2 ⋅ q ) .

This is the two-edge Gram system. Define g 11 , g 12 , g 22 from its left side and r 1 , r 2 from its right side. Eliminating the unknowns gives

D = g 11 g 22 − g 12 2 , β = g 22 r 1 − g 12 r 2 D , γ = g 11 r 2 − g 12 r 1 D .

D = ‖ e 1 × e 2 ‖ 2 . It vanishes for a degenerate triangle. Near degeneracy, solving for weights is sensitive even if a denominator happens to be nonzero in machine arithmetic.

If P is off the plane, this system returns coordinates of its orthogonal projection onto the plane, not proof that P belongs to the triangle. Check the reconstruction residual or signed plane distance separately.

Signed area also answers which side we are on

For two-dimensional points, define

orient 2 ( A , B , C ) = ( B x − A x ) ( C y − A y ) − ( B y − A y ) ( C x − A x ) .

It is the signed doubled area. Positive means counterclockwise in an upward-positive, positively oriented Cartesian drawing; negative means clockwise. Reverse vertical screen coordinates and the visual interpretation reverses too.

The barycentric weights can be written as signed area ratios:

α = orient 2 ( P , B , C ) orient 2 ( A , B , C ) , β = orient 2 ( A , P , C ) orient 2 ( A , B , C ) , γ = orient 2 ( A , B , P ) orient 2 ( A , B , C ) .

For the panel in its plane, the denominator is 12 . The three numerators are 5 , 3 , and 4 , reproducing the earlier weights. With a fixed base, a subtriangle’s area scales with the opposite point’s perpendicular height. That is why an area ratio gives an affine coordinate.

In three dimensions the four-point orientation quantity is

orient 3 ( A , B , C , D ) = ( ( B − A ) × ( C − A ) ) ⋅ ( D − A ) .

It tells which side of the oriented plane contains D . Some libraries define the opposite ordering and hence the opposite sign. Match the documented definition rather than comparing function names.

A predicate needs a trustworthy sign

A predicate answers a categorical question, such as left/right or inside/outside. A measurement estimates a magnitude. A slightly inaccurate area may be acceptable; a wrong sign used to connect a mesh can corrupt topology.

Near cancellation, ordinary floating-point determinants may have the wrong sign. Wider arithmetic helps but is not a universal certificate. Nor is replacing “small” values with zero: that changes the geometry and can make decisions inconsistent.

Adaptive predicates first use a fast calculation with a justified error bound, then use more exact arithmetic when the sign is uncertain. The same weights interpolate attributes

Suppose corner temperatures are 20 , 40 , and 50 degrees Celsius. An affine interpolation gives

T ( P ) = 5 12 ( 20 ) + 1 4 ( 40 ) + 1 3 ( 50 ) = 35.

The weights reconstruct any attribute assumed to vary affinely across this triangle: texture coordinates, scalar fields, and suitable vector-valued data. At a vertex the formula returns that vertex’s attribute. Along an edge the opposite attribute contributes nothing.

Interpolation preserves linear constraints, not all nonlinear ones. A weighted average of unit normals need not be unit. Colors stored in nonlinear transfer-encoded channels need conversion to a linear-light representation before physical light interpolation; see Color encoding and linear-light operations. A unit-direction interpolation needs a specified path and a zero/antiparallel policy, not just a call to lerp.

The barycentric gradients are constant over a flat affine triangle. For our panel, β = x / 4 , γ = y / 3 , and α = 1 − x / 4 − y / 3 . This means the temperature gradients are

∂ T ∂ x = 40 − 20 4 = 5 , ∂ T ∂ y = 50 − 20 3 = 10

in degrees Celsius per metre. A derivative is not an extra decoration: it tells the robot how the interpolated measurement changes as the sensor moves.

Perspective changes the interpolation weights

Screen-space barycentric weights λ i are not generally the original affine surface weights after perspective projection. For an attribute a i and clip-space coordinates w i at the three vertices, perspective-correct interpolation has the form

a ( P ) = ∑ i λ i a i / w i ∑ i λ i / w i .

To see the weighting issue without a full rasterizer, consider two endpoints with w 0 = 1 , w 1 = 2 and equal screen weights 1 / 2 . Their corrected weights are ( 1 / 2 ) / ( 3 / 4 ) = 2 / 3 and ( 1 / 4 ) / ( 3 / 4 ) = 1 / 3 , not equal halves. A texture that ignores this correction can appear to swim or bend incorrectly.

This formula requires a valid projected configuration and clipping policy. A denominator near zero or a primitive crossing the camera’s projective boundary cannot be repaired by casually dividing. The projective story below explains why.

Weighted points lead from affine geometry to collision shapes

We have now met three kinds of combinations. They answer different questions:

Combination Restrictions Meaning
Linear combination of vectors Arbitrary scalar weights Reachable movement in a span.
Affine combination of points Weights sum to one Origin-independent location, including extrapolation.
Convex combination of points Weights sum to one and are nonnegative Weighted location inside the contributors’ convex hull.

An affine combination can lie outside a segment or triangle. For example, 2 B − A extrapolates beyond B . A combination whose weights sum to zero transforms as a displacement rather than a point. These statements follow by translating every contributor and tracking the total translation coefficient.

Convex means every straight connection stays inside

A convex set contains the complete line segment joining any two of its points:

( 1 − t ) P + t Q ∈ C ( P , Q ∈ C ,   0 ≤ t ≤ 1 ) .

A filled disk is convex; its circular boundary alone is not. A rectangle is convex; a crescent-shaped region generally is not. Repeatedly applying the segment property gives closure under any finite convex combination.

The convex hull of a point set is all of its finite convex combinations. It is the smallest convex set containing those points. Imagine a taut rubber band around two-dimensional samples; in three dimensions imagine a tight enclosing convex shell. Interior samples do not necessarily become hull vertices.

The triangular panel is its corners’ convex hull. Convex models simplify collision queries, but a convex hull may fill holes and concavities in the original object. Faster geometry does not mean identical geometry.

Sweeping an object is a set addition

For sets expressed relative to a common frame and origin, the Minkowski sum is

A ⊕ B = { a + b : a ∈ A , b ∈ B } .

Here point locations are represented by position coordinates; the set operation is not an unrestricted intrinsic point-plus-point API. A singleton offset translates a shape. A horizontal segment plus a vertical segment makes a rectangle. A line-segment path plus a radius- r disk makes a capsule-shaped swept region in the plane.

For a circular robot moving around an obstacle O , forbidden center positions are O ⊕ ( − R ) , where R is the robot shape centered at its reference point. A symmetric disk equals its own reflection, so this becomes expansion of the obstacle by the robot radius. Non-symmetric robot shapes need the reflection explicitly.

Convexity is preserved. For x = a 1 + b 1 and y = a 2 + b 2 , their convex mixture is

( 1 − t ) x + t y = [ ( 1 − t ) a 1 + t a 2 ] + [ ( 1 − t ) b 1 + t b 2 ] .

Both bracketed terms remain in their respective convex sets, so the result stays in the sum. A naive vertex-pair construction produces m n candidate sums for m and n vertices; support queries can avoid materializing that whole set.

A swept region answers whether; a time-of-impact equation answers when

We just constructed the swept region of a moving disk as a Minkowski sum. The next question is more demanding: the robot or physics engine needs to know the first time its boundary touches another object, because it must integrate only up to that event before changing velocity. That is a different mathematical object from a yes/no overlap query. It is also the point where convex geometry, quadratic equations, constrained dynamics, and floating-point error become one computational system.

The same contact has a second quadratic structure: effective mass

At the impact, a velocity constraint determines what impulse is required. For planar rigid bodies, let r A = x c − x A and r B = x c − x B be contact-point offsets measured from centres of mass, with n a unit normal pointing A to B. The scalar k n is the inverse effective mass of the contact direction; the rotational terms are not arbitrary corrections but the projection of the angular impulse back into contact-point velocity. A positive inverse mass or inverse inertia is a measure of how readily the body responds, and a static body is represented by zero for both. The expression is nonnegative under physically valid inverse masses. A static/static pair has k n = 0 and cannot be solved by division. In matrix language this is one row of J M − 1 J T , where M − 1 is the generalized inverse-mass matrix and J maps generalized velocity to relative normal velocity.

These are constraints on impulses, not an invitation to add arbitrary forces every frame.

Conservative bounds are a mathematical inclusion guarantee, not a collision decision

A moving disk’s exact swept set over [ 0 , h ] is the Minkowski sum of its centre-path segment and its closed radius- r disk. Its axis-aligned bounding box has x limits min ( x 0 , x 0 + h v x ) − r and max ( x 0 , x 0 + h v x ) + r , and corresponding y limits. Let S i be a disk’s swept set and B i its box. By construction S i ⊆ B i . If two disks ever overlap at a shared time, some point belongs to both swept sets; hence B i ∩ B j ≠ ∅ . This gives the no false negatives proof for the broad phase.

The converse fails: boxes may overlap even if the disks are never at the same place at the same time. Such false positives are intentionally passed to a narrower collision test. In floating-point code, outward rounding and finite-input checks help preserve inclusion. For accelerating bodies, using only linear-motion endpoints may no longer enclose the true path, so the proof’s assumptions must be changed; invoking the same code without stronger motion bounds is unsound.

A full event step repeatedly finds the earliest surviving narrow-phase hit, advances all bodies to that time, solves simultaneous contacts, and recomputes candidates for the remaining interval after velocity changes. In the worked wall impact, after 0.08 seconds the moving disk is at x = 2 , reverses to + 100   m / s , and ends at x = 4 after another 0.02 seconds. Collision becomes a question about the origin

In collision detection, the commonly named Minkowski difference is

A − B = A ⊕ ( − B ) = { a − b } .

It is not ordinary set subtraction, and some mathematical sources use “difference” for erosion instead. Always check the definition.

The shapes intersect exactly when their difference contains zero:

A ∩ B ≠ ∅ ⟺ 0 ∈ A − B .

If they share point p , choose a = b = p and obtain zero. Conversely, zero difference means some a = b , a shared point. This proof works for arbitrary sets; convexity is an additional condition used by algorithms such as GJK.

For intervals A = [ 2 , 4 ] and B = [ 3 , 5 ] , the difference is [ − 3 , 1 ] , which contains zero. For A = [ 0 , 1 ] and B = [ 3 , 4 ] , it is [ − 4 , − 2 ] , which does not. The abstract construction has become a visible one-dimensional collision trace.

Support queries inspect the boundary without constructing it

For a nonempty compact set C , its support function is

h C ( d ) = max x ∈ C d ⋅ x .

Compact means closed and bounded in this finite-dimensional setting; it ensures a continuous linear measurement attains its maximum. For more general sets one uses a supremum, which may be infinite or unattained.

A support point is any location attaining the maximum. The function returns a scalar; the mapping returns a point. In a square and direction ( 1 , 0 ) , every point on the right edge is a maximizer. A deterministic implementation must define its tie rule if repeatability matters.

For a sphere with center c and radius r ,

support C ( d ) = c + r d ‖ d ‖ , h C ( d ) = d ⋅ c + r ‖ d ‖

for nonzero d . For an axis-aligned box, independently choose each coordinate’s upper endpoint for a positive direction component and lower endpoint for a negative one. For a finite convex hull, scan vertex dot products; one maximizer suffices.

Because choices in two sets are independent,

h A ⊕ B ( d ) = h A ( d ) + h B ( d ) , support A − B ( d ) = support A ( d ) − support B ( − d ) .

This lets GJK explore the difference shape on demand. GJK stands for Gilbert–Johnson–Keerthi. Its full simplex update, termination, degeneracy, and contact policies are not supplied by this identity; they belong to later collision lessons. Zero direction also needs a separate support-query policy.

Immediate footing — field

A field is a number system in which addition, subtraction, and multiplication follow the familiar algebraic rules, and every nonzero number has a reciprocal. The real numbers form a field: 3 / 2 is defined, while division by zero is not. Vector spaces use field elements as scalars. In graphics, calculations are modeled over the real numbers, while code stores rounded finite-precision approximations. The identity a ( b + c ) = a b + a c holds exactly in the model, though floating-point evaluation can round.

Immediate footing — matrix

A matrix is a rectangular table that defines an input-output rule formed from weighted sums after input and output coordinates are chosen. With column vectors, its columns are the images of the input coordinate directions. For example, A = [ 2 1 1 3 ] maps [ 1 2 ] to [ 4 7 ] because A [ 1 2 ] = [ 2 + 2 1 + 6 ] . The matrix shape determines which inputs it accepts. Row-major and column-major describe memory layout, not a different mathematical map.

Immediate footing — determinant

The determinant of a square matrix is its signed volume scale. For two columns u = ( a , b ) and v = ( c , d ) in 2D, det [ a c b d ] = a d − b c is their signed parallelogram area. The identity matrix has determinant 1 ; swapping its columns gives − 1 , indicating reversed orientation. A zero determinant means the columns are dependent and the map is not invertible. Determinants explain orientation, area scaling, and invertibility, but determinant formulas are usually a numerically fragile way to compute an inverse.

Immediate footing — inner product

An inner product pairs two vectors to produce a scalar and defines lengths and angles. In ordinary real orthonormal coordinates, ⟨ u , v ⟩ = ∑ i u i v i . For u = ( 1 , 0 ) and v = ( 3 , 4 ) , the value is 3 , the component of v along the unit direction u . The induced length is ‖ u ‖ = ⟨ u , u ⟩ , and perpendicular vectors have inner product zero. In a slanted basis, include the metric or Gram matrix; the plain coordinate dot product may not represent geometric measurement.

Immediate footing — linear map

A linear map T preserves vector addition and scalar multiplication: T ( u + v ) = T ( u ) + T ( v ) and T ( c u ) = c T ( u ) . Scaling and rotation about the origin are linear; translation by a nonzero offset is not, because it moves the zero vector. A map is determined by its action on a basis. The robot needs a rule for moving every direction

The panel from Phase 6 is still on the workbench. We know how to describe a point, a displacement, a tangent and a normal. Now the robot needs to turn an entire tool, stretch a model, express measurements in a different frame, or reverse a calibration. Storing a new answer for every possible input would be impossible. We need a compact rule that determines all those answers.

A matrix starts as a rectangular arrangement of scalars. The word scalar means one value from the number system being used; here the mathematical model uses real numbers, while the implementation stores floating-point approximations. The notation A ∈ R m × n says that A has m rows and n columns. The entry a i j is in row i , column j . Mathematical subscripts usually start at one; our C++ accessors use zero-based A(row, column).

Consider

A = [ 1 2 3 4 5 6 ] .

This is a 2 × 3 matrix, not a 3 × 2 matrix. Its shape is rows first, columns second. It has six scalar positions, and its two rows each contain three values. A matrix does not need to be square. Square means only that the two dimensions agree.

The rectangular table does not announce its meaning. It could hold coefficients of equations, a linear map, a covariance, or image samples. A transform interpretation needs an additional contract: what the input coordinates mean, what the output coordinates mean, which bases are used, and how the matrix acts. This is the same lesson as the earlier bit story: a representation acquires meaning through an interpretation.

The convention contract for this volume

Unless a section explicitly changes the convention, vectors are columns and a map acts on the left:

y = A x .

The geometric coordinate frame is right-handed, positive rotations follow the right-hand rule, and rotation examples are active: the vector moves while the coordinate axes stay fixed. Angles used by code are in radians. Thus 90 ∘ = π / 2 radians. Each of these choices is independent of how the scalars are arranged in memory.

Under this convention, A ∈ R m × n represents a linear map from an n -component input to an m -component output. It needs one column for each input basis direction, and each column contains the m coordinates of that direction’s output. Shape is therefore a mathematical type contract. A 2 × 3 map accepts a three-component vector and produces a two-component vector; equal byte counts do not make incompatible shapes interchangeable.

We also need to distinguish a vector’s role from its written orientation. A row of coefficients can represent a measurement, or it can be the row-vector convention’s representation of a vector. Those roles should not be inferred from the fact that three numbers happen to be horizontal. Later, a frame change will reveal why the distinction matters. ## A linear rule is determined by its basis images

A map assigns an output to each permitted input. A map T is linear when it preserves addition and scalar multiplication:

T ( a u + b v ) = a T ( u ) + b T ( v ) .

This is a rule about the behavior of the map, not about how straight its graph looks. In particular, T ( 0 ) = 0 : substitute zero for the scalar in the scaling rule. Translation by a nonzero vector therefore cannot be a linear map on ordinary point coordinates. Moving every point by t moves the origin to t . We will add a larger representation when we need translation.

Every vector in a chosen basis has an expansion. In the standard two-dimensional basis,

x = x 1 e 1 + x 2 e 2 .

Apply linearity:

T ( x ) = x 1 T ( e 1 ) + x 2 T ( e 2 ) .

So knowing the two basis images determines the output of every vector. Suppose the calibration sends

T ( e 1 ) = [ 2 1 ] , T ( e 2 ) = [ − 1 3 ] .

Put those images into the columns:

A = [ 2 − 1 1 3 ] .

The columns tell us where the input basis directions go. For x = ( 4 , 2 ) T , retain the same input coefficients but combine the new directions:

A x = 4 [ 2 1 ] + 2 [ − 1 3 ] = [ 8 4 ] + [ − 2 6 ] = [ 6 10 ] .

This is matrix-vector multiplication. The arithmetic comes directly from preserving the vector’s basis expansion. In general, if c j is column j of A ,

A x = ∑ j = 1 n x j c j .

Row-major does not mean row-vector mathematics. Column-vector multiplication works with either storage layout when the accessor returns the correct logical element. Conversely, changing from column-vector mathematics to an equivalent row-vector representation requires transposing the represented map:

y = A c x ⟹ y T = x T A c T .

Thus A r = A c T represents the same map with row vectors relative to the same corresponding bases. That is an algebraic conversion, not a decision to store the old table in a different order.

The matrix itself has m n scalar entries. A particular C++ object may also have padding or chosen alignment. Combining rules explains the multiplication law

Addition and scalar multiplication are the simplest matrix operations. They act entry by entry:

( A + B ) i j = A i j + B i j , ( λ A ) i j = λ A i j .

Addition needs the same shape. Scalar multiplication preserves shape. The meaning is also direct: ( A + B ) x = A x + B x , and ( λ A ) x = λ ( A x ) . Adding two maps adds their outputs; it does not apply one after the other. Two rotations generally do not add to a rotation, and multiplying every entry of a rotation by two doubles output lengths.

All m × n real matrices form a vector space of dimension m n : one basis matrix has a one at each chosen position and zero everywhere else. This vector-space structure is useful, but special families inside it can have extra constraints. The family of rotation matrices is constrained by orthonormality and positive orientation; arbitrary sums do not preserve those constraints.

To apply one rule after another, we need composition. Let B : R n → R p and A : R p → R m . The output of B is a valid input to A , so the combined map is A ∘ B . We want a matrix C satisfying C x = A ( B x ) for every permitted input.

Expand one component:

y k = ∑ j = 1 n B k j x j , z i = ∑ k = 1 p A i k y k = ∑ j = 1 n ( ∑ k = 1 p A i k B k j ) x j .

Comparing coefficients gives the product rule:

C = A B , C i j = ∑ k = 1 p A i k B k j .

The inner dimension is the intermediate coordinate space. In the product ( m × p ) ( p × n ) it must match, and the result has shape m × n . Equivalently, each column of A B is A applied to the corresponding column of B . This is why row-column multiplication is the rule: it makes the stored table agree with composed linear maps. For

A = [ 1 2 3 4 ] , B = [ 5 6 7 8 ] ,

the product is

A B = [ 1 ( 5 ) + 2 ( 7 ) 1 ( 6 ) + 2 ( 8 ) 3 ( 5 ) + 4 ( 7 ) 3 ( 6 ) + 4 ( 8 ) ] = [ 19 22 43 50 ] .

The reverse product is B A = [ 23 34 31 46 ] . Both products are defined here, but they differ. With rectangular matrices, one order may not even be defined.

Order describes actions; parentheses describe grouping

Take p = ( 1 , 1 ) T , an X stretch S = diag ( 2 , 1 ) , and a quarter-turn R = [ 0 − 1 1 0 ] . Applying scale then rotation gives

p → S ( 2 , 1 ) T → R ( − 1 , 2 ) T .

The product is R S p . Reverse the actions and the result is S R p = ( − 2 , 1 ) T . Matrix multiplication is noncommutative: order is not generally interchangeable. Some pairs commute, including diagonal matrices with each other and uniform scaling with any compatible linear map. Noncommutative does not mean that every pair fails to commute.

Grouping is different. Exact real matrix multiplication is associative: ( A B ) C = A ( B C ) when shapes permit the products. Both sides apply C , then B , then A . Expanding either component produces the same finite double sum. In floating-point arithmetic, different grouping can change rounding and overflow, so algebraic equality is not a bitwise reproducibility guarantee.

Grouping also changes cost. If A is 1000 × 10 , B is 10 × 1000 , and C is 1000 × 1 , the classical algorithm uses about 11 million multiply-accumulate terms for ( A B ) C , versus 20 thousand for A ( B C ) . The first route also creates a million-entry intermediate; the second creates ten entries. These are arithmetic counts, not measured timings.

For a classical dense product of shapes ( M × K ) ( K × N ) , there are M N output entries and K terms per entry, giving Θ ( M K N ) work. Layout and loop ordering change data reuse; they do not change these mathematical shapes or classical asymptotic counts.

A mathematical object is more than its coordinates

A graphics calculation begins with an object in a space and a question about that object. A point, a direction, a color, a velocity, a pixel measurement, and a list of unknown parameters may all be stored as arrays of numbers. They do not thereby become the same kind of thing. The meaning of each array depends on the operations it supports, the units of its entries, and the coordinate frame in which those entries were measured.

A vector space supplies operations for adding vectors and multiplying them by scalars. If u and v are vectors and a and b are real numbers, then a u + b v is another vector. A basis is a collection of independent vectors that lets every vector be expressed uniquely as a weighted sum. In a chosen basis B = ( b 1 , … , b n ) , the coordinate tuple [ v ] B records those weights; it is a description of v , not the vector itself. Changing the basis changes the tuple while leaving the represented geometric object unchanged. A linear map T preserves addition and scalar multiplication:

T ( a u + b v ) = a T ( u ) + b T ( v ) .

Once input and output bases are chosen, the map has a matrix representation. Its j th column is the coordinate vector of T ( b j ) , the image of the j th input basis vector. This fact is the simplest way to understand what a matrix means: each column records where one basis direction goes. Matrix multiplication follows function composition. If S acts first and T second, the composite is T ∘ S , represented by the product [ T ] [ S ] when the coordinate conventions agree.

This interpretation prevents a common error in graphics: treating a matrix as a bag of numbers divorced from its input frame, output frame, and storage convention. A transform may map object coordinates to world coordinates, world coordinates to camera coordinates, or one basis representation to another. Those maps have different semantic directions even if they happen to use the same dimensions. Multiplying in the wrong order is not a minor coding mistake; it composes different functions.

Coordinates become measurements only after geometry is specified. A dot product gives a way to measure length and angle. In an orthonormal Euclidean basis, u ⋅ v = u T v and ‖ u ‖ 2 = u T u . In a non-orthonormal basis, the coordinate dot product is generally not the geometric dot product. If the basis matrix is B , then the Gram matrix G = B T B stores the basis inner products, and

⟨ u , v ⟩ = [ u ] B T G [ v ] B .

The metric is therefore part of the problem. It says what counts as a unit of distance and which directions are orthogonal. This becomes essential when parameter vectors combine quantities with different units, when a fit weights some residuals more strongly, or when PCA is applied to differently scaled measurements.

For an approachable numerical example, let a 2D linear map send e 1 = ( 1 , 0 ) to ( 2 , 1 ) and e 2 = ( 0 , 1 ) to ( − 1 , 1 ) . Its matrix is

A = [ 2 − 1 1 1 ] .

A point with coordinates ( 3 , 2 ) maps to A ( 3 , 2 ) T = ( 4 , 5 ) T . This result can be reconstructed from the columns: 3 ( 2 , 1 ) + 2 ( − 1 , 1 ) = ( 4 , 5 ) . If the input coordinates had instead been expressed in a skew basis, the same geometric point would have different coordinates, and the matrix entries would have to describe the map relative to that basis. The picture is unchanged; its numerical description is not.

An eigenpair is a nonzero vector v and scalar λ satisfying A v = λ v . The map preserves the direction of v and scales it by λ ; a negative value also reverses orientation. For A = diag ( 2 , 3 ) , the coordinate directions e 1 = ( 1 , 0 ) and e 2 = ( 0 , 1 ) have eigenvalues 2 and 3 . Not every matrix has a full basis of real eigenvectors. Real symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and can be diagonalized with orthonormal eigenvectors. Eigenvectors of a general matrix may be sensitive to perturbations, especially when eigenvalues are close. A Gram matrix remembers the geometry of a slanted basis

In the slanted basis from earlier, dotting coordinate lists directly gives the wrong answer. Let basis columns be b 1 , … , b k . The Gram matrix stores all pairwise inner products:

G i j = b i ⋅ b j .

The diagonal holds squared lengths; off-diagonal entries hold alignment. With b 1 = ( 1 , 0 ) and b 2 = ( 1 , 1 ) ,

G = ( 1 1 1 2 ) .

If v = ∑ i c i b i and w = ∑ j d j b j , distribute the dot product:

v ⋅ w = ∑ i , j c i G i j d j = c T G d .

The superscript T means transpose: exchange rows and columns. It lets a column coordinate list act as the row that weights the next expression.

For c = ( 1 , 2 ) T , compute G c = ( 3 , 5 ) T , then c T G c = 3 + 10 = 13 . The reconstructed vector is ( 3 , 2 ) , whose Euclidean squared length is also 13 . Blindly squaring the coordinates would give 5 . G is symmetric. It is positive semidefinite because

c T G c = ‖ ∑ i c i b i ‖ 2 ≥ 0.

For independent columns it is positive definite: this quantity is positive for every nonzero coefficient vector. For dependent columns a nonzero coefficient list can reconstruct zero, so G is singular. An orthonormal basis has G = I , the identity matrix, explaining why ordinary coordinate dotting is then correct.

The Gram determinant measures squared area for two vectors and squared volume for three. But it does not retain orientation; reflecting every basis vector can preserve every dot product. A Gram matrix remembers metric geometry, not the complete physical frame.

To project onto a multi-vector span, require the residual to be perpendicular to every generator. In matrix notation this produces

G c = B T v .

This explains the system, but forming G = B T B can square the two-norm condition number of a full-column-rank B :

κ 2 ( B T B ) = κ 2 ( B ) 2 .

QR-based solves are usually a better route when numerical conditioning matters. We will build the orthogonalization intuition shortly rather than hide this behind a matrix recipe.

Special matrices tell us what information a rule preserves

The identity I n has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. It sends every basis direction to itself, so I n x = x . For an m × n matrix, use I m A = A and A I n = A . A transform product must begin with identity, while an accumulation of additions begins with the zero matrix. Starting a product at zero destroys every later contribution.

The zero matrix maps every input to zero. It is the additive identity, but it destroys all information as a map. From A B = A C one cannot generally cancel A ; if A is zero the equality tells us nothing about B and C .

A diagonal matrix permits nonzero entries only on the main diagonal. In D = diag ( d 1 , d 2 , d 3 ) , each output coordinate is d i x i ; coordinates do not mix. An ordinary three-axis scaling matrix has exactly this form. Uniform nonzero scaling preserves angles, while nonuniform scaling can change angles between arbitrary directions. A negative scale reverses a basis direction; a zero scale erases the corresponding coordinate and cannot be undone.

A shear changes one coordinate in proportion to another. The elementary planar example

H = [ 1 k 0 1 ] ⟹ ( x , y ) ↦ ( x + k y , y )

slides higher layers farther sideways, like a stack of cards. It sends e 1 to ( 1 , 0 ) and e 2 to ( k , 1 ) , preserving area but generally changing angles. Off-diagonal entries mean coordinate coupling, but they are not by themselves proof of shear: rotation also mixes coordinates. Nor does a general matrix with all diagonal entries equal to one automatically have determinant one. For example [ 1 2 3 1 ] has determinant − 5 ; it is not the area-preserving elementary shear above.

Derive rotations from the directions that stay fixed

A proper rotation preserves Euclidean lengths, angles and orientation. In two dimensions, rotate the first unit axis through angle θ . Its image is ( cos ⁡ θ , sin ⁡ θ ) T . The second unit axis began at angle π / 2 , so its image is

[ cos ⁡ ( π / 2 + θ ) sin ⁡ ( π / 2 + θ ) ] = [ − sin ⁡ θ cos ⁡ θ ] .

Place the two images into columns. Writing c = cos ⁡ θ and s = sin ⁡ θ gives

R ( θ ) = [ c − s s c ] , x ′ = c x − s y , y ′ = s x + c y .

At θ = π / 2 , e x goes to e y , and e y goes to − e x . These basis tests expose a wrong sign immediately. A general vector ( 2 , 3 ) T goes to ( − 3 , 2 ) T .

Why is this truly length preserving? Expand the squared output length:

( c x − s y ) 2 + ( s x + c y ) 2 = x 2 ( c 2 + s 2 ) + y 2 ( c 2 + s 2 ) = x 2 + y 2 .

The cross terms cancel and c 2 + s 2 = 1 . The columns are also unit length and mutually perpendicular, summarized by R T R = I . Therefore R − 1 = R T = R ( − θ ) . Multiplying two such matrices and applying the angle-addition identities gives R ( α ) R ( β ) = R ( α + β ) , so planar rotations commute.

The oriented plane explains the Y-rotation signs

In three dimensions, rotation about an axis leaves that axis fixed. Choose the ordered perpendicular-plane coordinates so their basis cross product points along the positive rotation axis. For X use ( y , z ) because e y × e z = e x . For Y use ( z , x ) because e z × e x = e y . For Z use ( x , y ) because e x × e y = e z . Insert the same planar rotation into each ordered plane. For X,

y ′ = c y − s z , z ′ = s y + c z , x ′ = x , R x = [ 1 0 0 0 c − s 0 s c ] .

For Y, first rotate the ordered pair ( z , x ) :

z ′ = c z − s x , x ′ = s z + c x , y ′ = y .

Then return to the usual output order ( x ′ , y ′ , z ′ ) :

R y = [ c 0 s 0 1 0 − s 0 c ] .

The differing-looking signs are consequences of choosing an oriented plane, not exceptions to the two-dimensional rule. Finally,

R z = [ c − s 0 s c 0 0 0 1 ] .

Use the actual vector v = ( 2 , 3 , 4 ) T at a positive quarter-turn:

R x v = ( 2 , − 4 , 3 ) T , R y v = ( 4 , 3 , − 2 ) T , R z v = ( − 3 , 2 , 4 ) T .

For the Y case, explicitly read the rows: x ′ = 0 ( 2 ) + 0 ( 3 ) + 1 ( 4 ) = 4 , y ′ = 3 , and z ′ = − 1 ( 2 ) + 0 ( 3 ) + 0 ( 4 ) = − 2 . Its squared length is 16 + 9 + 4 = 29 , matching the input’s 4 + 9 + 16 = 29 . The right-hand rule agrees: positive Y sends positive Z toward positive X and positive X toward negative Z.

Arbitrary rotations in three dimensions do not share one axis. For example, with e z and quarter-turns, R y R x e z = − e y while R x R y e z = e x . This is why a rotation API must state the axes, the coordinate space and the composition order.

A diagonal matrix can represent some nonidentity rotations: in two dimensions diag ( − 1 , − 1 ) is a half-turn, and in three dimensions diag ( − 1 , − 1 , 1 ) is a half-turn about Z. What a diagonal matrix cannot do is continuous general coordinate mixing. Do not turn a useful pattern-recognition shortcut into an absolute claim.

Repeated floating-point rotation products can drift away from orthonormality. Check R T R against identity with a meaningful residual. Finite entries alone do not prove that a matrix remains a rotation. Later quaternion normalization gives a compact orientation-maintenance method, with its own preconditions.

Transpose lets us compare measurements and structure

The transpose exchanges logical indices: ( A T ) i j = A j i . A 2 × 3 table becomes 3 × 2 . This operation is not a physical rotation of the represented geometry, nor is it merely a memory-layout conversion. For real columns x , y in an orthonormal Euclidean basis, x T y is their dot product.

Transpose twice returns the original matrix; it distributes over addition and scalar multiplication. Products reverse order:

( A B ) T = B T A T .

Indeed, the ( i , j ) entry on the left is ∑ k A j k B k i , which is the ( i , j ) entry of the right side after commuting each pair of real scalars. The dimensions also force the reversed order. This identity will support the inverse-transpose normal rule later.

A real square matrix is symmetric when A T = A . Its opposite-side entries match; only n ( n + 1 ) / 2 values are independent. A covariance matrix is symmetric because exchanging the two variables leaves the covariance unchanged. The product A T A is symmetric by the transpose identity, though using it to solve systems can worsen conditioning and is not a universal numerical recommendation.

A matrix is skew-symmetric when K T = − K . Over the reals its diagonal is zero because k i i = − k i i . In three dimensions,

[ ω ] × = [ 0 − ω z ω y ω z 0 − ω x − ω y ω x 0 ] ⟹ [ ω ] × v = ω × v .

For ω = ( 1 , 2 , 3 ) T and v = ( 4 , 5 , 6 ) T , both calculations give ( − 3 , 6 , − 3 ) T . This connects a cross product to a linear operator. A small rotation has the form I + θ [ a ^ ] × + O ( θ 2 ) ; dropping the higher terms gives a first-order approximation, not an exactly orthogonal finite rotation.

Every real square matrix separates uniquely into symmetric and skew parts:

A = A + A T 2 ⏟ S + A − A T 2 ⏟ K .

For A = [ 1 4 2 3 ] , these are S = [ 1 3 3 3 ] and K = [ 0 1 − 1 0 ] . This is an additive decomposition of coefficients. It is not a rotation-times-stretch factorization.

The trace of a square matrix is tr A = ∑ i a i i . For compatible rectangular A and B with square products, expanding the sums gives tr ( A B ) = tr ( B A ) . This permits cyclic reorderings of longer products when dimensions permit, not arbitrary permutations. If P is an invertible change-of-basis matrix,

tr ( P − 1 A P ) = tr ( A P P − 1 ) = tr ( A ) .

Trace is therefore independent of the chosen basis for the same endomorphism, meaning a linear map from a vector space to itself. It summarizes one invariant; it does not identify the complete transformation. Eigenvalue interpretations will be developed when those terms have their own foundations.

Determinant measures oriented volume and exposes lost dimensions

In two dimensions, the columns of A = [ a b c d ] are the images of the unit square’s edge directions. Their oriented parallelogram area is

det A = a d − b c .

For A = [ 3 1 2 4 ] , the determinant is 12 − 2 = 10 : ordinary areas scale by ten and orientation is preserved. A reflection diag ( − 1 , 1 ) has determinant − 1 : area magnitude is preserved but orientation reverses. The singular table [ 1 2 2 4 ] has dependent columns and zero area.

In three dimensions, let the columns be u , v , w in that order. Their oriented parallelepiped volume is

det A = u ⋅ ( v × w ) .

This is the scalar triple product from Phase 6: base area times signed height. For a general table,

det [ a b c d e f g h i ] = a ( e i − f h ) − b ( d i − f g ) + c ( d h − e g ) .

Use one calibration throughout the following inversion examples:

A = [ 1 2 3 0 1 4 5 6 0 ] .

Expansion gives − 24 + 40 − 15 = 1 . Independently, its columns are ( 1 , 0 , 5 ) T , ( 2 , 1 , 6 ) T and ( 3 , 4 , 0 ) T . The cross product of the last two is ( − 24 , 18 , 5 ) T , whose dot product with the first is − 24 + 25 = 1 .

This map preserves volume and orientation, but not necessarily lengths or angles. A determinant of one does not prove rotation. Proper rotations additionally satisfy R T R = I . Elementary shears also have determinant one while distorting angles.

In n dimensions, | det A | is the ordinary n -volume scale. The sign records orientation. Composition multiplies the scales: det ( A B ) = det A det B . Therefore det ( A − 1 ) = 1 / det A when an inverse exists. A scale diag ( 2 , 3 , 4 ) multiplies volume by 24; its inverse multiplies it by 1 / 24 .

Recovering an input has an algebraic route and a computational route

An inverse A − 1 must satisfy both A − 1 A = I and A A − 1 = I for a square map. It undoes the same specified input/output relationship; it does not reverse lost information. Use known structure when it is guaranteed rather than invoking an unnecessary general inverse.

Minors and cofactors build a symbolic inverse

The minor M i j is the determinant remaining after deleting row i and column j . The cofactor is C i j = ( − 1 ) i + j M i j , adding alternating signs. The adjugate is the cofactor matrix transposed, adj A = C T . Do not confuse adjugate with a conjugate-transpose adjoint used elsewhere.

For our determinant-one calibration, the cofactor matrix is

C = [ − 24 20 − 5 18 − 15 4 5 − 4 1 ] .

For instance, deleting row one and column two leaves [ 0 4 5 0 ] , with minor − 20 and cofactor + 20 . The resulting inverse is

A − 1 = adj A det A = [ − 24 18 5 20 − 15 − 4 − 5 4 1 ] .

Why does the formula work? A diagonal entry of A adj A is a row’s cofactor expansion, hence det A . An off-diagonal entry has the same expansion with a different row inserted, producing a determinant with repeated rows, hence zero. Therefore A adj A = ( det A ) I . Division is valid only when det A ≠ 0 .

Verify rather than trust the table. The first row of A A − 1 is ( − 24 + 40 − 15 ,   18 − 30 + 12 ,   5 − 8 + 3 ) = ( 1 , 0 , 0 ) . The second row is ( 20 − 20 , − 15 + 16 , − 4 + 4 ) = ( 0 , 1 , 0 ) ; the third is ( − 120 + 120 , 90 − 90 , 25 − 24 ) = ( 0 , 0 , 1 ) . The reverse product also equals identity. A left-side check alone is insufficient as a general software interface test, even though exact square algebra makes the two-sided relationship follow. For a 2 × 2 matrix this construction reduces to

[ a b c d ] − 1 = 1 a d − b c [ d − b − c a ] .

Closed formulas are useful for symbolic reasoning and some carefully implemented small matrices. Recursive cofactor expansion is not an appropriate general dense-matrix inversion algorithm. A compact formula does not automatically minimize numerical error.

An inverse can exist and still amplify uncertainty

An ill-conditioned problem is one whose exact answer changes greatly when its input changes slightly, measured relative to the stated input/output scales. Algorithmic stability describes how much additional error an algorithm introduces. A stable algorithm cannot erase uncertainty already inherent in an ill-conditioned problem. Take

A ε = [ 1 1 1 1 + ε ] , A ε − 1 = 1 ε [ 1 + ε − 1 − 1 1 ] .

The columns approach parallel directions as ε → 0 . For ε = 0.001 , the input ( 1 , 1 ) T produces ( 2 , 2.001 ) T . Change only the second observed output to 2.002 . The exact recovered input becomes ( 0 , 2 ) T : the equations give x 1 + x 2 = 2 and 0.001 x 2 = 0.002 . The output perturbation is small, but the reconstructed input changes substantially. No implementation bug is needed to produce this effect.

A compatible induced matrix norm measures the largest amplification relative to a chosen vector norm. A corresponding normwise condition number for a nonsingular square matrix is

When both matrix and right-hand side change, additional terms and smallness conditions enter the bound. A condition number is tied to a problem and scaling; it is not an unqualified quality score for every possible downstream use.

In the 2-norm, κ 2 ( A ) = σ max / σ min , where the singular values describe the largest and smallest directional stretches. This is a preview of later SVD, not a requirement to learn its full construction now. Equal stretches have condition one; a huge ratio means one direction was compressed relative to another.

This immediately disproves a determinant-only numerical threshold. Compare two matrices with the same determinant:

An inverse API should therefore return a failure state or diagnostic result when it cannot meet its contract. Choose tolerances from the represented units, precision and use case; a global determinant epsilon hides those decisions.

A normal is a measuring rule before it is an arrow

The panel is scaled nonuniformly. Its edges transform as ordinary movements. Its normal must remain perpendicular to those transformed edges. Why does the same transform not always work?

A linear functional is a rule ω that turns a vector into a scalar and obeys

ω ( a u + b v ) = a ω ( u ) + b ω ( v ) .

“Functional” simply means a scalar-valued function in this setting. A covector is such a linear measurement rule. The dual space V ∗ is the vector space of these rules on V .

For ω ( x , y ) = 2 x + y , movement ( 3 , 2 ) gives measurement 8 . Think of a price list applied to a quantity basket: the price list is not another basket even though both can be stored as two numbers. A covector’s units can be reciprocal to the vector’s units.

Its kernel is the set of vectors it measures as zero. Saying the rule annihilates a tangent only means it returns zero for that tangent. This vocabulary is useful only after the ordinary meaning is clear.

A plane normal defines such a rule: ω n ( v ) = n ⋅ v . Tangent vectors lie in its kernel. In orthonormal Euclidean coordinates we identify the measurement with a vector using the inner product, hiding the distinction. In general basis coordinates, the conversion uses the metric: a coordinate vector n represents covector coefficients G n .

Derive the inverse transpose

Let an invertible active linear map send tangents to v ′ = A v . To preserve the measurement relationship, require

n ′ T A v = n T v for every  v .

Therefore n ′ T A = n T , and

n ′ = A − T n .

The inverse compensates for transformed motion; the transpose converts row measurement coefficients back into the column storage convention. This is a derived requirement, not a special shader incantation.

Oriented areas deserve their own representation

The cross product encodes area as a vector only in a special setting. Exterior algebra keeps the area itself. Its wedge product a ∧ b is bilinear and antisymmetric:

a ∧ b = − b ∧ a , a ∧ a = 0.

In three-dimensional coordinates,

a ∧ b = ( a x b y − a y b x ) e x ∧ e y + ( a x b z − a z b x ) e x ∧ e z + ( a y b z − a z b y ) e y ∧ e z .

A bivector represents oriented area; it is not merely two independent vectors stored together. A grade counts the dimensional character: scalars have grade zero, vectors grade one, area elements grade two, and volume elements grade three. A decomposable wedge of two vectors is a simple bivector. In higher dimensions, a sum of simple bivectors need not describe one single plane.

Expand the wedge in a basis to obtain those signed area coefficients. If the inputs are dependent, all two-dimensional minors vanish and the wedge is zero. In an appropriate Euclidean metric, its squared magnitude is the two-vector Gram determinant. A metric and orientation supply the Hodge dual identification that turns a three-dimensional bivector into the familiar cross-product vector. This identification is extra structure, not part of the wedge’s definition.

Geometric algebra unifies measurement and area

In Euclidean geometric algebra, the product of two vectors combines their inner and exterior products:

a b = a ⋅ b + a ∧ b .

The symmetric and antisymmetric pieces recover the two operations:

a ⋅ b = a b + b a 2 , a ∧ b = a b − b a 2 .

In two orthonormal axes, the plane element I = e 1 e 2 has

I 2 = e 1 e 2 e 1 e 2 = − 1.

This reconnects planar rotation to complex-number behavior. A rotor encodes rotation as a sandwich action, for example v ′ = R v R ~ , where the reverse R ~ reverses product order. In one oriented plane we can use R = cos ⁡ ( θ / 2 ) − I sin ⁡ ( θ / 2 ) with the corresponding convention. The half-angle appears because the rotor acts on both sides.

With unit plane normal n , reflection of a vector in the plane can be written − n v n . Expanding the geometric product recovers v − 2 ( v ⋅ n ) n , the earlier formula. Composition of reflections naturally produces rotations.

This chapter establishes motivation and basic identities, not a complete geometric-algebra implementation or performance claim. Matrices and quaternions remain useful representations with established engine interfaces. The gain here is understanding why vectors, areas, measurements, and orientations should not all be collapsed into an unqualified Vec3. ### Immediate footing — homogeneous coordinates

Homogeneous coordinates append a scale component to ordinary coordinates. An affine point can be written ( x , y , z , 1 ) and a direction ( d x , d y , d z , 0 ) . Multiplying a homogeneous tuple by a nonzero common scale leaves the represented projective point unchanged. A tuple with nonzero last component w returns to an affine chart by dividing the first components by w . A zero last component represents an ideal direction, so that division is invalid. This representation lets translation and perspective participate in matrix operations, while their geometric meanings remain distinct. Full explanation and worked examples in this book

Homogeneous coordinates preserve the role of a location

Translation affects a point but not a free direction. Homogeneous coordinates make that distinction visible in an extra component:

P h = ( x , y , z , 1 ) , d h = ( d x , d y , d z , 0 ) .

For an affine transform with linear part A and translation t , the rule on a homogeneous pair is

( x , w ) ⟼ ( A x + w t , w ) .

When w = 1 , translation is added; when w = 0 , it is not. Subtract two canonical point representatives and the result has w = 0 . Add a direction to a point and the result retains w = 1 . This is the affine algebra from earlier, not an arbitrary convention.

A projective point has many representatives

A nonzero tuple H = ( X , Y , Z , W ) represents the same projective point as λ H for any nonzero scalar λ . We write H ∼ λ H . If W ≠ 0 , divide to select a finite representative:

( x , y , z ) = ( X W , Y W , Z W ) .

For example, ( 2 , 4 , 6 , 2 ) and ( 1 , 2 , 3 , 1 ) represent the same finite point. The all-zero homogeneous tuple is invalid: it describes no projective line through the origin.

Nonzero tuples with W = 0 describe projective points at infinity. They encode directions of parallel lines. This is the formal origin of vanishing-point intuition. However, a projective point identifies H with − H , so it forgets an oriented direction’s sign and magnitude. A physical direction represented as ( d , 0 ) retains vector information in the affine transform pipeline; treating it as a projective equivalence class is a different semantic step.

Adding two canonical point representatives gives ( P + Q , 2 ) ; dividing by the last component gives their midpoint. But projective representatives may be independently rescaled, so adding arbitrary representatives is not a well-defined projective point operation. Define affine weights before combining them.

NDC is not unit-vector normalization

After a camera projection, a point may have clip coordinates ( X c , Y c , Z c , W c ) . Normalized device coordinates, or NDC, typically follow from perspective division:

x n = X c / W c , y n = Y c / W c , z n = Z c / W c .

“Normalized” here means mapped to a canonical coordinate range, not length one. Depth conventions and clipping ranges depend on the chosen API and projection. State them rather than infer them from the letters NDC.

The division is not linear. If F ( X , W ) = X / W , then F ( 1 , 1 ) = 1 , yet F ( ( 1 , 1 ) + ( 1 , 1 ) ) = F ( 2 , 2 ) = 1 , not 2 . A homogeneous matrix stage can be linear while the complete dehomogenized mapping is not.

Clip primitives in the proper homogeneous domain before division. In particular, negative common scaling is valid projective equivalence but does not preserve an oriented clip representative’s inequalities. Do not rescale clip coordinates arbitrarily and assume rasterization is unchanged.

For a viewport of width W and height H with downward-positive pixel coordinates, one common mapping is

x pixel = x n + 1 2 W , y pixel = 1 − y n 2 H .

For x n = − 0.5 and W = 1920 , the horizontal coordinate is 480 . This maps continuous boundaries; the last pixel index is W − 1 , and sample-center conventions require their own care. Apply this to NDC, not raw clip coordinates.

The origin exposes the limit of an ordinary linear map

Our robot can now rotate, stretch and shear a displacement. But it still cannot place the panel somewhere else in the workcell using a three-by-three linear map alone. The reason is already inside the definition of linearity. A linear map L obeys L ( a u + b v ) = a L ( u ) + b L ( v ) . Set both coefficients to zero and we obtain L ( 0 ) = 0 . The origin is fixed. A translation by a nonzero vector t sends zero to t , so translation is not linear in ordinary three-dimensional coordinates.

This is a useful boundary, rather than a defect in matrices. We were asking a displacement-only operation to answer a location question. A point names a location; a free vector names a displacement independently of where its arrow is drawn. Changing the panel’s position should move its points, while a displacement between two of its points should receive no extra translation. If P ′ = A P + t and Q ′ = A Q + t , then

Q ′ − P ′ = A ( Q − P ) .

The translations cancel because a displacement measures the difference between locations. That cancellation is the bridge from the vector-space material in Phase 6 to the affine transformations needed for scenes. ### Homogeneous coordinates give points and directions different roles

An affine map has the form

P ′ = A P + t ,

where A is its linear part and t is its translation. A may rotate, scale, reflect, shear or collapse directions. An affine map need not be invertible; we will require invertibility when it is used as a full coordinate frame or when an inverse is requested.

Append a fourth coordinate and write an ordinary finite point as P ~ = ( P x , P y , P z , 1 ) T . Write a displacement as v ~ = ( v x , v y , v z , 0 ) T . The affine matrix is

F ( A , t ) = [ A t 0 T 1 ] .

Multiplication makes the roles explicit:

F [ P 1 ] = [ A P + t 1 ] , F [ v 0 ] = [ A v 0 ] .

The final coordinate is not a flag that authorizes arbitrary operations. In projective geometry a finite point also has representatives ( w P , w ) for any nonzero w , and recovering ordinary coordinates requires division by w . For the present affine matrices, the conventional representatives with w = 1 for points and w = 0 for directions make translation behavior especially easy to inspect. A general four-by-four matrix may change w and need not be an affine frame at all.

Composition follows the dependency of locations

Suppose one transform first applies P 1 = B P + u , and a second applies P 2 = A P 1 + t . Substitute before memorizing any product formula:

P 2 = A ( B P + u ) + t = A B P + ( A u + t ) .

Consequently,

F ( A , t ) F ( B , u ) = F ( A B , A u + t ) .

The earlier translation u is also transformed by the later linear map. Merely adding translation columns is incorrect when the later transform rotates or scales their frame. With our column-vector convention, M 2 M 1 P means apply M 1 first. A notation change to row vectors would change how the same composition is written; a storage-layout change would not.

If an object should be scaled in local axes, then rotated, then placed in world coordinates, its matrix is M = T R S . The rightmost operation acts first. If it should rotate about a pivot c expressed in the same coordinate frame as the points, use T ( c ) R T ( − c ) : move the pivot to zero, rotate, move back. For a door hinge at c = ( 1 , 0 , 0 ) , a point P = ( 2 , 0 , 0 ) rotated 90 ∘ about Z follows ( 2 , 0 , 0 ) → ( 1 , 0 , 0 ) → ( 0 , 1 , 0 ) → ( 1 , 1 , 0 ) . That trace explains the order more clearly than naming a “pivot matrix.”

A transform hierarchy stores relationships such as panel relative to robot base, then base relative to world. If L maps child-local to parent-local and W p maps parent-local to world, the child’s world matrix is W c = W p L . This is ordinary composition with meaningful endpoints.

If world matrices are cached, a dirty flag means “this cached result no longer agrees with the data it depends on.” Changing a parent’s local transform invalidates its own world matrix and the world matrices of dependent descendants. A correct update evaluates the parent before the child or ensures the parent is current on demand. The invariant is explicit: whenever a world matrix is consumed, it equals the current parent world matrix times the current local matrix. The word “dirty” is bookkeeping for that invariant, not a mathematical property of a matrix.

Do not assume arbitrary hierarchy products remain expressible as translation, rotation and diagonal scale without shear. A rotated child under a nonuniformly scaled parent can produce a linear block whose transformed basis columns are not perpendicular. A four-by-four affine matrix preserves that full result; a restricted TRS representation may require decomposition, approximation, or rejection. Later quaternion operations do not remove this closure problem.

Undo the linear action before undoing its translated origin

From Q = A P + t , subtract the translation and solve:

P = A − 1 ( Q − t ) = A − 1 Q − A − 1 t .

Therefore, if A is invertible,

F ( A , t ) − 1 = F ( A − 1 , − A − 1 t ) .

The inverse translation is generally not just − t ; it is expressed after the inverse linear action. For a rigid frame whose linear block R is an orthonormal rotation, R − 1 = R T , and the inverse is F ( R T , − R T t ) . That shortcut does not apply to a basis containing nonunit scale or shear. Check the actual promise, not the variable name rotation.
