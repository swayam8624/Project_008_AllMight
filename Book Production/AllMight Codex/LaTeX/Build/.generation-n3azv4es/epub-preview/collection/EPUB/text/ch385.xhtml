Acceleration structures and robust queries

Acceleration stores reusable spatial bounds so queries can avoid most scene geometry while preserving correctness.

Geometric queries and acceleration structures

A ray is r ( t ) = o + t d , with origin o , direction d , and parameter t ≥ 0 . To intersect triangle a , b , c , solve

o + t d = a + u ( b − a ) + v ( c − a ) .

The unknowns are t , u , v . A forward hit must satisfy t ≥ 0 , u ≥ 0 , v ≥ 0 , and u + v ≤ 1 . The barycentric weights are ( 1 − u − v , u , v ) . A degenerate triangle makes the system singular. A ray exactly on a shared edge can be inconsistently classified by floating-point arithmetic, so robust tests need a consistent edge rule or conservative predicates. CMU-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Closest-point queries project a point onto a triangle plane, then clamp to the appropriate edge or vertex when the projection is outside the face. Signed distance adds a sign indicating inside versus outside, often determined by orientation or a winding test. These queries occur in collision detection, surface reconstruction, and modeling. The mathematical answer should be independent of the data structure used to accelerate it. CMU-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Naively testing a ray against every triangle costs O ( n ) operations for n faces. A bounding-volume hierarchy groups primitives inside boxes. A ray-box slab test computes an interval of possible ray parameters for each coordinate axis; intersect the three intervals, rejecting when their common interval is empty. If a ray is parallel to a slab and its origin is outside, reject; if inside, that axis imposes no extra restriction. The bound must be conservative: a false positive costs time, but a false negative loses a real hit. CMU-13 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

BVH traversal descends only into boxes hit by the ray. Visiting the nearer child first can find a closest hit early; any later node whose nearest possible hit is farther can be pruned. A shadow query can stop at its first blocker, while a transparent visibility query may need several hits. Uniform grids, k-d trees, and BVHs suit different geometry distributions and update patterns. A BVH can be refit after small motion or rebuilt for tighter bounds. Acceleration changes the cost, not the result, of a query. CMU-13 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Bounding-volume hierarchy invariants

A BVH is a tree. Every leaf contains one or more primitives; every interior node has children whose primitive sets partition its own. The children’s boxes may overlap spatially, but a primitive belongs to only one child set. A node bound encloses every descendant primitive. This containment property ensures that a ray missing a node cannot hit any geometry below it. The split heuristic affects speed, not correctness. A surface-area heuristic (Light Transport and Image Synthesis, Part 1, Reference notes: Bounding volume hierarchies) estimates expected traversal cost by balancing the cost of testing a node against the probability that a ray visits a child.

For an axis-aligned box with coordinate bounds [ b i m i n , b i m a x ] , a ray coordinate is o i + t d i . If d i > 0 , the slab-entry and exit parameters are ( b i m i n − o i ) / d i and ( b i m a x − o i ) / d i . If d i < 0 , their ordering reverses. Taking the maximum of entry parameters and minimum of exit parameters across axes gives the intersection interval. The ray hits if the interval is nonempty and overlaps allowed t values. Parallel components require explicit treatment to avoid division by zero. Floating-point implementations often use reciprocal directions and precomputed sign bits, but must preserve conservative behavior near boundaries. CMU-13 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Dynamic geometry can update a BVH by refitting node boxes from child bounds. Refit preserves correctness but can leave poor partitions after large deformation. Rebuilding costs more immediately but may produce tighter bounds and faster future traversal. If a scene receives many rays between updates, rebuilding can pay off; for rapidly changing scenes with few rays, a quick refit may be preferable. This is a workload decision. CMU-13 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Data structures shape the algorithm

A uniform grid maps each primitive to intersected cells. Ray traversal steps cell by cell, but a large primitive may occupy many cells and produce duplicate tests. A k-d tree partitions space with planes; splitting a primitive can require storing it in multiple leaves. A BVH partitions primitives into disjoint sets while allowing spatially overlapping bounds. This is especially useful when objects are clustered or empty space is irregular. Different acceleration structures are not interchangeable abstractions in performance terms, though each should produce the same geometric answer.

The choice of query and structure should be tested against expected rays. Primary camera rays tend to be coherent; neighboring rays point in similar directions. Shadow rays point toward light sources. Diffuse bounce rays are more scattered. A structure optimized for one ray distribution may perform differently for another. This is a general systems lesson: data structures encode assumptions about future queries. CMU-13 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Robustness and conditioning are related but different

Conditioning describes how sensitive the exact mathematical answer is to input changes. Algorithmic stability describes whether a numerical method adds additional error beyond that sensitivity. Nearly parallel line intersection is ill-conditioned: a tiny angular change can move the intersection far away. Higher precision cannot make the geometric problem well-conditioned, but a stable method can avoid making it worse. For a well-conditioned problem, a numerically unstable method can still return a poor answer. Robust geometry needs both an understanding of conditioning and careful arithmetic.

Orientation predicates, such as determining whether one point lies to the left or right of a directed edge, often reduce to the sign of a determinant. Near zero, roundoff can flip the sign, causing inconsistent topology decisions. Adaptive precision evaluates cheaply in ordinary cases and uses more precision only when the result is uncertain. Such predicates matter in triangulation, ray-edge tests, and mesh repair. The point is not to eliminate all floating-point error; it is to prevent uncertain local calculations from violating global invariants. Acceleration: spend scene work once so each ray can skip most geometry

A ray tracer’s brute-force inner loop compares each ray with every primitive. With P image samples, N triangles, and an average of R rays generated per sample (including shadow and secondary rays), a crude work model is O ( P R N ) . If an object contains 1,300 triangles, a bounding-volume miss can save all 1,300 triangle tests for that ray. The bounding volume must be conservative: it may contain extra empty space, but it cannot exclude any part of the object. A loose bound wastes work; a costly, tight bound can cost more than it saves. Construction is amortized across queries: if setup costs B and it saves s work per ray, it becomes worthwhile after about B / s rays. This changes for a deforming animated object, where bounds may need refitting or rebuilding each frame; a wider bound can instead cover several poses at the cost of weaker culling. MITG-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:07:00–00:20:00)

For an axis-aligned bounding box, store minimum corner b min and maximum corner b max . Along each axis, the box defines a slab interval. For one coordinate x ( t ) = o x + t d x , intersect the two planes x = b min , x and x = b max , x to get two parameters; sort them as t x , min and t x , max . Repeat for y and z . The ray enters the box at the largest of the three entry parameters and exits at the smallest of the three exit parameters:

t enter = max ( t x , min , t y , min , t z , min ) , t exit = min ( t x , max , t y , max , t z , max ) .

The intervals overlap if t enter ≤ t exit . For a forward ray, also require t exit ≥ 0 ; when recursively testing a subregion, intersect with its inherited interval [ t start , t end ] . If one direction component is zero, the ray is parallel to that pair of slab planes: it misses if its origin lies outside the slab, otherwise that axis places no bound on t . This is the slab method and it reduces an AABB query to a few min/max operations. A triangle bound is formed by coordinatewise minima/maxima of its three vertices; a sphere centered at c with radius R has bounds c ± ( R , R , R ) . An infinite plane has no useful finite AABB. MITG-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:20:30–00:30:00)

A bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) groups primitives under a tree of conservative boxes. Each primitive belongs to one leaf, while sibling boxes may overlap. Each internal node has the bound of its descendants. To query, intersect a node box; if it misses, discard the entire subtree. If it hits, visit its children, then test primitives in reached leaves. The data structure is useful only when bounds prune enough work: a camera inside a single bunny-sized box, or a million identical triangles, may yield little improvement. Simple split rules divide a list in half, sort by centroid and take the median, or reuse an artist’s modeling hierarchy. The lecture stresses that the common constructions are heuristics, not a proof of O ( log ⁡ N ) traversal. A balanced-looking tree can still overlap heavily or be poorly aligned to actual rays. MITG-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:30:00–00:38:30; 00:45:45–00:46:40)

A BVH must not stop merely because it finds a primitive in the first child box it intersects. Sibling boxes can overlap: a ray may enter the right sibling’s box first, hit a primitive geometrically assigned to that box, and still encounter a nearer primitive assigned to the left sibling. The safe general rule is to examine all candidate children, retain the least valid t , and prune only when a node’s near bound is farther than the best hit already found. The BVH stores each object once, so avoiding duplicate primitive tests costs little memory. MITG-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:38:30–00:46:40)

A k -d tree makes a different trade. It partitions space with axis-aligned split planes, so the cells do not overlap, but a triangle crossing a split may be stored in both children. A node records the split axis, split coordinate, and front/back children or leaf primitives. This avoids overlapping spatial regions and enables near-to-far traversal, at the expense of duplicated storage and possibly repeated primitive tests. There is no universal construction rule: splitting the world cell in half can leave one child full and one almost empty; splitting by median primitive centroid balances counts but not necessarily ray cost. The surface area heuristic (SAH) estimates traversal cost from the areas of candidate child regions and the number of primitives assigned to them, reflecting the likelihood that rays visit those regions. It is a useful engineering heuristic rather than a guarantee. MITG-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:46:40–00:54:10; 01:01:45–01:05:10)

Traversal correctness comes from parameter intervals. At a node, let the parent region be intersected by the ray over [ t s , t e ] . Find the ray parameter t m at the split plane. If t m < t s , the ray reaches only the back child in this interval. If t m > t e , it reaches only the front child. Otherwise it reaches both; determine which is near from the ray direction, recurse into that child over its subinterval first, and search the far child only if no valid hit was found before t m . A primitive duplicated into both cells may have a geometric hit outside the current cell interval; reject such a hit here, or the first cell could report a surface that lies beyond the split and incorrectly hide a nearer object. The parent bounds and inherited interval are therefore not bookkeeping trivia—they are what make early termination safe. MITG-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:50:45–01:01:30)

A regular grid provides another organization. Insert each primitive into every cell its bound or geometry overlaps. Traverse the cells in the order the ray crosses them and test only their contents. The 3D digital differential analyzer (DDA) keeps the next crossing parameter for each axis. From current cell entry t , advance to the smallest next boundary time, update the corresponding cell index, and update that axis’s next time by a constant increment. For example, if the next x face is at t x = 2.4 and the next y face is at t y = 2.1 , advance y first; then compare the updated times again. Stop once a valid primitive hit lies within the current cell’s interval. A primitive spanning several cells can be tested repeatedly unless the implementation marks already-tested primitives. Tiny cells increase walking overhead; large cells put many primitives in each bucket. Uniform grids suit scenes with roughly uniform object scale, and their ordered loops can be simpler to parallelize than recursive trees. MITG-12 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (01:09:20–01:18:50)

Visibility: compare depth locally or trace paths

After projection, screen x and y say where a sample lands; depth says which opaque surface is in front. A depth buffer stores one depth per pixel next to the color buffer. When a triangle rasterizes a pixel, interpolate its depth and compare it with the stored value. If the new fragment is nearer, replace both color and stored depth; otherwise leave the current pixel alone. This makes the result independent of the order in which opaque triangles were submitted and reduces to a local comparison that GPUs can perform in parallel. (P3-07)

The comparison direction and clear value are paired with the chosen depth mapping. In this lecture’s image-space convention, near is larger than far, so it initializes to a very negative value and keeps the larger depth. In a common modern window-depth setup, nearer values are smaller, the buffer is cleared to 1, and a LESS test keeps the smaller value. Reversing one part without the other makes occlusion wrong. Perspective depth is nonlinear after divide: depth values are crowded toward the far end, so a needlessly tiny near distance relative to the far distance reduces precision. (P3-05–07)

A depth buffer solves visibility for opaque samples, not every light-transport problem. Transparent surfaces need blending and ordering or a different method; a single nearest-surface value cannot represent multiple layers. A shadow asks what is hidden from a light, while the camera depth test asks what is hidden from the eye. These are separate visibility queries. (P3-07)

Ray tracing asks the visibility question differently. For each pixel sample, cast a primary ray from the eye through the image plane, intersect it with scene objects, and keep the nearest hit. A naïve implementation costs about O ( P N ) for P pixel samples and N objects. As a scale check, one million pixels times one thousand objects is roughly one billion intersection tests. A ray-sphere test can reduce to distance from the ray to the sphere center; triangles need a ray-triangle intersection test. Each pixel ray can be processed independently, but a straightforward implementation may perform much more work than rasterization.

Secondary rays add effects: send a shadow ray from a surface point toward a light and mark it shadowed if another object is encountered first; send a reflected ray in the mirror direction; send a refracted/transmitted ray through transparent material. Recursive effects can branch, so realism brings a potentially large cost. This is the geometric-optics view of light paths traced backward from the eye. The lecture contrasts its cost with the practical depth-buffer solution and previews later ray-tracing work. (P3-07)

Painter’s algorithm sorts surfaces back-to-front and draws them in order, but cyclic overlap can make a global ordering impossible unless geometry is split. A Warnock-style algorithm handles a complex screen region by subdividing it into four smaller regions and repeating until each region is simple enough to resolve. These methods illustrate different ways to turn a global visibility problem into a useful order or smaller local problems. (P3-07)

Choose visibility algorithms by their ordering and data needs

The 2009 survey groups five approaches by where they solve visibility:

Depth buffer Per pixel, keep the nearest depth Submission order can be arbitrary; maps well to parallel hardware Extra memory; transparent layers need more than one depth
Scanline Process polygon spans row by row, updating depth/order Incremental edge/attribute bookkeeping More complex active-edge state; often combined with depth buffer

Ray tracing Send rays through samples and choose nearest intersections Natural reflection, shadow and refraction paths Intersection cost grows; acceleration structures required

A painter’s algorithm is like painting distant scenery first and nearer objects over it. If every polygon can be placed in one back-to-front list, later drawing hides earlier color. Intersecting polygons can form a cycle—A in front of B in one region, B in front of C elsewhere, C in front of A—so there is no global scalar sort that works. A binary space partition (BSP) tree chooses a polygon plane, classifies other polygons against it, and splits polygons that cross. The tree stores an ordering, and traversal visits the far subtree, the node polygon, and the near subtree; which subtree is “far” depends on the camera’s side of the plane. Bounding volumes can avoid unnecessary split tests. A badly chosen splitter produces a deep, unbalanced tree and many tiny fragments, so random or heuristic plane selection can help. (P3-07, P3-14)

Warnock’s method works in screen space. For a viewport region, collect the polygons that overlap it. If there are none, leave it blank; if one surface alone determines the region, draw it; if a nearer polygon covers the whole region (a surrounder), discard surfaces hidden behind it. Otherwise divide the rectangle into four child windows and repeat with smaller polygon lists. At a one-pixel region, choose the nearest candidate; subdividing below pixel size and averaging samples improves edge coverage. This is a quadtree. Its tasks can be sent to separate processors, which explains why its recursive subdivision remains useful even when the original algorithm is not the main renderer. (P3-14)

The methods are complementary. Rasterization still needs coverage and interpolated attributes; the depth test chooses visible opaque samples. A BSP or painter order can avoid a per-pixel depth store in special geometry, but incurs scene sorting and splitting. Ray tracing resolves nearest intersections per ray and naturally adds secondary visibility queries. The lecture also mentions scanline/depth hybrids. Do not read the historical claim “this algorithm is dead” as a timeless API fact: hardware shifts the balance, and components of older methods reappear inside newer parallel renderers. (P3-14)

Ray intersection, recursive paths, and acceleration

A ray is an origin O and direction D : R ( t ) = O + t D for t ≥ 0 . Normalize D if you want t to measure distance. For a plane through point Q with normal N , an intersection lies on both the ray and plane, so

N ⋅ ( O + t D − Q ) = 0 , t = N ⋅ ( Q − O ) N ⋅ D .

If N ⋅ D = 0 , the ray is parallel; if it also lies in the plane, the intersection is non-unique and must be handled separately. Reject negative t because those hits lie behind the ray origin. This dot-product formula is the same signed-plane test used for clipping, now used to find a location rather than classify a whole endpoint. (P3-15)

For a sphere centered at C with radius r , let V = C − O and let D be unit length. Project V onto the ray with h = V ⋅ D . The squared perpendicular distance is d ⊥ 2 = V ⋅ V − h 2 . If this exceeds r 2 , the ray misses. Otherwise its roots are t n e a r = h − r 2 − d ⊥ 2 and t f a r = h + r 2 − d ⊥ 2 . Keep the smallest nonnegative root. This geometric derivation shows why spheres are an easy primitive, though a production implementation can solve the quadratic directly and must consider floating-point tolerances. For a triangle, intersect its plane and test the hit’s barycentric coordinates; all three coordinates must be nonnegative and sum to one. (P3-15)

A recursive Whitted-style renderer starts with the camera ray and shades the nearest surface. For each point light, cast a shadow ray toward it; a hit before the light means the direct source is blocked. For a reflective surface, reflect the incoming direction about the normal and trace a new ray; for a dielectric surface, use its refractive index and Snell’s law to trace transmission, with total internal reflection as an edge case. Add each returned radiance scaled by the material’s reflective/transmissive coefficient. Stop at a maximum bounce depth or when contribution becomes negligible, otherwise repeated mirrors can recurse indefinitely. The source transcript says “Turner Whitted”; the correct author is Turner Whitted, whose 1980 paper describes the recursive ray tree (ACM record, paper copy). (P3-07, P3-15)

Backward ray tracing traces from the eye because only paths contributing to visible pixels matter. It efficiently handles direct shadows and ideal mirror/refraction chains. It can miss a narrow light path that does not begin at the camera ray, such as a caustic where a lamp reflects from a mirror onto a diffuse table. Forward light tracing marks where emitted paths land; combining sampling strategies is harder but recovers such paths. Diffuse interreflection requires many scattered directions, so each surface hit can spawn many more rays. More rays improve coverage but increase work. This is the geometric-optics model, not a wave-optics simulation. (P3-15)

The naïve primary-ray cost is roughly P × N primitive tests for P samples and N objects. Spatial acceleration groups objects. An octree recursively divides 3D space into eight cells; a ray visits only cells it crosses and tests the objects stored there. A kd-tree uses axis-aligned plane splits, often at adaptive positions rather than the exact center. Smaller leaves reduce primitive tests but increase tree traversal and storage. Objects crossing cell boundaries may be referenced in multiple nodes or handled by a spatial split rule. The ray-versus-cell traversal resembles grid line rasterization because both increment through ordered cells. (P3-15)

Implicit surfaces give another difficulty. If a surface is F ( x , y , z ) = 0 , substitute the ray coordinates to obtain F ( O x + t D x , O y + t D y , O z + t D z ) = 0 , a one-variable root-finding problem. A quadratic can be solved directly; a generic polynomial of degree five or more has no general formula in radicals. Numerical root-finding is needed for many implicit surfaces. Neighboring pixel rays usually hit nearby points, so the previous ray’s root can seed the next search, but at silhouettes a tiny screen shift may jump to a different branch or miss the surface. This is why ray tracing a sphere or triangle is straightforward while ray tracing a high-order freeform surface is a numerical-analysis problem. (P3-15)

Scene graphs and articulated geometry

Hierarchies separate reusable local structure from global pose, allowing articulated surfaces to follow moving skeletons.

Forward kinematics makes inverse kinematics differentiable

A rig is a hierarchy of segments and joints. Forward kinematics starts at a root and composes each joint transform down the hierarchy to compute the position of a hand, foot, or other end effector. For a two-link planar chain with root p 0 , local segment vectors u 0 , u 1 , and joint angles θ 0 , θ 1 , let R ( θ ) be the 2D rotation matrix. Then

p 1 = p 0 + R ( θ 0 ) u 0 , p 2 = p 1 + R ( θ 0 ) R ( θ 1 ) u 1 .

The second rotation is relative to the first segment, so rotating the root joint also moves every child. Equivalently in the plane, rotations can be written as multiplication by complex numbers e i θ , where i 2 = − 1 .

Inverse kinematics reverses the question. Rather than specifying joint angles and asking where the hand goes, specify a target and ask which angles place the hand there. For a target p ∗ , one simple objective is

E ( θ 0 , θ 1 ) = 1 2 ‖ p ∗ − p 2 ( θ 0 , θ 1 ) ‖ 2 .

The Jacobian is the matrix of endpoint derivatives with respect to joint angles. The chain rule combines it with the residual p 2 − p ∗ to get a gradient, which can drive gradient descent. A character rig applies the same idea to many joints: asking the hand to reach a prop allows an optimizer to coordinate the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and torso. Joint limits, balance, and pose preferences can be added as constraints or penalties. Different configurations may reach the same target, and a target beyond the limb’s reach cannot achieve zero error, so the solver’s returned pose is a best feasible fit, not necessarily a unique or globally ideal artistic pose. Why linear blends can twist a character limb

Suppose one bone transform rotates a vertex +90 degrees and another rotates it -90 degrees. A naïve matrix blend at equal weights can approach a collapsed matrix rather than a halfway rotation. Applied to vertices along a limb, this can create a candy-wrapper twist or volume loss. A better rotation interpolation preserves the group structure of rotations, for example by quaternion interpolation, while translation or stretch is blended separately. This is one reason a transformation representation must reflect the object being interpolated. Scene graphs: reuse local structure, compute global pose

A scene graph represents a complex object as a hierarchy of local coordinate frames. A robot arm is a useful abstract model: the forearm is positioned relative to the upper arm; the hand is positioned relative to the forearm. If the parent is moved or rotated, every descendant follows automatically. For a node with local transform M local , its world transform is

M world = M parent,world M local .

The ordering is the whole point: local transforms are composed along the path from root to node. A tree can reuse one definition of a hand or wheel and place it in multiple poses. It also provides a natural animation state: joint parameters modify local transforms, and traversal computes the consequences. The lecture explores hierarchical modeling, multiplying transforms along the graph, and inverse-kinematics questions such as choosing joint angles to place an endpoint MITG-05 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:10:00–00:25:00; 00:35:00–00:50:00).

Each edge in the tree has a local transform, and a node’s world transform is the product of all edge transforms from the root. For a torso, shoulder, elbow, and wrist, for instance,

M wrist world = M torso world M shoulder torso M elbow shoulder M wrist elbow .

The wrist mesh can be authored once in wrist-local coordinates and transformed by this product. If the shoulder rotates, the elbow and wrist move because their world transforms inherit that factor; the wrist’s own local geometry does not need to be rewritten. A useful tree drawing is: world → torso → left upper arm → left forearm → left hand; the right arm is a second child branch. This is also why a scene graph is a reuse structure, not just a list of objects. An instanced chair can reuse a single chair mesh under several chair-to-world transforms. A character’s parts use parent-child transforms that encode attachment. The 2021 lecture gives the nested arm/hand/finger example and describes matrix push/pop as a traversal technique: descend while accumulating transforms, then restore the parent’s transform when returning to a sibling branch MITG-04 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) · MITG-05 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:53:20–00:54:33; , 00:02:14–00:04:46).

Rotation around a joint requires one more composition detail. A rotation matrix rotates around its own origin, so a forearm joint displaced from the local origin needs T ( c ) R T ( − c ) : move the pivot c to the origin, rotate, then move it back. With column vectors, the rightmost operation happens first. Forgetting either translation makes the bone spin around the model origin rather than bend at the elbow. This same “translate to pivot, rotate, translate back” pattern appears in mechanical parts, doors, and wheels.

An inverse-kinematics (IK) problem reverses the usual direction of evaluation. Forward kinematics says, “given joint values, where is the hand?” IK says, “given a target for the hand, what joint values reach it?” There may be many solutions, no exact solution, or solutions that violate joint constraints. Even a two-link planar arm makes the geometry clear: with lengths L 1 , L 2 , a target at distance r is reachable only when | L 1 − L 2 | ≤ r ≤ L 1 + L 2 . Animation then needs a policy to choose among valid poses, such as smoothness over time or a preferred bend direction. A hierarchy supplies the forward model; solving for a pose is a separate optimization problem.

For a planar two-link arm with lengths L 1 , L 2 and joint angles θ 1 , θ 2 , the wrist position is

x = L 1 cos ⁡ θ 1 + L 2 cos ⁡ ( θ 1 + θ 2 ) , y = L 1 sin ⁡ θ 1 + L 2 sin ⁡ ( θ 1 + θ 2 ) .

The cosine rule gives cos ⁡ θ 2 = ( r 2 − L 1 2 − L 2 2 ) / ( 2 L 1 L 2 ) , where r = x 2 + y 2 . This immediately gives the reach condition | L 1 − L 2 | ≤ r ≤ L 1 + L 2 . If both links are one unit long and the target is at ( 1 , 1 ) , then r = 2 and cos ⁡ θ 2 = 0 , so the elbow angle has the two familiar bent configurations θ 2 = ± π / 2 . The endpoint alone does not choose between them; a rig needs an elbow-side preference, joint limits, or a continuity rule from the previous frame.

For a longer chain, define the forward-kinematics function e = f ( θ ) , mapping all joint parameters to an end-effector position. Given target e ∗ , one seeks f ( θ ) = e ∗ . Near a current guess θ 0 , first-order Taylor expansion gives

f ( θ 0 + Δ θ ) ≈ f ( θ 0 ) + J f ( θ 0 ) Δ θ .

The Jacobian J f contains the partial derivative of each end-effector coordinate with respect to each joint parameter. Solve J f Δ θ ≈ e ∗ − f ( θ 0 ) , update the joint values, and repeat. This is a Newton-style local solve: it works well when the current pose is close and the Jacobian is well-conditioned, but may fail when the target is unreachable, constraints are active, or a poor initial guess crosses into another bend configuration. Representing orientation with Euler angles is compact and readable but can suffer gimbal lock, where one degree of freedom is lost for a particular alignment. Unit quaternions encode rotation without that singularity and allow interpolation along a rotation path, but q and − q represent the same orientation. The 2021 hierarchy lecture mentions the quaternion double-cover issue while discussing rotation and IK MITG-05 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:25:00–00:45:00). This is a reminder that representation choices have side effects: a representation that makes composition easy may need care when interpolating or enforcing continuity.

A kinematic chain records rigid transforms and joint degrees of freedom. A hinge contributes one angle; a ball joint can contribute three rotational degrees of freedom; a slider contributes translation. Each joint may also have limits. A skeleton provides a compact pose; skinning maps that pose to the many vertices of a surface. In linear blend skinning, also called skeleton-subspace deformation, vertex (x) receives a weighted blend of bone transformations:

x ′ ( t ) = ∑ j = 1 m w j ( x ) M j ( t ) M j ( 0 ) − 1 x , ∑ j w j ( x ) = 1.

The inverse bind transform M j ( 0 ) − 1 first expresses the vertex in bone j ’s bind-pose frame; the current bone transform sends it into the animated pose. The weights say how strongly each bone influences a vertex. A vertex at a joint might be influenced by both adjacent bones; a vertex in the forearm mostly follows that bone. Weights should sum to one so the blend remains normalized. Skinning can transform thousands of vertices using a small set of joint matrices and weights, allowing artists to shape a mesh once and then animate it through the skeleton.

To unpack the formula, let B j map bone j ’s local coordinates into world coordinates in the bind pose, and let P j ( t ) map that bone’s local frame into world coordinates in the current pose. The vertex’s bone-local bind coordinate is B j − 1 x . Its posed candidate from that bone is P j ( t ) B j − 1 x . Then blend those candidates:

x ′ ( t ) = ∑ j = 1 m w j ( x ) P j ( t ) B j − 1 x , w j ( x ) ≥ 0 , ∑ j w j ( x ) = 1.

The inverse bind matrix matters because a bone’s motion is measured relative to its rest placement. If a joint is translated one unit from the model origin, applying only its current transform would rotate a vertex around the origin. Multiplying by B j − 1 first removes the rest placement; applying P j ( t ) then moves it with the animated bone. If the current pose equals the bind pose, P j = B j , so every bone candidate is B j B j − 1 x = x and the mesh stays exactly in its rest position. This identity is a valuable implementation check.

For a simple numerical example in 2D homogeneous coordinates, take a rest vertex x = ( 2 , 0 , 1 ) T and a bone whose bind transform is B = T ( 1 , 0 ) . Its bone-local coordinate is B − 1 x = ( 1 , 0 , 1 ) T . If the posed bone transform is P = T ( 1 , 0 ) R 90 ∘ , then P B − 1 x = ( 1 , 1 , 1 ) T : the point has rotated about the bone origin at x = 1 , not about the model origin. With two bones, calculate each posed candidate this way first, then blend the resulting points using their weights.

Linear blend skinning is fast because each vertex needs a small number of matrix-vector products, but averaging rotations can shrink the mesh. Let a point be x = ( 1 , 0 ) and let two bones rotate it by + 90 ∘ and − 90 ∘ around the same joint. The candidate points are ( 0 , 1 ) and ( 0 , − 1 ) . Equal weights produce

1 2 ( 0 , 1 ) + 1 2 ( 0 , − 1 ) = ( 0 , 0 ) .

The point collapses onto the joint even though neither bone transform collapses anything. Along a real elbow or wrist this appears as volume loss or a twisting “candy-wrapper” pinch. More weights or smoother interpolation do not remove the underlying issue: a weighted average of rotation matrices need not be a rotation matrix. Common improvements include dual-quaternion blending for rigid motions, additional corrective shapes, or artist-authored weight adjustments; each trades extra machinery or authoring work for better shape preservation. Hierarchical modeling: reuse geometry through transforms

A scene is easier to build from reusable pieces than from one long list of hard-coded world coordinates. The lecture’s 1977-era Midas example treats a text file as a small modeling program. A polygon command defines a unit square. A transform command moves or rotates it; a create command makes a transformed copy while keeping the source; an extrude command sweeps a face into side polygons; an assemble command combines named pieces. A table can reuse one cube model for four legs and a top, changing only each instance’s transform. In the column-vector convention, that product order reverses. Smooth interpolating paths and camera motion

A Bezier control polygon is a handle: its interior controls generally do not lie on the curve. A camera path often needs to pass through chosen waypoints, so Catmull–Rom uses interpolating cubic segments. Between waypoints P 1 and P 2 , a uniform Catmull–Rom segment takes endpoint tangents from neighbors, commonly m 1 = ( P 2 − P 0 ) / 2 and m 2 = ( P 3 − P 1 ) / 2 . With these tangents, adjacent pieces meet at each waypoint and share a tangent direction and scale, giving C 1 continuity for uniform parameter spacing. For unevenly spaced waypoints, chord-length or centripetal parameterization is needed to avoid inappropriate speed and loops; the uniform formula assumes equal parameter steps. (P3-20)

The Hermite basis writes this segment directly from endpoints and tangents:

P ( t ) = ( 2 t 3 − 3 t 2 + 1 ) P 1 + ( t 3 − 2 t 2 + t ) m 1 + ( − 2 t 3 + 3 t 2 ) P 2 + ( t 3 − t 2 ) m 2 .

At t = 0 the weights select P 1 and its derivative is m 1 ; at t = 1 the curve reaches P 2 with derivative m 2 . These four boundary conditions determine the four coefficients of a cubic polynomial. Bezier, Ferguson/Hermite, and Catmull–Rom are different ways to specify a cubic; converting between their coefficient/control-point forms is a matrix change of basis, not a different geometric degree. (P3-20)

For a numeric intuition, let neighboring one-dimensional waypoints be P 0 = 0 , P 1 = 0 , P 2 = 10 , P 3 = 10 . Uniform Catmull–Rom gives both tangents as 5. At t = 1 / 2 , the Hermite basis has weights 1 / 2 , 1 / 8 , 1 / 2 , − 1 / 8 , so P ( 1 / 2 ) = 0.5 ( 0 ) + 0.125 ( 5 ) + 0.5 ( 10 ) − 0.125 ( 5 ) = 5 . The path reaches the midpoint smoothly; unlike straight interpolation, its endpoint speeds match the neighboring waypoint context. A small perturbation to a point affects nearby segments rather than making a single global Lagrange polynomial swing wildly across the entire route. (P3-20)

Continuity labels describe different experiences. C 0 means neighboring pieces meet in position but may have a corner. C 1 also matches first derivatives, removing a velocity jump. C 2 matches second derivatives, so curvature changes less abruptly. Animation, dynamics, optimization, and PDEs

Animation makes scene state change over time. Keyframes specify selected values, and interpolation fills the intervals. Position often lies in Euclidean space; orientations lie on a curved space, such as the unit quaternion sphere, so componentwise interpolation can take a poor path. Cubic splines use polynomial segments and control continuity: positional continuity means segments meet, tangent continuity means velocities agree, and higher continuity constrains acceleration. Smoothness and temporal coherence matter as much as matching endpoints. CMU-20 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Animation has long used sequences of still images to suggest movement. A spinning phenakistoscope hides the changing disc behind a slit so each drawing appears as a separate snapshot rather than a blur. Muybridge’s photographs answered whether all four horse legs leave the ground during a gallop. Digital animation continues this pattern: a new display or computer changes how artists can construct and show motion. The perceptual explanation is not simply that an image lingers on the retina. The brain compares successive visual events and anticipates likely movement (the beta and phi phenomena); a flashing pattern can therefore make equal-speed objects appear to move differently. These cues help explain why the timing and spacing of animated frames matter. CMU-20 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Hand-drawn production assigns key poses to a senior animator and fills the intervening drawings with assistants. Computer keyframing applies the same division to scene parameters: key values can be position, color, light intensity, camera zoom, or the degrees of freedom in a character rig. A bouncing ball, for example, may have keys at launch, ground contact, and successive rises and falls. The computer interpolates the parameter values between these events. This saves repetitive work, but the interpolation rule determines the perceived motion. CMU-20 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

A spline is a piecewise polynomial function. Let t i denote an ordered key time (a knot), and let f i be the value required at that time. Interpolation means f ( t i ) = f i . Linear interpolation joins keys with straight segments; position stays continuous, but the velocity jumps at each key, which implies an abrupt acceleration. A cubic polynomial has four coefficients, so two endpoint positions alone leave two degrees of freedom unresolved. Supplying both endpoint velocities makes a unique cubic Hermite segment. On normalized time s = ( t − t 0 ) / ( t 1 − t 0 ) , where s = 0 is the first key and s = 1 is the second, the segment is

p ( s ) = h 00 ( s ) p 0 + h 10 ( s ) m 0 + h 01 ( s ) p 1 + h 11 ( s ) m 1 ,

where p 0 , p 1 are endpoint positions, m 0 , m 1 are endpoint tangent vectors scaled for the segment’s time interval, and

h 00 = 2 s 3 − 3 s 2 + 1 , h 10 = s 3 − 2 s 2 + s , h 01 = − 2 s 3 + 3 s 2 , h 11 = s 3 − s 2 .

These four basis polynomials satisfy the four endpoint constraints: p ( 0 ) = p 0 , p ( 1 ) = p 1 , p ′ ( 0 ) = m 0 , and p ′ ( 1 ) = m 1 . A cubic is useful partly because a slightly bent physical strip minimizes an energy involving the squared second derivative; it also provides convenient continuity with few parameters. Raising polynomial degree is not a universal cure: high-degree interpolation can oscillate sharply near the ends (the Runge phenomenon). CMU-20 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

For a sequence of keys, each segment has its own polynomial coefficients. Matching the value at every knot gives interpolation; matching first derivatives gives continuous velocity; matching second derivatives gives continuous acceleration, or C 2 continuity. A natural cubic spline closes the remaining degrees of freedom by setting second derivative to zero at the first and last keys. It interpolates and is C 2 , but the segment coefficients are coupled through a global linear system, so moving one key can affect distant parts of the curve. A Bezier/Hermite chain instead gives each segment its endpoints and endpoint tangents independently. It has interpolation and local editing, but second derivatives may jump. Catmull–Rom splines infer tangent at key i from neighboring values, approximately ( f i + 1 − f i − 1 ) / ( t i + 1 − t i − 1 ) , avoiding the need to hand-author velocities; this makes it a practical starting point for camera paths. A cubic B-spline basis has local support and smooth joins, but the curve generally approximates rather than passes through its control points. These are tradeoffs among interpolation, smoothness, and local control; no one cubic spline provides all three automatically. NURBS can exactly represent circles and ovals, and clothoid curves are chosen in road design for their acceleration behavior. CMU-20 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

ODE integration and stability

An ordinary differential equation specifies the derivative of a state. If state y obeys y ˙ = f ( y , t ) , explicit Euler advances by y n + 1 = y n + h f ( y n , t n ) . Its local truncation error is proportional to h 2 and accumulated global error is generally proportional to h . Halving the time step roughly doubles work while often reducing error by about a factor of two. Higher-order Runge-Kutta methods sample the derivative several times per step to improve accuracy.

Stability is distinct from accuracy. A method may have small one-step error but amplify it over repeated steps. For a damped oscillator or stiff spring, explicit Euler becomes unstable if the step is too large relative to the natural frequency. Implicit Euler evaluates f at the next state and requires solving an equation, but it can remain stable for much larger steps. Symplectic Euler updates momentum-like variables before positions and often preserves the qualitative energy behavior of mechanical systems. No method is best for all tasks: real-time games may favor predictable cost, offline simulation may favor accuracy, and stiff material models may need implicit solves. A solver can project a tentative update back onto that set or use Lagrange multipliers to introduce constraint forces. The numerical step must respect these conditions; otherwise joints stretch or objects interpenetrate. Keep physical and numerical error separate

An image can look wrong for several distinct reasons. The scene model can be wrong, the chosen physical model can omit effects, the discretization can be coarse, the estimator can have high variance, or the display transform can be inappropriate. These errors should not be conflated. More samples reduce Monte Carlo variance but do not repair a biased material model. More mesh triangles reduce geometric discretization error but do not fix a bad camera. A correct linear-light solution can still look unexpected when mapped into a limited display gamut. A stable simulation can solve the wrong PDE if boundary conditions were encoded incorrectly.

When diagnosing a result, identify the mathematical target first. For rasterization, is the target pixel area coverage or a single center sample? For a light integral, is the measure solid angle or area? For geometry processing, is the target a smooth surface, a discrete mesh, or preserved topology? For animation, should the curve match only positions or also velocities? For optimization, are constraints part of the problem or just penalties? Good questions reveal which error source must be addressed. This precision is the common thread across the course’s rendering, imaging, geometry, and animation topics.

First-order state formulation

Many second-order mechanical equations can be rewritten as first-order systems by including velocity in the state. A particle satisfies x ˙ = v and v ˙ = F ( x , v , t ) / m . The state vector is ( x , v ) . This allows the same integrator to advance different systems. For a thrown rock, force is gravity and acceleration is constant. For a spring, force depends on displacement and produces oscillation. For a pendulum, position is constrained to a circle, so the force must be projected onto the tangent or represented with an angle coordinate. Lagrangian mechanics and generalized coordinates

Newtonian mechanics tracks forces in Cartesian coordinates. Lagrangian mechanics instead defines kinetic energy T and potential energy V , then uses Lagrangian L = T − V . For generalized coordinate q , the Euler-Lagrange equation is

d d t ∂ L ∂ q ˙ − ∂ L ∂ q = Q ,

where Q is generalized nonconservative force. A pendulum can use one angle θ instead of two Cartesian coordinates plus a length constraint. Reduce constraints with a coordinate that fits the motion

A generalized coordinate is a number (or compact vector of numbers) that specifies a system’s configuration without storing redundant positions. A freely moving point in the plane needs two coordinates. Six freely moving billiard balls need twelve. A pendulum bob in the plane appears to need two Cartesian coordinates, but its distance from the pivot is fixed. Recording both coordinates and separately enforcing the circle constraint carries an unnecessary degree of freedom. Instead, use the angle θ from the downward vertical: the bob location is x = ℓ sin ⁡ θ , y = − ℓ cos ⁡ θ . Every value of θ already lies on the circle of radius ℓ .

The Lagrangian method makes the benefit concrete. The bob’s speed is ℓ θ ˙ , so its kinetic energy is T = 1 2 m ℓ 2 θ ˙ 2 . Taking zero potential energy at the bottom gives V = m g ℓ ( 1 − cos ⁡ θ ) . The Lagrangian is L = T − V . Its derivatives are

∂ L ∂ θ ˙ = m ℓ 2 θ ˙ , d d t ∂ L ∂ θ ˙ = m ℓ 2 θ ¨ , ∂ L ∂ θ = − m g ℓ sin ⁡ θ .

With no external torque, Euler–Lagrange says the time derivative on the left equals the coordinate derivative on the right. Dividing by m ℓ 2 gives

θ ¨ = − g ℓ sin ⁡ θ .

This is already a complete equation of motion. Near the stable resting position, θ is small and sin ⁡ θ ≈ θ . The equation becomes θ ¨ + g ℓ θ = 0 , a harmonic oscillator with angular frequency ω = g / ℓ . Its motion is θ ( t ) = A cos ⁡ ( ω t ) + B sin ⁡ ( ω t ) , where A and B are set by initial angle and velocity. This approximation predicts that a small pendulum’s period is nearly independent of amplitude. At larger angles, the sine cannot be replaced by its argument; the nonlinear equation is generally advanced numerically. CMU-21 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

A model can be simple locally and rich globally

Particle systems illustrate a useful modeling pattern: give each element a small state and simple local rules, then let interactions produce a large-scale phenomenon. A particle’s state typically includes position and velocity. A flocking agent can experience three forces: attraction toward a nearby group’s center, separation from neighbors that are too close, and alignment toward their average velocity. These rules are a model of motion, not a claim that the simulation contains a complete psychology of birds or people. They can still reproduce recognizable group motion or estimate where a crowd may bottleneck during evacuation.

The same representation appears at very different scales. Particle interactions approximate fluid motion; contact and friction rules produce granular motion such as sand or popcorn; atom-scale forces support molecular dynamics; gravitational interactions model stars or dark matter. A naive all-pairs force calculation costs roughly N 2 interactions for N particles. Hierarchical approaches such as Barnes–Hut or the fast multipole method group distant particles and approximate their combined influence, reducing work for appropriate spatial distributions. For sufficiently dense fluids or elastic materials, a continuum model may be a better description: it assigns fields such as velocity and density at every point and expresses their evolution with partial differential equations.

A mass-spring system is a discrete elastic model. For two endpoints at positions x 1 and x 2 , the current length is ℓ = ‖ x 2 − x 1 ‖ . A spring with rest length ℓ 0 and stiffness k stores potential energy

V s p r i n g = 1 2 k ( ℓ − ℓ 0 ) 2 .

The spring force pulls the endpoints toward the rest length; differentiating the energy gives its direction and magnitude. A mesh can turn this small rule into cloth: each vertex is a particle, edges connect springs, and some vertices can be pinned to a moving character. Hair strands can be chains of particles joined by springs. These models need collision detection and collision response as well: otherwise a cloth point may pass through a character’s body. Their simplicity is attractive, but spring networks can become stiff when springs strongly resist stretching. That stiffness is exactly where integration choices matter. Time steps change the behavior of a simulated system

An ordinary differential equation describes change continuously in time. A computer instead stores snapshots q k separated by time step h . The derivative is approximated by a difference,

q ˙ ( t k ) ≈ q k + 1 − q k h .

For an equation q ˙ = f ( q , t ) , forward Euler evaluates the rate at the known current state and steps along it:

q k + 1 = q k + h f ( q k , t k ) .

This is explicit: the right-hand side uses only known values. Imagine trying to follow a curved path by looking at its current tangent, then walking a distance proportional to h . If the time step is large, the tangent may point away from the path as it curves, so each step can make the next error worse.

A scalar equation exposes the stability limit without requiring a picture. Let u decay according to u ˙ = − a u , where a > 0 . The exact solution shrinks exponentially: u ( t ) = u ( 0 ) e − a t . Forward Euler gives

u k + 1 = ( 1 − a h ) u k , u n = ( 1 − a h ) n u 0 .

For numerical values to shrink rather than grow without bound, the multiplier must have magnitude below one: | 1 − a h | < 1 , which means 0 < h < 2 / a . If a is large, the allowed step is small. A stiff spring behaves like a system with a large rate constant: hair barely stretches, so explicit Euler may require thousands of internal steps per rendered frame to keep the simulation stable.

Backward Euler uses the unknown next state to evaluate the derivative:

q k + 1 = q k + h f ( q k + 1 , t k + 1 ) .

It is implicit because both sides depend on q k + 1 . The solver must find a state satisfying the equation, often through an iterative method for nonlinear forces. For the scalar decay test this rearranges to u k + 1 = u k / ( 1 + a h ) , which shrinks for every positive h ; this property is called unconditional stability for this test equation. But stability is not the same as physical accuracy. Backward Euler can drain energy from a frictionless pendulum, making the swing decay even though the model includes no friction.

Symplectic Euler staggers the update: first compute velocity using the current configuration, then update position using the new velocity. For acceleration a ( q ) ,

v k + 1 = v k + h a ( q k ) , q k + 1 = q k + h v k + 1 .

For simple conservative systems this method tends to keep energy bounded over long runs, often producing a much more convincing pendulum than either forward Euler’s energy growth or backward Euler’s numerical damping. Related methods include leapfrog and Verlet. No integrator wins on every criterion: stability, local accuracy, long-run conservation, symmetry and computational cost matter differently in visual animation, engineering and scientific prediction. A frame rate does not dictate a safe physics step; a simulation may need multiple internal substeps before each displayed frame. CMU-21 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Simulation: from forces to trajectories

A particle is a useful abstraction for a point-like object whose state changes over time. Its state might contain position (x(t)) and velocity (v(t)). Newton’s law gives

x ˙ = v , v ˙ = m − 1 F ( x , v , t ) .

The differential equation defines a continuous-time evolution, but a computer advances in finite time steps. Explicit Euler evaluates the derivative at the current state:

x n + 1 = x n + h v n , v n + 1 = v n + h a n .

This is simple and inexpensive, but the error accumulates and stiff systems can become unstable unless (h) is very small. For a pendulum, the acceleration depends on position, so each step’s estimated angle changes the next acceleration. With a coarse time step, the numerical trajectory can gain or lose energy even if the ideal pendulum conserves it. A spring is a force derived from an energy

Represent a cloth vertex by a point mass at x i . A spring joins vertices i and j , with rest length ℓ 0 and stiffness k . Define d = x i − x j , its length ℓ = ‖ d ‖ , and unit direction u = d / ℓ . The elastic potential energy is

U ( x i , x j ) = k 2 ( ℓ − ℓ 0 ) 2 .

The force is the negative energy gradient. Since ∇ x i ℓ = u ,

F i = − ∇ x i U = − k ( ℓ − ℓ 0 ) u , F j = − F i .

The sign has a direct interpretation. If the edge is too long, ℓ > ℓ 0 , then F i points toward j , pulling it shorter. If it is compressed, the force pushes the vertices apart. Hooke’s law is linear in extension magnitude, but the direction also depends on vertex positions. MITG-08 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:56:00–01:01:00)

For example, place i at ( 0 , 0 ) and j at ( 1.2 , 0 ) , with ℓ 0 = 1 and k = 10 . Then ℓ = 1.2 , u = ( − 1 , 0 ) , and F i = ( 2 , 0 ) ; vertex i is pulled right toward j while j receives ( − 2 , 0 ) . A vertex attached to several springs receives the vector sum, not the average of their magnitudes.

At ℓ ≈ 0 , the direction is undefined; robust code must avoid division by zero or handle coincident endpoints explicitly.

Contact dynamics with a nonintersection guarantee

Connect to: mesh geometry and deformation; numerical optimization; finite-element dynamics; collision detection; friction.

Incremental Potential Contact (IPC) addresses a difficult guarantee: an elastic mesh should not pass through itself, an obstacle, or itself invert between time samples. The method solves each implicit time step by minimizing an incremental action that combines inertial, elastic, and external-force terms. It treats admissible motion as a trajectory through configurations that stay intersection-free. This distinction matters: checking only that the two endpoints of a time step do not overlap can miss a collision that happened in between.

The CCD guarantee preserves feasibility from an admissible starting configuration. If the elastic model also forbids inversion, the initial state must satisfy that condition as well. For a set of nonadjacent surface primitive pairs, compute unsigned distances d k ( x ) . A trajectory is intersection-free when these distances stay positive for every time in the step. The admissible set is defined as the closure of the intersection-free trajectories: a motion that just touches and turns away is a limit of valid motions, but a motion that crosses through a surface is not. This definition handles surface obstacles, curves, and points in one framework. It is stronger than merely demanding a nonnegative unsigned distance at the endpoints.

The method adds a localized barrier to the incremental potential. Let d ^ be the user-set contact-distance accuracy. For 0 < d < d ^ , the barrier is

b ( d , d ^ ) = − ( d − d ^ ) 2 ln ⁡ ( d d ^ ) ,

and it is zero when d ≥ d ^ . It rises without bound as d approaches zero, but vanishes for pairs farther apart than the target distance. The barrier-augmented potential is

B t ( x ) = E t ( x ) + κ ∑ k ∈ C b ( d k ( x ) , d ^ ) ,

where E t is the time-step potential and κ adjusts barrier stiffness. The clamping is smooth enough for Newton optimization, and distant pairs need no barrier evaluation. The chosen d ^ controls geometric accuracy; the solver adapts κ to balance force strength against conditioning. Too small a κ allows surfaces to get unnecessarily close before the barrier repels; too large a value makes the system stiff.

The barrier by itself does not guarantee that a numerical descent step stays valid. A Newton direction can pass through a collision and land in a lower-energy configuration on the other side. IPC uses a collision-aware line search: continuous collision detection (CCD) finds an upper bound on the step before any primitive pair intersects; then backtracking reduces the step until the potential decreases. For noninverting elastic models, an inversion-aware limit is combined with the collision limit. This is the mechanism behind the paper’s intersection-free trajectory claim. Floating-point CCD is used in the solver; the authors ran slower exact rational CCD afterward on selected difficult examples as a check, and report that rational CCD was roughly 30 times slower.

The Newton solver projects local elasticity and barrier Hessians to positive semidefinite form before assembly. The assembled system is then positive definite with the inertial mass contribution. Each iteration solves a linear system, takes a CCD-limited descent step, rebuilds the nearby contact set, and repeats until a velocity-scaled step tolerance is met. The paper measures momentum-balance accuracy through the optimization residual. Barrier derivatives yield nonnegative contact-force magnitudes λ k ; a force is applied only within the selected contact distance. This gives an d ^ -complementarity condition: force-bearing pairs must be within d ^ of contact.

The barrier needs accurate distance derivatives. A vertex-triangle distance and an edge-edge distance each have several closest-point cases: vertex-to-vertex, point-to-edge, or interior projection. Each active case has a closed-form distance function and analytic derivatives. The method stores the active case with its culled contact set. At parallel edge-edge configurations, the ordinary distance function is not differentiable. IPC multiplies the edge-edge barrier by a smooth mollifier that fades it out near parallel degeneracy. Point-triangle pairs remain available to prevent missing collisions. This smoothing protects Newton convergence without claiming that the raw distance formula is smooth at every configuration.

Friction inside the same solve

Coulomb friction depends on the normal contact force and opposes tangential sliding. At a contact pair, write the relative tangential displacement as u k = T k ( x ) T ( x − x t ) , where T k gives a two-dimensional tangent basis. The sliding velocity is u k / h . Under the maximal dissipation principle, friction uses the largest opposing force allowed by μ λ k . This law is difficult for Newton’s method because it switches nonsmoothly between sliding and sticking, and the force depends on the yet-unknown contact reaction.

IPC smooths the force near zero sliding speed with a user-controlled velocity tolerance ϵ v . Then, within a nonlinear solve, it lags the tangent basis and normal force at values from the preceding iterate. With those quantities held fixed, the smoothed friction force becomes the gradient of a scalar dissipative potential. The method minimizes the barrier-augmented potential plus this lagged friction potential, then updates the basis and contact force and solves again. This is a practical variational approximation: the geometric contact and the frozen friction potential fit in one Newton framework. The authors do not guarantee convergence of every lagged force/basis update in all settings, so that limitation should be retained.

Optimization, constraints, and inverse kinematics

Optimization makes inverse problems explicit: define variables, constraints, objectives, and derivative information.

Inverse kinematics as constrained optimization

Inverse kinematics asks for joint parameters that place an end effector at a target. Forward kinematics multiplies transforms along a skeleton chain to map joint angles to hand position. Inverse kinematics reverses that relationship: find joint variables whose forward result matches the target while satisfying joint limits or pose preferences. Define objective E ( θ ) = 1 2 ‖ x h a n d ( θ ) − x t a r g e t ‖ 2 . Its gradient uses the kinematic Jacobian. A damped least-squares update avoids unstable large joint motion near singular configurations. Multiple poses can reach the same target, so regularization can prefer a natural pose or smaller change from the current configuration. This is an optimization problem embedded in a transform hierarchy. CMU-05 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) · CMU-22 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Feasibility, constraints, and optimality

An optimization problem has a domain of possible parameters, an objective to minimize, and possibly constraints. A feasible point satisfies every constraint. If two constraints have no common solution, the problem is infeasible; no optimizer can find an answer that satisfies both. For an unconstrained differentiable objective, a local minimum generally has zero gradient, and a twice-differentiable minimum has positive-semidefinite Hessian. Under constraints, these conditions change: the gradient need not be zero because motion may be blocked by a boundary. Lagrange multipliers combine objective and active constraints into stationarity equations.

Convexity means every local minimum is global. A quadratic objective with positive-semidefinite Hessian is convex. Convexity is valuable because optimization guarantees become stronger; nonconvex graphics problems such as camera pose, shape recovery, and neural rendering can have many local minima. Starting points and parameterization matter. CMU-22 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Quadratic objectives and linear systems

A least-squares problem has residual vector r = A θ − b and objective E ( θ ) = 1 2 ‖ A θ − b ‖ 2 . Differentiation yields gradient A T ( A θ − b ) ; setting it to zero gives normal equations A T A θ = A T b . If columns of A are nearly dependent, the system is poorly conditioned and small data changes can cause large parameter changes. QR or SVD factorization can solve least squares more stably than directly forming normal equations. The connection between optimization and numerical linear algebra is direct: the objective’s geometry is encoded in matrix conditioning. Feasible sets, existence, and the difference between local and global

A useful standard form is: choose a parameter vector x ∈ R n to minimize an objective f 0 ( x ) subject to inequalities g i ( x ) ≤ b i . The objective assigns a cost to each candidate; the constraints determine which candidates are allowed. A maximization can be written as minimization by negating the score. An equality g ( x ) = c can be encoded by two inequalities, g ( x ) ≤ c and − g ( x ) ≤ − c . For example, maximize the area enclosed by a curve while fixing its perimeter. The circle solves this classical isoperimetric problem, but the optimization setup is simply “minimize negative area subject to the perimeter constraint.”

Before searching for a minimum, check whether an allowed point exists. That is feasibility. Contradictory requirements such as x = 1 and x = − 1 leave no feasible point; a solver cannot satisfy both. Even when a feasible point exists, a minimum need not be attained. The function f ( x ) = x over the whole real line decreases without bound, so there is no smallest value. The function f ( x ) = e − x is bounded below by zero but approaches zero only as x → ∞ ; no finite x attains it. Sufficient existence conditions include a continuous objective over a nonempty compact feasible set (closed and bounded), or a coercive objective on a closed feasible set, where “coercive” means the cost grows toward + ∞ as the parameter moves arbitrarily far away.

A global minimum is no worse than every feasible alternative. A local minimum is no worse than candidates in a small neighborhood. The two can differ: pulling a string tight along a path tangled around buildings can produce a locally shortened path that is still globally much longer than a direct route. Multiple global minima are possible too. In the lecture’s visualization, minimizing f ( x 1 , x 2 ) = x 1 2 − x 2 2 over the unit disk x 1 2 + x 2 2 ≤ 1 gives two equal optima, ( 0 , 1 ) and ( 0 , − 1 ) . Global search can be expensive, so many practical graphics methods seek a useful local solution and depend on initialization.

For a differentiable unconstrained function, a local minimum must have zero gradient: to first order, no direction can reduce the value. But this only identifies a critical point, which may also be a maximum or saddle. The Hessian H is the matrix of second derivatives and describes local curvature. At a twice-differentiable local minimum it must be positive semidefinite, meaning v T H v ≥ 0 for every direction v . A bowl passes this test; a saddle has a direction with negative curvature. With active constraints, the unconstrained gradient need not vanish: the lowest point of a bowl restricted to a boundary can have a slope pointing outside the allowed region. Convexity and why quadratic energy often reduces to linear algebra

A set is convex if the straight segment between any two points in it stays inside it. A function is convex if its graph lies below every chord joining two points on the graph. This rules out hidden bowls separated by hills: every local minimum of a convex function is global. Strong convexity adds curvature in every direction and gives a unique minimum. These properties make convex problems attractive in geometry processing, image processing, and animation, though “convex” alone does not guarantee every large problem is cheap.

A central example is a quadratic objective

f ( x ) = 1 2 x T A x − b T x ,

where x is a vector of unknowns, A is a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix, and b is a fixed vector. The gradient is ∇ f ( x ) = A x − b and the Hessian is A . Setting the gradient to zero yields A x = b . Thus a linear system with a positive semidefinite matrix is also the condition for the bottom of a convex quadratic bowl. If A is positive definite, the bowl curves upward in every direction and the minimizer is unique. If A is only semidefinite, the bowl may be flat along some directions, so many points can achieve the same minimum. If A is indefinite, the graph has saddle directions and may fall without bound. The quadratic error used to choose a mesh-collapse position is one graphics example of this structure. CMU-22 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Gradient descent, step control, and curvature

Gradient descent is a repeated local improvement rule. At a candidate x k , the gradient ∇ f ( x k ) points toward steepest increase, so a small move in the opposite direction decreases the objective:

x k + 1 = x k − τ k ∇ f ( x k ) .

The positive step length τ k determines how far to travel. This update is forward Euler applied to the gradient-flow differential equation x ˙ = − ∇ f ( x ) . The connection explains why step size matters: a huge step can overshoot and jump around the objective; a tiny step makes slow progress. A line search tests candidate lengths and selects one that decreases the objective while meeting sufficient-decrease and curvature conditions, such as Armijo–Wolfe conditions. A small fixed step is simpler but can waste iterations or fail on poorly scaled problems.

The method follows only the slope at the current point. In a long, narrow valley, the steep direction across the valley can dominate the gradient even though progress toward the minimum is along the valley. Repeated steps zigzag across it. Newton’s method uses the Hessian to scale directions according to curvature:

x k + 1 = x k − τ k H ( x k ) − 1 ∇ f ( x k ) .

This is obtained by minimizing a local quadratic approximation of the objective. Near a well-behaved minimum it can converge much faster than gradient descent. However, an indefinite Hessian can direct a step toward a saddle instead of a minimum, and computing or solving with a full Hessian may be costly. Quasi-Newton methods such as L-BFGS approximate curvature from gradients; trust-region methods limit the update to a neighborhood where the local approximation is credible. There is no universal optimizer for all nonconvex graphics problems; objective structure, scaling, initialization, constraints, and prior experience guide the choice. Automatic differentiation

Automatic differentiation computes derivatives by applying the chain rule to the arithmetic operations in a program. A value can be paired with its derivative; addition and multiplication propagate both. If z = x y , then derivative with respect to a parameter is z ˙ = x ˙ y + x y ˙ . This differs from finite differences, which estimate a derivative by evaluating nearby values and can suffer cancellation or step-size error. It also differs from symbolic differentiation, which transforms an expression into a new symbolic formula. In graphics optimization, automatic differentiation can produce gradients through complex energy computations, though discrete visibility changes and branching still require care. Computing derivatives: four different contracts

A simulation or optimizer often needs a derivative of a complicated energy with respect to thousands of positions. The derivative is not merely a formula: the method used to obtain it has its own accuracy and cost contract.

Finite differences perturb one input and compare two outputs. For scalar f , a forward difference is

f ′ ( x ) ≈ f ( x + h ) − f ( x ) h .

This works even when f is a black box, which is why it is useful for debugging and checking a derivative implementation. But choosing h is delicate. A large step measures an average slope over a nonlocal interval (truncation error); a tiny step subtracts nearly equal floating-point numbers, losing significant digits through cancellation. In many dimensions, estimating every component this way may require one or more function evaluations per input variable.

Automatic differentiation carries derivative information through the operations the program performs. In forward mode, a scalar is paired with its value and derivative, ( x , x ˙ ) . For a product z = x y , the product rule gives z ˙ = x ˙ y + x y ˙ . For instance, initialize x = ( 2 , 1 ) , where the second component means d x / d x = 1 . Multiplying it by itself gives x 2 = ( 4 , 4 ) ; multiplying by a constant a gives ( 4 a , 4 a ) , the value and derivative of a x 2 at x = 2 . This computes derivatives to floating-point precision without selecting a perturbation size. It requires arithmetic types that propagate derivatives. Forward mode is especially natural when there are few inputs; reverse mode is advantageous for many inputs and a small number of outputs, which is common when differentiating one scalar loss with respect to many parameters.

Symbolic differentiation transforms an expression tree into a new tree for its derivative. Once built, the derivative expression can be evaluated repeatedly. Symbolic tools are helpful for algebra and analysis, but repeated chain-rule expansion can create very long formulas, especially for vector and matrix expressions. The result may be mathematically correct yet obscure a simple geometric structure.

Geometric differentiation asks what perturbation changes the quantity and by how much. For a triangle with fixed edge vector e as base and a moving opposite vertex p , area is A = 1 2 ‖ e ‖ h , where h is the perpendicular height. Moving p parallel to e changes neither the base nor height, so the gradient has no component along e . Moving it one unit in the height-increasing direction changes area by 1 2 ‖ e ‖ , so the gradient’s magnitude is half the base length and its direction is perpendicular to e . In 3D, if n is the oriented unit normal, that direction is given up to sign by n × e ; the sign depends on which triangle vertex moves and the orientation convention. This compact answer is easier to reason about than an expanded symbolic formula. The gradient points toward fastest increase; for potential energy V , the corresponding conservative force is F = − ∇ V , pointing toward decreasing potential.
