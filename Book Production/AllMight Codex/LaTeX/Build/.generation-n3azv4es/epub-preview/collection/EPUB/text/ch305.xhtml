Lens optics, focus, and image formation

Optics forms an image before a sensor samples it; focus and blur follow from lens geometry and output tolerance.

How a lens forms an image

A lens bends rays by refraction. In the thin-lens approximation, object distance u , image distance v , and focal length f satisfy

1 f = 1 u + 1 v .

This model is useful near the optical axis and for understanding focus, but a real photographic lens is a group of elements whose surfaces and refractive indices are chosen to manage aberrations. When an object lies closer than the focal length, outgoing rays diverge and the eye traces them backward to a virtual image. A magnifying glass exploits that virtual image. A close-up lens adds converging power in front of a camera lens; an extension tube moves the lens away from the sensor and changes the focus range; a macro lens is designed to focus at short distances and often supports near one-to-one image magnification. A teleconverter increases effective focal length while reducing the light reaching the sensor. These accessories change practical focus and exposure behavior, not the basic viewpoint rule. PHOTO-03 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Focus is not simply in or out across the whole scene. A point away from the focus distance forms a blur circle on the sensor. If the circle is smaller than a chosen limit, we treat the point as acceptably sharp. The circle of confusion depends on sensor size, enlargement, viewing distance, and the detail we want to see. Depth of field is the range of distances whose blur circles stay below that limit. It is a viewing tolerance, not a physical slab with sharp boundaries. A print may look sharp across a room but reveal defocus when enlarged and viewed closely. PHOTO-03 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) · PHOTO-11 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

The hyperfocal distance is the focus distance that places infinity at the far acceptable depth-of-field boundary. Focusing at or near that distance is useful for landscapes when a foreground and distant background both need acceptable sharpness. Stopping down increases depth of field, but it does not increase all forms of sharpness without limit: a smaller aperture also increases diffraction. Bokeh names the character of defocused highlights and backgrounds, not merely the amount of blur. Out-of-focus lights may be smooth discs, polygonal shapes from the aperture, cat-eye shapes near the field edge, or rings and swirls caused by optical design and vignetting. These appearances can become compositional features. PHOTO-03 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) · PHOTO-04 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Real lenses exhibit several aberrations. Spherical aberration occurs when marginal and paraxial rays do not meet at the same focus. Chromatic aberration arises because refractive index varies with wavelength, so colors focus or magnify differently. Coma makes off-axis points form comet-like shapes; astigmatism gives different focus for tangential and sagittal directions; field curvature means a flat subject may not focus uniformly on a flat sensor; distortion bends image geometry into barrel or pincushion forms. Vignetting reduces brightness toward corners. Internal reflections create flare and ghost images. Coatings, element shapes, lens groups, and software correction trade among these defects, size, weight, cost, transmission, and artistic character. A lens used “wrong” by a technical chart can still be useful if its flare, swirl, or distortion suits the image. Diffraction is a wave effect, not a manufacturing defect. Light passing through a finite aperture spreads into a point-spread pattern with a central Airy disc and weaker rings. Stopping down can improve aberrations and increase depth of field, but eventually the growing diffraction blur reduces fine detail. The practical optimum depends on the lens, sensor sampling, intended output size, focus range, and whether depth of field matters more than pixel-level acutance. “Use the sharpest aperture” is not a complete photographic instruction; one must ask which scene details should remain in focus. Focus is determined by a tolerance and an output

Imagine a portrait made with a 300-mm lens at a wide aperture. The eye nearest the camera may be perfectly focused, while the far eye or hair is already outside the acceptable blur range. Whether the portrait “has enough depth of field” depends on how it will be displayed. At a small social-media size, downsampling averages small blur differences away. At a life-size print viewed close, the same differences become visible. Richard Avedon’s large portraits exploit scale and detail; a modest screen preview does not give the same viewing experience. The thin-lens model gives a simple depth-of-field calculation. Focusing at distance s forms a sharp image at v s . An object at another distance would focus at v o . The sensor stays at v s , so the rays have not yet converged there; they make a blur circle instead. For a fixed lens and acceptable circle diameter c , a smaller aperture narrows the ray cone and reduces c . Choosing a smaller c —for a larger print or closer viewing—also narrows the acceptable depth range. So the hyperfocal distance depends on focal length, aperture, and the chosen blur limit; it is not a property of the lens alone. There are situations where one focus plane cannot cover a subject. Focus stacking captures multiple focus distances and combines the sharpest local regions. It works best for static scenes because changes in pose, wind, or occlusion complicate alignment. A light-field camera records angular information as well as position, allowing refocusing or an all-in-focus render after capture, but allocating sensor samples across those dimensions reduces conventional spatial resolution. The design question is whether the additional computational flexibility is worth the spatial detail and noise cost. Deriving the acceptable focus interval

The thin-lens equation gives a convenient way to calculate focus tolerance. Let the camera focus at subject distance s , and let the corresponding sharp image distance be v s . If an object instead lies at distance x , its ideal image would form at v x . The sensor remains at v s , so the rays from the object have not yet converged to a point when they reach it. For an aperture diameter D , similar triangles give the blur-circle diameter b on the sensor:

b = D | v s − v x | v x .

The thin-lens equation can express each image distance as v s = f s / ( s − f ) and v x = f x / ( x − f ) . Substituting those expressions and using D = f / N , where N is the f-number, produces the blur circle for an object distance x :

b = f 2 N | s − x | x ( s − f ) .

This is a paraxial thin-lens estimate: distances are measured from the lens’s principal plane, the lens is treated as thin, the subject is well outside the focal length, and the blur is judged geometrically on the sensor. Real compound lenses, pupil magnification, focus breathing, diffraction, aberrations, and print enlargement add detail to the model. The equation nevertheless shows the main dependencies: blur grows with aperture diameter, with focal length squared when f-number is held fixed, and with the separation between the focused and unfocused subject distances.

Set the largest acceptable blur to c . The near and far depth limits occur where b = c . Solving for the object distance gives

D near = s ( H − f ) H + s − 2 f , D far = s ( H − f ) H − s ,

where H is the hyperfocal distance,

H = f 2 N c + f .

When s ≥ H , the denominator in the far-distance expression is zero or negative, which means the far acceptable boundary is at infinity. In the common photographic case where H is much larger than f , the extra f is small relative to f 2 / ( N c ) , so the approximation H ≈ f 2 / ( N c ) is often used. It is important to know which version is being applied, especially for close-up work where the simple approximations become less reliable.

For a numerical example, take a 50 mm lens at f / 8 and choose an acceptable circle of confusion of 0.03 mm . The latter is a conventional full-frame planning value, not a universal sharpness standard; a very large print viewed closely would call for a smaller c . The hyperfocal distance is

H = ( 50 mm ) 2 8 ( 0.03 mm ) + 50 mm ≈ 10,467 mm = 10.47 m .

Focus at s = 3.00 m , keeping all distances in millimeters while evaluating the formulas. Then

D near = 3000 ( 10467 − 50 ) 10467 + 3000 − 100 mm ≈ 2.34 m , D far = 3000 ( 10467 − 50 ) 10467 − 3000 mm ≈ 4.19 m .

So the approximate acceptable interval is 2.34 to 4.19 m under the stated assumptions. Focus at H ≈ 10.47 m and the near boundary is approximately H / 2 ≈ 5.23 m while the far boundary reaches infinity. This result does not say that the landscape is equally sharp at every distance. It says that blur remains under the chosen circle-of-confusion tolerance, which was selected for a particular format and viewing condition. For a foreground flower at 0.5 m and a mountain at infinity, neither focusing at 3 m nor using the hyperfocal setting can make the flower and mountain both meet that same criterion with this lens and aperture. The photographer must change aperture, focus strategy, focal length, viewpoint, or use focus stacking, and then judge the resulting diffraction and motion costs. The Airy pattern supplies another scale for that judgment. For a circular aperture, monochromatic light of wavelength λ has a central diffraction-disc diameter to the first dark ring of approximately

d Airy = 2.44 λ N .

At a green wavelength of 0.55 μ m and f / 8 , this idealized diameter is 2.44 × 0.55 × 8 ≈ 10.7 μ m . With a 4 μ m pixel pitch it spans about 2.7 pixel intervals; with a 6 μ m pitch it spans about 1.8 . At f / 16 the ideal Airy diameter doubles to roughly 21.5 μ m . These ratios do not mean that detail smaller than one Airy disc disappears abruptly: sensor sampling, demosaicing, contrast, color wavelength, and output enlargement all affect visibility. They do show why a small-aperture image can look softer at pixel level even when the lens is well corrected.

Diffraction and aberration must not be conflated. Diffraction is the wave-spread limit that remains for a perfect finite aperture and grows as the aperture closes. Aberrations come from the lens’s imperfect geometric and chromatic correction; many are strongest at large apertures and diminish when marginal rays are blocked. A lens can therefore improve as it is stopped down from wide open, then lose fine contrast at very small openings as diffraction dominates. The recorded point-spread function combines these effects with sensor filtering, pixel integration, demosaicing, motion, focus error, and any later sharpening. A useful aperture is the one that gives the desired depth and acceptable blur after the complete capture and viewing chain, not the one that wins an isolated resolution test. The ideal pinhole and a real lens answer different questions

The pinhole camera is a geometric model: rays pass through one point, so any object distance can form a sharp projection. There is no finite lens focal surface, giving the ideal pinhole infinite geometric depth of field. But it does not capture infinite detail. A real hole has finite diameter and causes geometric blur. Making it smaller reduces that blur but increases diffraction. Together these effects set a practical resolution limit. A pinhole has no refractive lens surfaces, so it avoids chromatic aberration and lens distortion. Its image still has limited field of view and sensor resolution, plus diffraction blur. It models central perspective clearly, but not a full photographic camera. PHOTO-01 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

A lens’s f-number N = f / D compares focal length f with entrance-pupil diameter D . It describes geometric light-cone width and supports DoF reasoning. A real lens absorbs and reflects some light, so equal f-numbers do not guarantee equal transmitted exposure. A T-stop incorporates measured transmission loss: a lens with a geometric f-number N and transmission fraction ( τ ) has approximately T = N / τ . For example, if N = 2 and ( τ = 0.81 ), then T ≈ 2.22 . Use the f-number to reason about pupil geometry and the T-stop to compare exposure transmission more directly. Aberrations, field falloff, and correction

Chromatic aberration has two useful forms. Longitudinal aberration puts different wavelengths at different axial focus positions, so colored fringes can appear in front of and behind focus across the frame. Stopping down can hide some of this blur by blocking marginal rays. Lateral chromatic aberration gives different wavelengths different image magnifications; color fringes grow toward the edge of the frame and are often corrected by rescaling color channels in software. That correction cannot recreate information if channels have been clipped or blurred beyond recovery. PHOTO-04 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Spherical aberration means rays passing through different pupil radii focus at different axial positions. Coma gives an off-axis point an asymmetric, comet-like blur. Astigmatism makes two perpendicular families of rays from an off-axis point focus at different depths. Field curvature means best focus lies on a curved surface even when the subject and sensor are flat. Geometric distortion changes where points land, while vignetting changes how much light reaches them. A lens can therefore have a sharp center and soft corners, or preserve detail but render straight lines bowed. Many lenses naturally lose corner illumination approximately as cos 4 ⁡ θ , where θ is the ray’s angle from the optical axis. Mechanical blockage and pixel-level response can add further falloff. Software brightening of dark corners raises their noise too. PHOTO-04 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Exposure, sensors, and dynamic range

Exposure and sensors set how scene photons become measurable values, with tradeoffs in noise, range, and motion rendering.

From scene radiance to pixel brightness

Radiometry gives precise names to the different powers in the image-formation chain. Irradiance is incoming power per area, measured in watts per square meter. A point source’s intensity is power per solid angle. A solid angle Ω is an area cut from a sphere divided by the squared sphere radius; all directions occupy 4 π steradians and a hemisphere occupies 2 π . Radiance L is power per projected surface area per solid angle: it describes how much light from a scene patch reaches a particular viewing direction. A camera sensor measures irradiance at its image plane, so the camera model must relate object radiance to sensor irradiance. A pinhole explains perspective but gathers too few photons when made tiny, while a wide pinhole blurs. An ideal thin lens preserves the central ray (and therefore pinhole projection), sends a ray from its focal center out parallel to the optical axis, and sends an incoming parallel bundle through the focal center. Those three rules and similar triangles give focus and magnification. The lens gathers a finite solid angle and focuses rays from an in-focus object patch into a corresponding image patch; an object at another depth blurs because its rays meet on a different plane. Gauss’s point is that no physical lens satisfies the ideal ray rules perfectly. Designers trade defects: a fisheye lens captures a very wide field by squeezing the outer directions toward the image center, introducing radial distortion that calibration must model. E = π 4 L ( D F ) 2 cos 4 ⁡ α ,

where D is aperture diameter, F is lens-to-image distance, and α is the angle of the chief ray from the optical axis. The factor ( F / D ) 2 is the square of the familiar F-stop, so doubling the F-stop reduces sensor exposure by four. Changing aperture trades exposure against depth of field; a small aperture gives more depth of field but needs longer exposure and can blur moving scenes, while a wide aperture isolates the subject and blurs the background. Crucially, for a fixed aperture and field angle, image irradiance is proportional to scene radiance. This justifies using image gray levels as a proxy for scene brightness under controlled exposure. Exposure: choosing what the sensor can collect

For a stationary scene, the sensor’s exposure is roughly proportional to image irradiance multiplied by exposure time. Image irradiance is controlled by scene radiance, lens transmission, and aperture. The f-number is

N = f D ,

where f is focal length and D is the entrance-pupil diameter. A smaller f-number admits more light. Each full stop changes aperture area by a factor of two; because area scales with diameter squared, adjacent full-stop f-numbers differ by approximately 2 . Shutter time is a second exposure control: doubling it collects about twice the photons from an unchanged scene point, but also doubles motion displacement during exposure. ISO changes how the signal is amplified or encoded; it does not cause the scene to deliver more photons to the sensor. The camera’s shutter is not always a single instantaneous gate. A focal-plane shutter has curtains that cross the sensor. At ordinary speeds, the first curtain opens and the second closes after the chosen interval. At high speeds, the second curtain begins before the first has finished, so a moving slit exposes different rows at different times. A global shutter exposes all pixels together; a rolling shutter exposes them sequentially. A fast subject can therefore bend, skew, or stretch in a rolling-shutter image even if the exposure time of each row is short. Shutter speed controls both total exposure and temporal sampling; it is not just a brightness dial. Sensors turn photons into measurements

A digital sensor measures photoelectric charge. A photon absorbed in a photosensitive region may release an electron; the quantum efficiency is the fraction of incident photons that produce collected charge. A pixel’s well has finite capacity. Once it fills, additional charge cannot be represented faithfully and the pixel clips; charge spill can bloom into nearby regions in some sensor designs. The pixel’s conversion gain and analog-to-digital converter determine how charge becomes code values. An ADC divides a continuous voltage range into discrete bins. Quantization error is the difference between the underlying analog value and the chosen code. PHOTO-08 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

CCD and CMOS describe different circuit organizations for reading pixels. A CCD shifts charge through the array toward output amplifiers, historically offering uniformity at the cost of power and readout structure. CMOS sensors can place readout circuitry near or within pixels, allowing flexible and efficient readout. Neither word alone guarantees image quality. Sensor area, pixel pitch, quantum efficiency, full-well capacity, read noise, color filters, readout speed, and processing all matter. Back-side illumination moves wiring away from the light-entry side to improve the fraction of light reaching the photosensitive region. Microlenses direct light toward the active part of each pixel. Optical low-pass filters intentionally soften high spatial frequencies before sampling to reduce moiré and false color. PHOTO-08 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Most consumer color sensors use a color-filter array, often a Bayer pattern with twice as many green samples as red or blue. Each photosite records one filtered measurement, not a complete RGB triplet. Demosaicing estimates missing channel values using neighboring samples. A naive interpolation can smear color across an edge or create zippering and false color. Better demosaicers detect likely edges and avoid mixing values across them; some combine denoising, edge estimation, and interpolation. Alternative designs include other filter mosaics, three-sensor systems with a beam splitter, and layered sensors such as Foveon-type designs. Every design allocates sensitivity, color fidelity, resolution, and complexity differently. Noise is uncertainty in a measurement. Photon shot noise follows the random arrival of photons. If the expected count is N , its standard deviation is approximately N , so the shot-noise-limited signal-to-noise ratio is

SNR ≈ N N = N .

Doubling exposure therefore improves shot-noise-limited SNR by 2 , not by two. Four times as many photons doubles SNR. Averaging K independent aligned frames with similar exposure improves SNR by approximately K , because the wanted signal adds coherently while independent noise grows more slowly. In practice, dark current, read noise, fixed-pattern noise, clipping, motion, and imperfect alignment alter this result. Cooling reduces dark current; dark-frame subtraction can estimate fixed dark patterns; hot-pixel maps can identify persistent outliers. Dynamic range and SNR are related but different. Dynamic range compares the largest representable signal with the lowest usable signal floor. SNR asks how reliably a particular measurement rises above its noise. A scene can have large dynamic range while its shadows are noisy; a high ISO image may be bright in code values yet contain no more captured photons. Raising analog gain can make a weak signal use more of the ADC range and reduce the relative effect of some downstream noise, but it cannot remove photon shot noise or restore clipped highlights. Larger pixels often collect more photons at equal irradiance and exposure, although sensor size and lens equivalence complicate comparisons. For a more explicit budget, measure signal in collected electrons and let n be the expected photoelectron count in one pixel. Photon arrivals have approximately Poisson statistics, so shot-noise variance is n . If the readout contributes an independent root-mean-square error σ r electrons and dark current contributes σ d electrons of uncertainty, a useful first-order model is

σ total 2 ≈ n + σ r 2 + σ d 2 , SNR ≈ n n + σ r 2 + σ d 2 .

This model omits correlated pattern noise, quantization, and color-filter effects, but makes the exposure trade concrete. If a pixel collects 400 photoelectrons and other noise is small, shot noise is about 20 electrons and SNR is about 20 . Collecting four times as many photons gives 1600 electrons, about 40 electrons of shot noise, and SNR about 40 : four times the exposure gives twice the shot-noise-limited SNR. At very low signal, read noise matters more. With 25 photoelectrons and 5 electrons rms read noise, shot noise and read noise each have variance 25 , so total noise is about 7.1 electrons and SNR is only about 3.5 .

The same model explains why a single long exposure can outperform several very short frames at equal total integration time when read noise is important. Four frames each collecting 25 electrons contribute 100 electrons of total signal, but incur four readout-noise variances. With 5 electrons read noise per frame, the combined standard deviation before averaging is 4 ( 25 + 25 ) ≈ 14.1 electrons, giving SNR about 7.1 . One exposure collecting the same 100 electrons has variance approximately 100 + 25 = 125 , standard deviation about 11.2 , and SNR about 8.9 . The four-frame method may still be preferable when a long exposure would smear motion, saturate a highlight, or be impractical; modern burst processing also rejects moving objects and defective pixels. The best capture strategy follows the scene’s motion and brightness distribution as well as the sensor noise budget. ISO should be interpreted alongside the pixel’s full-well capacity and the ADC. Full well describes the maximum charge a photosite can hold before saturation. Analog gain multiplies the voltage before digitization, while digital gain multiplies values after the ADC. Raising analog ISO can make a shadow signal occupy more ADC codes and can reduce the relative influence of some downstream electronic noise, but the sensor still received the same photons. If a bright highlight already filled the well, gain cannot restore its missing gradation. A low ISO can preserve highlight headroom in some cameras but may expose shadows to more readout noise; actual sensor response differs, so ISO behavior should be understood as a capture-pipeline property rather than a universal law. RAW data preserve per-photosite measurements before the camera has fully reconstructed color and applied a display tone curve. “RAW” does not mean untouched photons: analog gain, black-level subtraction, defect correction, and packing may already have occurred. A raw converter still must infer color at each location, scale channels for white balance, map camera responses into a working color space, manage highlights, denoise, sharpen, and encode an output. If a raw channel reaches its full-well or ADC limit, later white balance cannot recover that clipped channel; clipped color may appear as a flat patch or a hue shift. JPEG processing makes many of these choices in camera and then quantizes and compresses the rendered image. A raw file offers more control over these decisions, but it cannot undo a missed focus, motion blur, insufficient photon count, or missing spectral illumination. Equivalent exposures preserve brightness but not the photograph

An exposure value can be shifted among aperture, shutter time, and gain to keep a similar rendered brightness. Consider a baseline image made at 1 / 125 s, f / 8 , ISO 100. Opening one stop to f / 5.6 admits about twice as much light per unit time. To preserve the same sensor exposure, halve the exposure time to approximately 1 / 250 s. The resulting average brightness may be similar, but the image is not equivalent in every sense: f / 5.6 gives shallower depth of field, while 1 / 250 s freezes motion more strongly. The aperture-shutter pair changes what spatial and temporal detail the photograph contains.

A similar trade is to raise ISO by one stop and shorten the shutter by one stop. The rendered brightness can remain similar, but the shorter exposure collects about half as many photons. Stops, EV, and scene ratios

Photographic exposure often uses stops because the eye and practical settings respond naturally to ratios. One stop is a factor of two in exposure; a change of three stops is a factor of eight. An exposure value can be written, for ISO 100, as

E V = log 2 ⁡ ( N 2 t ) ,

where N is f-number and t is shutter time in seconds. If aperture is fixed, doubling shutter time lowers EV by one; if shutter time is fixed, moving from f / 4 to approximately f / 5.6 raises EV by one stop because the aperture area halves. The formula is a compact exposure equivalence, not a statement that scenes should be rendered at a particular brightness. Metering and artistic placement decide how a measured scene maps to tone. Meter readings are estimates, not scene knowledge

A reflected-light meter measures light arriving from the scene and cannot know whether a large measured region is white snow, a black coat, or a neutral wall. A simple meter therefore places the measured brightness near a reference middle tone; an 18-percent gray card is a familiar reference, while actual meter calibration constants depend on the device and standard. If a snowfield fills the frame, the automatic exposure may darken it toward gray; a dark subject may be brightened. Exposure compensation tells the camera to shift this placement. Cameras, projection, and calibration

A camera maps 3-D structure into measurements, and calibration or pose recovery asks which scene parameters those measurements constrain.

Rotation and projective cameras

A 2D rotation through angle θ is

R ( θ ) = [ cos ⁡ θ − sin ⁡ θ sin ⁡ θ cos ⁡ θ ] .

Its columns are orthonormal, so its inverse is its transpose. Three-dimensional orientation has three degrees of freedom, but Euler-angle coordinates suffer from gimbal lock when axes align. Axis-angle specifies a unit axis and a rotation amount. Quaternions encode a rotation with four constrained values and avoid Euler singularities. A unit quaternion for axis a and angle θ is q = ( cos ⁡ ( θ / 2 ) , a sin ⁡ ( θ / 2 ) ) . Quaternion multiplication composes rotations; conjugation supplies the inverse for unit quaternions. Because q and − q encode the same orientation, interpolation must choose a consistent short arc on the unit sphere. Complex numbers play the corresponding role for 2D rotation: multiplying by a unit complex number rotates the plane. CMU-06 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Perspective projection maps camera-space point ( X , Y , Z ) to ( x , y ) = ( f X / Z , f Y / Z ) , where f is focal length. The division by depth makes farther objects appear smaller. Homogeneous coordinates let matrix operations happen before perspective division. A projected vertex’s attributes cannot generally be interpolated linearly in screen space: projection has already divided by depth. If screen barycentric weights are λ i and vertex clip-space denominators are w i , perspective-correct interpolation of attribute a is

a ( p ) = ∑ i λ i ( a i / w i ) ∑ i λ i ( 1 / w i ) .

This restores the correct variation across a receding surface. Without it, a checkerboard floor visibly warps toward the horizon. CMU-05 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) · CMU-07 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Perspective is linear before the divide

A pinhole camera projects by drawing a line from the camera center through a 3D point and finding where it meets the image plane. With the camera at the origin and the image plane one unit along the depth axis, a point ( x , y , z ) projects to ( x / z , y / z ) . This divide makes perspective non-linear in ordinary 3D coordinates: doubling both object size and camera distance leaves its projected size unchanged. Homogeneous coordinates store that divide as the final coordinate of a linear map. In a simplified camera aligned to the axes,

[ x y z 1 ] ⟼ [ x y z z ] ⟼ ( x z , y z , 1 ) .

The first step is matrix multiplication; the second is dehomogenization, dividing by the last coordinate. Thus the apparent non-linearity is deferred to one final divide. For example, ( 6 , 4 , 12 ) projects to ( 1 / 2 , 1 / 3 ) on the unit image plane. A point twice as far away, ( 12 , 8 , 24 ) , projects to the same pixel. Real camera matrices add orientation, focal length, principal point, and clipping conventions, but this similar-triangles argument is the core of perspective projection. Perspective projection links 3-D velocity to image flow

For a pinhole camera, a 3-D point ( X , Y , Z ) projects to the image plane as

x = f X Z , y = f Y Z .

Here Z is positive distance along the optical axis under the usual camera convention. Differentiating x with respect to time gives

x ˙ = f U Z − X W Z 2 = f Z U − x Z W ,

where U = X ˙ and W = Z ˙ are the point’s horizontal and optical-axis velocities. Similarly, with V = Y ˙ ,

y ˙ = f Z V − y Z W .

These expressions separate lateral translation from expansion or contraction due to optical-axis motion. For a rigid scene translating along the viewing direction, U = V = 0 for static world points and W < 0 while the camera approaches them. Then x ˙ = − ( x / Z ) W and y ˙ = − ( y / Z ) W : image vectors point radially away from the point where the translation axis meets the image plane. This point is the focus of expansion (FOE). It identifies the direction of translation, but perspective projection alone does not reveal absolute distance. Camera calibration from straight lines and vanishing points

Under perspective projection, parallel 3-D lines with a common direction converge to a vanishing point in the image. For a line direction d and camera projection matrix P , its vanishing point depends on P [ d ; 0 ] : translation does not affect a direction at infinity. Two or more known or mutually orthogonal directions therefore constrain camera orientation and intrinsic parameters. The practical logic is: detect image lines, estimate their intersections, associate vanishing points with world directions, and solve calibration constraints. A vanishing point is not automatically a precise calibration. Calibration is strongest when long image lines and independent 3-D directions constrain different degrees of freedom. Orthographic projection as an engineered measurement choice

Perspective projection is valuable when depth variation encodes motion, yet it is an unwanted nuisance when inspecting parts whose dimensions should not change with working distance. Compound lenses have multiple elements because chromatic aberration and other defects cannot be eliminated with one ideal thin lens. The thick-lens approximation represents the system with front and rear nodal points and principal planes separated by a signed thickness T . A negative T can fold a long effective focal length into a shorter telephoto body. Moving one nodal point far away produces telecentric projection. Object-space telecentric lenses make magnification nearly independent of object depth, which suits conveyor measurements and printed-circuit-board inspection. Their field is a cylinder rather than the usual perspective cone, so the optics must be at least as wide as the object and use substantial glass. MITV-08 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (video 01:04:10–01:13:52)

Image-space telecentricity sends rays approximately perpendicular to the sensor. This stabilizes magnification if focusing shifts the image plane and reduces the cos 4 ⁡ α illumination falloff. The geometry also suits pixel microlenses: each microlens concentrates light from a pixel’s whole area onto its photosensitive region, avoiding inactive circuitry and reducing aliasing through area integration. Oblique rays miss that intended region and can leak into circuitry. Double telecentric systems constrain both sides, at added optical cost. These details explain why industry pays for telecentric lenses when measurement repeatability matters. MITV-08 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (video 01:14:00–01:22:08)

Finally, orthographic projection can be an approximation even without a telecentric lens. If object depth Z differs little from a large mean distance Z 0 , then perspective coordinates x = f X / Z are approximately x = ( f / Z 0 ) X . The scale is nearly constant, giving a linear mapping from world X , Y to image x , y and letting image dimensions measure object dimensions without knowing each point’s exact depth. This approximation simplifies later reconstruction math; it is a deliberate limit on the scene geometry, not a different universal camera law. Interior calibration from known scene points

Interior orientation recovers camera intrinsics such as principal point ( x 0 , y 0 ) , principal distance f , and distortion. Exterior orientation recovers the calibration target’s camera-relative pose: three translations and three rotation parameters. They are solved together because the target’s exact position, rotation, and the lens’s internal projection center cannot normally be measured directly. A rectangular brick and vanishing points can estimate principal point and focal distance, but are not precise enough for high accuracy and do not conveniently model radial lens distortion. Tsai’s approach instead uses corresponding points on a calibration object whose coordinates are known. A planar lithographic target is cheap and accurate; a 3-D object gives additional depth variation but is harder to manufacture and maintain precisely. Radial distortion shifts image coordinates along rays from a distortion center. A polynomial model writes Δ x = x ( k 1 r 2 + k 2 r 4 + ⋯ ) and Δ y = y ( k 1 r 2 + k 2 r 4 + ⋯ ) , where r 2 = x 2 + y 2 in coordinates about that center. A positive or negative leading coefficient produces opposite barrel or pincushion curvature. Plumb lines—straight parallel hanging wires—provide a calibration reference because their image bending reveals radial distortion. The same problem is often called camera resection: given 3-D control points and 2-D image points, estimate the six exterior parameters. The image observations are coupled, so simple parameter counting does not make the nonlinear solve trivial. A practical strategy begins with a pose estimate from a small set of control points, then minimizes image-plane reprojection error using many observations. If measurements contain outliers, RANSAC can select consistent subsets. The lecture’s drone mapping example motivates the need: a drone captures overlapping images of terrain; a prior digital terrain model supplies control landmarks, while each image provides their pixel locations. A rough initial pose helps avoid an incorrect local minimum. MITV-22 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (video 00:00:43–00:03:52, 00:12:59–00:24:44)

The minimum three-point construction separates position from attitude. For each calibrated image observation, construct a unit camera ray. The angular separations between the three rays are known from their dot products, with a cross product supplying orientation/sign information. The three corresponding world landmarks also have known pairwise distances. The unknown ranges r 1 , r 2 , r 3 from camera center C to the landmarks obey the law of cosines; for a pair i , j with known baseline d i j and included ray angle γ i j ,

d i j 2 = r i 2 + r j 2 − 2 r i r j cos ⁡ γ i j .

Three such nonlinear equations determine the three ranges in the generic case. The center is then the intersection of the three spheres centered at the landmarks with radii r i ; the intersection can have two mirror solutions. Their physical setting (an aircraft above terrain) and the reversed cyclic order of the image points reject the below-ground mirror. Once C is known, subtract it from each landmark coordinate, normalize the three world direction vectors, and align them with the corresponding camera rays. This estimates the rotation, but simply inverting the two stacked direction matrices need not produce a perfectly orthonormal matrix in noisy data; it is an initialization that should be refined. More than three matches allow a least-squares pose fit. MITV-22 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (video 00:02:19–00:18:53)

For many overlapping views, bundle adjustment jointly estimates all camera centers and rotations, all unknown 3-D feature coordinates, and potentially interior orientation and radial distortion. Its objective is a sum of image-plane reprojection residuals: project each current 3-D feature estimate through the corresponding camera model and compare the predicted pixel with the measured pixel. The course stresses minimizing where measurement noise lives—in the image plane—rather than an arbitrary 3-D distance. Levenberg–Marquardt is a suitable nonlinear optimizer, but requires a useful starting estimate and can settle into a local minimum. A drone survey of a rarely visited caldera in the African Rift illustrates the payoff: overlapping images plus approximate flight information support a detailed terrain reconstruction. Panoramas: geometry before stitching

“Panorama” can mean a wide-format camera image, a stitched set of overlapping frames, a cylindrical or spherical capture, or simply a carefully cropped long aspect ratio. A fixed-back camera gives a planar perspective image, even at a wide field of view. A swing-lens camera rotates a lens and slit across a curved film plane, producing a cylindrical projection in which straight lines need not remain straight. A phone sweep mode often extracts a narrow strip from each frame, reducing the impact of parallax at the cost of a nonstandard horizontal projection. PHOTO-18 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

To stitch ordinary images into a coherent panorama, rotate the camera around its center of projection, approximately near the lens entrance pupil. If the camera translates instead, nearby objects shift relative to distant backgrounds; this parallax cannot be corrected by a single global warp. When rotation is correct and the scene is static, corresponding scene rays can be reprojected to a common picture plane. A planar projective transform, or homography, maps one image plane to another:

[ x ′ y ′ w ′ ] = H [ x y 1 ] , ( x o u t , y o u t ) = ( x ′ w ′ , y ′ w ′ ) .

Four or more corresponding points can estimate the eight independent parameters of H up to scale; practical systems use many feature matches and robust fitting. After warping, seams still need blending, exposure compensation, and sometimes local ghost handling. PHOTO-18 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

A single planar projection becomes unwieldy for a very wide view because edges stretch and the result may not fit a natural viewing position. A cylindrical panorama maps rays around a vertical cylinder, stores the unwrapped result as a rectangle, then reprojects to a viewable perspective when the user pans. A spherical panorama extends this to vertical directions, but a rectangular latitude-longitude map stretches near the poles; alternative projections can compress the top and bottom. A panorama is therefore not a neutral “bigger photograph.” Its projection determines which lines stay straight, where scale expands, and how the viewer experiences the field. PHOTO-18 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Composition remains relevant across formats. A panoramic image needs a subject path, visual rhythm, and a reason for its width. Extremely thin strips may document a long space yet resist reading as a single composition. Vertical panoramas can connect ground and sky when that relation matters. High-resolution gigapixel mosaics permit inspection of detail but still need a navigable overview. A useful workflow chooses the intended viewing geometry first, captures overlap around the correct pivot, then stitches and crops for the final display. Field of view, image scale, and formats

For a rectilinear lens with focal length f and sensor width w , the horizontal field of view is

θ h = 2 arctan ⁡ ( w 2 f ) .

This equation makes the interaction between format and focal length explicit. A smaller sensor with the same f records a narrower angle because its image plane samples a smaller portion of the image circle. To compare two formats at the same camera position and framing, the longer focal length on the larger format can be exchanged for a shorter focal length on the smaller format. If framing is held constant and the aperture diameter is also adjusted proportionally, depth-of-field comparisons can become similar, but photon collection and sensor noise still depend on total sensor area, transmission, exposure time, and readout. Claims that one format “has more depth of field” or “gathers more light” should state what is held fixed. From a camera view transform to viewport pixels

A 2D world window mapped to a screen viewport

Viewing starts by choosing which part of the world to show. In 2D, call the selected rectangle the window: x m i n ≤ x ≤ x m a x and y m i n ≤ y ≤ y m a x . The viewport is the destination rectangle in screen coordinates: u m i n ≤ u ≤ u m a x and v m i n ≤ v ≤ v m a x . They are both rectangles, but their coordinate units and locations differ. The mapping preserves each point’s fractional position in the source window, then scales that fraction to the viewport and offsets it by the viewport’s lower coordinate. (P1-25)

For the horizontal coordinate, the fraction of the window’s width to the right of the left edge is ( x − x m i n ) / ( x m a x − x m i n ) . Multiply it by the viewport width and add u m i n . The vertical formula is analogous:

u = u m i n + x − x m i n x m a x − x m i n ( u m a x − u m i n ) , v = v m i n + y − y m i n y m a x − y m i n ( v m a x − v m i n ) .

For a concrete mapping, take a world window [ − 5 , 5 ] × [ − 2 , 2 ] and viewport [ 100 , 900 ] × [ 50 , 650 ] . Point ( 0 , 1 ) is halfway across the window, so u = 100 + 0.5 ( 800 ) = 500 . It is three quarters of the way up the window, so v = 50 + 0.75 ( 600 ) = 500 . The world point maps to viewport coordinate ( 500 , 500 ) . If the target screen convention has y increasing downward, arrange the viewport’s y endpoints or invert the mapping deliberately; mixing conventions flips the image. (P1-25)

The same mapping is an affine transform. Define s x = ( u m a x − u m i n ) / ( x m a x − x m i n ) , s y = ( v m a x − v m i n ) / ( y m a x − y m i n ) , t x = u m i n − s x x m i n , and t y = v m i n − s y y m i n . Then u = s x x + t x , v = s y y + t y , represented with homogeneous coordinates by

[ s x 0 t x 0 s y t y 0 0 1 ] .

This 2D window-to-viewport mapping answers how a selected planar rectangle fills a screen rectangle. It is distinct from the 3D camera view transform (which changes coordinates relative to a camera) and perspective projection (which maps 3D view geometry into clip coordinates). The lecture calls the destination camera/view space while developing this rectangle map; keep the concepts separate when building the full 3D pipeline. (P1-25)

The 3D view transform moves the world around a fixed camera

A view transform expresses world coordinates from the camera’s point of view. In the OpenGL convention used in this course, view-space camera position is the origin and the camera looks down negative z. Instead of physically moving the camera in the coordinate calculation, transform the world so the camera lands at the origin. First translate every world point P by − E , where E is the camera eye position. For example, with eye E = ( 3 , 4 , 5 ) , world origin ( 0 , 0 , 0 ) becomes ( − 3 , − 4 , − 5 ) after this translation. Objects all move because they share this world-to-view operation. Then rotate the translated coordinates so camera right, up, and forward align with the view axes. This is the inverse of the camera’s world pose: translation by negative eye followed by a change of basis. (P1-26)

The camera is described by three vectors in world space: eye E is its location, target T is the point it looks toward, and up U supplies an approximate vertical direction. Build an orthonormal camera frame:

F = T − E ‖ T − E ‖ , R = F × U ‖ F × U ‖ , U ′ = R × F .

F points from eye to target. The cross product F × U gives a direction perpendicular to both, which is camera-right under this handedness; normalization gives it unit length. U ′ = R × F corrects the supplied up direction so the three axes are mutually perpendicular. This correction matters because an approximate up vector may not be exactly perpendicular to the viewing direction. If F and U are parallel, the cross product is zero and cannot define right; choose a different approximate up direction. (P1-26; the degenerate case is a mathematical consequence of the cross product.)

For column-vector points and a camera looking along negative z, put the camera basis vectors in the rows of the view rotation: right R , corrected up U ′ , and backward − F . Combine this with the translation by − E :

V = [ R x R y R z − R ⋅ E U x ′ U y ′ U z ′ − U ′ ⋅ E − F x − F y − F z F ⋅ E 0 0 0 1 ] .

The first row computes R ⋅ ( P − E ) = R ⋅ P − R ⋅ E , so it measures the point’s displacement along the camera’s right axis after the eye translation. The other rows do the same for up and backward. This also clarifies the signs: a camera-facing point lies along F , so its view-space z coordinate is negative, consistent with OpenGL looking down − z . The camera-orientation matrix is orthogonal, so its inverse is its transpose; the translation is then composed with that basis change to form the full view matrix. (P1-26)

As a small check, take E = ( 3 , 4 , 5 ) , F = ( 0 , 0 , − 1 ) , U = ( 0 , 1 , 0 ) , so R = ( 1 , 0 , 0 ) and U ′ = ( 0 , 1 , 0 ) . The matrix is

V = [ 1 0 0 − 3 0 1 0 − 4 0 0 1 − 5 0 0 0 1 ] .

It sends the eye ( 3 , 4 , 5 , 1 ) T to the view origin ( 0 , 0 , 0 , 1 ) T , and sends target ( 3 , 4 , 4 , 1 ) T to ( 0 , 0 , − 1 , 1 ) T . This numeric witness checks both the translation signs and the negative-z viewing direction. (P1-26)

The camera pose transform is not the projection. In a 3D pipeline, model transforms place objects in world space; the view matrix expresses them relative to the camera; a later projection maps view-space geometry into clip space. A view frustum selects the visible volume, clipping handles geometry crossing its boundaries, and normalized device coordinates (NDC) place the clipped result in the canonical cube (lecture states −1 to 1 on each axis) before viewport mapping to screen pixels. The 2D window-to-viewport mapping from P1-25 is a simpler rectangle-to-rectangle case; it does not replace the 3D camera transform or perspective projection. P1-26 focuses on view-space construction and defers detailed projection, screen-space work, and code. (P1-26)

A model matrix moves model-space points into world space. A view matrix expresses the world relative to the camera. A projection matrix maps view-space geometry into clip coordinates; after perspective division, normalized device coordinates are mapped to pixels by the viewport transform. Keep these roles separate: object placement, camera pose, and projection answer different questions. (P1-06–07; P1-22–26)

From a camera frustum to the image-space cube

A camera has a pose and a lens-like viewing volume. The pose says where the eye is, where it points, and which direction counts as up. The volume says what lies inside the field of view and which depths are retained. The 2009 lecture starts with a pinhole camera: rays pass through one point, so distant objects take less screen space. This is a useful model because perspective follows from a division by depth. A real lens can bend and focus rays, but this course deliberately leaves that richer optics model out. (P3-05–06)

Use two transforms for two different jobs. First, the camera transform moves the eye to the origin and rotates the camera frame into a standard orientation. In this course’s convention the camera looks down negative Z. A practical camera is specified by eye E , target T , and an approximate up direction U . Compute forward F = normalize ( T − E ) , right R = normalize ( F × U ) , then corrected up U ′ = R × F . The cross products make an orthogonal frame; the input up direction only chooses a roll preference. If U is parallel or nearly parallel to F , the cross product is zero or unstable, so choose a fallback up direction. The lecture’s “center of attention” is simply T ; subtracting E from T gives the viewing direction. (P3-06)

The second operation maps a truncated pyramid (the frustum) to a canonical cube. Let the vertical field of view be α , near distance n , and far distance f , with 0 < n < f . At the near plane, the top edge is y = n tan ⁡ ( α / 2 ) ; this is the right-triangle link between the angle and the viewport. A point at camera depth d = − z projects to y s c r e e n ∝ y / d , so twice as far away means half the projected height. This reciprocal-depth effect is exactly what homogeneous coordinates provide: place − z in the output W coordinate, then divide x, y and z by W.

For row points multiplied on the right, the lecture’s perspective matrix (square viewport, camera looks down − Z ) is

V = [ cot ⁡ ( α / 2 ) 0 0 0 0 cot ⁡ ( α / 2 ) 0 0 0 0 f + n f − n − 1 0 0 2 f n f − n 0 ] .

For Q = ( x , y , z , 1 ) this gives Q V = ( x cot ⁡ ( α / 2 ) ,   y cot ⁡ ( α / 2 ) ,   z f + n f − n + 2 f n f − n ,   − z ) . Dividing by W makes the first two coordinates inversely proportional to depth. The depth coefficients are chosen by boundary conditions, not guesswork: set z = − n and require the divided depth to be + 1 ; set z = − f and require it to be − 1 . Solving those two linear equations yields the fractions in the matrix. This near-to- + 1 , far-to- − 1 convention is the lecture’s image-space convention. A modern API may use another clip-depth range or reverse-Z; use that API’s documented convention rather than copying these signs blindly. With a non-square viewport, use a separate horizontal scale. For vertical field of view and aspect ratio a = width / height , the horizontal scale is the vertical scale divided by a .

A numerical check makes the divide visible. Take n = 1 , f = 10 , α = 90 ∘ , and point ( 1 , 0 , − 2 ) . Since cot ⁡ 45 ∘ = 1 , the projected homogeneous point is ( 1 , 0 , − 2 / 9 , 2 ) . Divide by W=2: the image-space point is ( 0.5 , 0 , − 1 / 9 ) . At depth 2, x has halved; the near and far planes still map exactly to +1 and −1. A cube’s nearer face therefore appears larger than its farther face. (P3-05)

In the lecture’s row-vector notation, first orient/translate the camera, then apply projection: Q ′ = Q C V . Matrix order matters because the camera must be put in its canonical pose before the frustum is projected. This order is the transpose/reverse-composition counterpart of the column-vector convention used elsewhere in the teaching notes. The course calls the frustum-to-cube stage the “viewing transform”; many current APIs call it the projection matrix, reserving “view” for the camera pose. Keep the names and operation boundaries explicit when reading older OpenGL code. gluLookAt and gluPerspective were convenience calls in the 2009 fixed-function workflow; they do not replace understanding the matrices, and they are not modern core-profile shader setup. (P3-05–06, P3-08)

Clipping as signed distance and interpolation

Projection transforms every point whether it is visible or not. Clipping removes geometry outside a chosen half-space before rasterization. Define a plane by point P and normal N . For any query point Q , the signed value d ( Q ) = N ⋅ ( Q − P ) says which side it lies on. Choose N to point inward: d > 0 is inside, d < 0 is outside, and d = 0 is on the plane. Reversing N reverses the signs but not the plane. The common equation a x + b y + c z + d 0 = 0 is the same test after expanding the dot product. (P3-06–07)

Suppose a segment goes from Q 1 to Q 2 . Every point on it is Q ( t ) = Q 1 + t ( Q 2 − Q 1 ) , 0 ≤ t ≤ 1 . Let d 1 = N ⋅ ( Q 1 − P ) and d 2 = N ⋅ ( Q 2 − P ) . At the crossing, the signed distance is zero:

0 = d 1 + t ( d 2 − d 1 ) , t = d 1 d 1 − d 2 .

For example, clip against the plane x = 0 with inside x ≥ 0 . If Q 1 = ( − 2 , 1 , 0 ) and Q 2 = ( 4 , 1 , 0 ) , then d 1 = − 2 , d 2 = 4 , and t = 1 / 3 . The intersection is ( − 2 , 1 , 0 ) + ( 1 / 3 ) ( 6 , 0 , 0 ) = ( 0 , 1 , 0 ) . The retained segment is the intersection-to- Q 2 part; the rejected part is Q 1 to the intersection. If an endpoint already lies on the plane, the formula returns t = 0 or t = 1 , a useful sanity check. (P3-06)

For a convex polygon, walk its edges in order and classify each endpoint. Keep inside vertices in an inside list and outside vertices in an outside list. When an edge crosses the plane, compute the intersection once and add it to both lists. The in-list remains an ordered polygon boundary; the out-list is the complementary clipped piece for this one plane. To clip to a frustum, repeat the operation plane by plane, feeding each surviving polygon to the next plane. Near/far plus the four side planes form six frustum boundaries. Exact floating-point equality to zero is rare; use a small tolerance and handle points near the boundary consistently so adjacent triangles do not develop cracks. Concave polygons can split into disconnected regions, so first triangulate or decompose them into convex pieces. Triangles are planar by construction; arbitrary polygon vertices must be coplanar for this geometry model. (P3-06)

The lecture’s historical OpenGL pipeline delays clipping until homogeneous clip coordinates because the six canonical comparisons are cheap. For the conventional OpenGL clip cube, a clip point must satisfy − W ≤ X , Y , Z ≤ W before perspective division; this rejects points behind the eye as well as outside the frustum. The lecture describes a preliminary W = 0 test and then the six cube-plane tests, effectively counting seven operations. Treat that as a description of its implementation story, not an API-independent rule: clip-depth conventions differ, and homogeneous clipping should be done before dividing by W. After division, clipping the normalized cube is mathematically simple but the geometry must already have been handled correctly around the eye plane. (P3-06–07)

Clipping is also a modeling tool. The instructor recounts cutting a strip of polygonal text with a plane, rotating the retained part, and repeating the cut so the text appears to wrap around a building. The same signed-distance test underlies both view-volume rejection and deliberate geometric slicing. (P3-07)

Sampling continuous signals and image formation

Finite image samples stand in for continuous signals and areas; aliasing appears when the sampling grid cannot represent the variation being measured.

Frequency, alias, and reconstruction

A sinusoid f ( x ) = cos ⁡ ( 2 π ν x ) has frequency ν cycles per unit distance. Sampling every Δ x units yields sample rate f s = 1 / Δ x . If frequency exceeds f s / 2 , the sampled sequence can be indistinguishable from a lower-frequency wave. For instance, samples of a sinusoid at exactly integer cycles per sample may all equal 1, making an oscillating signal look constant. This fold-over is aliasing. In images, fine stripes can appear as broad bands or change pattern as the camera shifts.

The sample grid is not the only ingredient; reconstruction determines what continuous image is inferred between samples. Nearest-neighbor reconstruction uses a piecewise constant basis. Bilinear reconstruction uses tent-shaped basis functions in two dimensions. A box filter integrates uniformly over a footprint. A Gaussian filter weights nearby values smoothly, reducing high frequencies but blurring detail. Sharper kernels preserve edges but can introduce ringing near discontinuities. Filter design is a tradeoff among blur, ringing, and alias suppression.

Convolution is a sliding weighted average. For image signal I and kernel h , filtered image is ( I ∗ h ) ( x ) = ∫ I ( y ) h ( x − y ) d y . The kernel’s frequency response states how strongly each sinusoidal frequency survives. A low-pass filter attenuates high frequencies before samples are reduced. Without it, decimation discards samples and aliases unresolved detail. Mipmaps approximate this operation at a sequence of scales. The scale chosen should match the footprint of a pixel mapped into texture space; anisotropic filtering models elongated footprints more accurately than one scalar level. Raster sampling of a blurred signal

A blurred high-resolution photograph can be downsampled more reliably than a sharp checker pattern because optical blur attenuates high spatial frequencies before the pixel grid records them. This is the same prefiltering principle used by texture mipmaps and pixel reconstruction: remove detail the target grid cannot represent before reducing resolution. Sampling and aliasing: pixels measure areas, not mathematical points

A continuous image has detail at arbitrarily high spatial frequencies. A pixel is not an infinitely small point detector; ideally it measures an average over an area. If a renderer takes only one point sample per pixel, fine stripes or edges may be mistaken for lower-frequency patterns. This is aliasing: different continuous signals become indistinguishable after insufficient sampling. The familiar symptom is a jagged diagonal or a distant brick pattern that shimmers as the camera moves. The antialiasing lecture motivates the problem through sampled images and high-frequency signals, introduces Fourier-frequency reasoning, and discusses sample placement and reconstruction MITG-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:05:00–00:20:00; 00:25:00–00:55:00; 01:10:00–01:20:00).

The Nyquist principle says a band-limited signal must be sampled at more than twice its maximum frequency to avoid frequency overlap. Real images are not perfectly band-limited, so graphics filters first suppress frequencies that would exceed the pixel grid’s capacity, then sample. Supersampling estimates a pixel’s footprint with several subpixel rays and averages them. Jittering samples can reduce structured artifacts, but random noise is still error. A reconstruction filter combines samples to form the final pixel; a broad filter can blur detail, while a narrow filter can leave aliasing. Antialiasing is thus a balance between removing unrepresentable frequencies and preserving visible sharpness.

Mipmaps store progressively downsampled texture levels. If a surface projects to a small screen footprint, the renderer samples a lower-resolution level rather than querying a very detailed image and hoping interpolation repairs aliasing. Trilinear filtering blends between two levels; anisotropic filtering handles elongated footprints better than a single isotropic level. The rasterization-II lecture revisits mip-level choice and texture filtering in the context of projected pixels MITG-18 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:45:00–01:10:00). Sampling is a shared problem in ray tracing, rasterization, textures, shadows, and display; each is a different place where continuous signals meet a finite grid.

Sampling and reconstruction

A continuous image contains spatial variation at many scales. Fourier analysis represents that variation as a weighted sum of spatial sinusoids. Low spatial frequencies describe broad changes in illumination and shape; high frequencies describe fine edges and texture. A lens and sensor have a modulation transfer function (MTF): the fraction of contrast preserved at each spatial frequency. Resolution is not a single threshold independent of contrast. A system may preserve coarse contrast strongly while attenuating fine patterns until they disappear.

Sampling records the image at discrete sensor locations. If a repeating pattern has a spatial frequency above half the sampling frequency, the Nyquist condition is violated and the pattern can alias into a false lower-frequency pattern. Moiré is a visible beat pattern produced by interference between scene structure and the sensor grid. Temporal aliasing is the same issue across time: a rotating wheel may appear stopped or reversed if sampled too slowly. Optical blur or a low-pass filter can suppress frequencies that the sensor cannot represent. Reconstruction then estimates a continuous-looking image from the samples. Nearest-neighbor, bilinear, bicubic, and more sophisticated filters trade sharpness, ringing, and computational cost. PHOTO-07 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) · PHOTO-08 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Sampling is not quantization. Sampling chooses where measurements are taken; quantization chooses which discrete amplitude code represents each measurement. Dithering adds controlled noise or pattern before quantization so that average tonal structure survives with fewer levels. Newspaper halftoning achieves a related effect spatially: varying the size or density of black dots makes an image appear gray from a normal viewing distance. The same logic joins nineteenth-century photoengraving and modern digital printing: a continuous tonal image must be translated into marks that a reproduction process can produce. Pixel count, storage, and bandwidth

A raster is stored samples, not tiny squares

A raster image is a rectangular grid of samples called pixels. A pixel stores a color value; it does not directly store a tiny physical square. If a raster has width W and height H, it stores WH samples. With three 8-bit RGB channels, each pixel uses 24 bits = 3 bytes, so an uncompressed pixel array needs 3WH bytes before row padding, headers, metadata, or compression. For 4000 by 3000 pixels, that is 12,000,000 pixels and 36,000,000 bytes (about 34.3 MiB). A compressed JPEG may be much smaller, but its file size depends on image content and quality settings. A grayscale 8-bit image uses WH bytes; a 1-bit monochrome image ideally uses WH/8 bytes, apart from padding and file overhead. These are storage calculations, not predictions of perceptual quality. P1-05 works through a 1,365×612 cropped Polyfield frame: 1,365×612 = 835,380 pixel samples. At 60 frames per second that is about 50,122,800 samples each second. For RGB8, the raw pixel payload alone is 835,380×3 = 2,506,140 bytes per frame; a compressed image file can be much smaller, while metadata and headers add file bytes. The lecture inconsistently says 1,635×612 later in the same example; that product is 1,000,620, so use the stated 1,365×612 dimensions for the 835,380 figure. (P1-05; P1-06; P1-03–04)

An animation is a sequence of images shown over time. Resolution controls spatial sampling; frame rate controls temporal sampling. More samples can represent finer spatial or temporal variation, but they also increase bandwidth and processing costs. A displayed image is therefore the end of a chain that begins with numeric data and ends with a perceptual signal. The pixel count is width×height; aspect ratio is width/height. A 16:9, 4:3, or 20:9 display has a different shape even if its pixel count matches another display. Frame rate counts images per second, so resolution and frame rate together determine how many sample colors must be produced and transmitted. This is why selecting 144p, 360p, or 4K video changes both visual detail and data cost. (P1-05; P1-03–07)
