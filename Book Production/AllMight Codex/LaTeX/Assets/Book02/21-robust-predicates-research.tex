\section*{Research reconstruction: when the sign of a triangle is a theorem}
\noindent\textbf{Primary lineage:} Jonathan Richard Shewchuk,
\emph{Adaptive Precision Floating-Point Arithmetic and Fast Robust
Geometric Predicates}, 1997.
\href{https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~quake/robust.html}{Author's predicate sources
and explanatory page}. This laboratory reproduces the \emph{decision
problem}, error-filter idea and exact sign contract independently.
It does \textbf{not} implement or benchmark Shewchuk's original
floating-point expansion-arithmetic implementation.

\paragraph{What the predicate decides.}
Three planar points \(a,b,c\) define the oriented area of a triangle.
Twice its signed area is
\[
D=(a_x-c_x)(b_y-c_y)-(a_y-c_y)(b_x-c_x).
\]
When \(D>0\), the order \(a,b,c\) is counterclockwise; \(D<0\)
is clockwise, and \(D=0\) means exact collinearity. This sign is a
\emph{topological decision}: a wrong answer can change an algorithm's
face ownership, edge intersection, polygon decomposition or
triangulation, not merely make a drawn line a little blurry.

\paragraph{Where finite arithmetic changes the question.}
Computers evaluate the expression using binary floating-point numbers.
Two rounded products may be almost equal even when the exact determinant
of the stored inputs is nonzero. Their subtraction can then lose
significant information. For extremely large coordinates, the
products can overflow independently and a naive calculation of
\(\infty-\infty\) becomes a NaN. A predicate must therefore distinguish
a reliable sign, an uncertain sign, a genuinely zero determinant and
invalid input.

Let \(L=(a_x-c_x)(b_y-c_y)\) and
\(R=(a_y-c_y)(b_x-c_x)\). A \emph{filtered predicate} computes
an inexpensive approximation \(d=L-R\), estimates a conservative
absolute error \(E\) from arithmetic and operand magnitudes,
and accepts the sign only if \(|d|>E\). Otherwise it switches
to a more exact computation. The complete proof of a certified
filter depends on the exact floating-point model, operation ordering,
underflow, overflow and error-bound derivation. Choosing an arbitrary
epsilon is \emph{not} such a proof.

\paragraph{Our teaching implementation.}
The companion \nolinkurl{tools/labs/book02_papers_geometry.py} first
uses a deliberately conservative magnitude-based float filter.
When that result is uncertain, it converts each original binary64
coordinate to an exact rational number and evaluates the determinant
with rational operations. This gives the exact orientation of the
\emph{stored binary64 inputs}. It does not reconstruct a lost decimal
digit from an already rounded measurement, and it is slower than
Shewchuk's adaptive expansions.

For a concrete adversarial witness choose \(a=(0,0)\),
\(b=(M,M)\), \(c=(0.9M,\operatorname{nextafter}(0.9M,0))\),
where \(M=10^{308}\) is a finite binary64 number.
The exact stored-coordinate determinant is negative because
\(c_y<b_y\); a naive float determinant can become NaN through
intermediate overflow. The rational fallback still produces
the correct sign. The sign itself is the ground truth;
claiming a reliable finite approximate determinant is unnecessary.

\paragraph{Tests that are stronger than a single picture.}
Check order reversal
\(\operatorname{orient}(a,b,c)=-\operatorname{orient}(a,c,b)\);
translation invariance when translated coordinates are exactly
representable; exact collinearity; nearly collinear representable
points; enormous coordinates; and explicit rejection of NaNs and
infinities. Distinguish \emph{wrong arithmetic}, \emph{wrong input
model} and \emph{wrong geometry convention} in the laboratory log.

\paragraph{What remains for an exact reproduction.}
Reimplement or integrate the expansion primitives from the original
paper with licensing and attribution, validate all rounding
preconditions across compilers/architectures, compare all predicate
families (orientation, incircle and 3D), and run the paper's data and
performance protocol. None of those additional achievements is
implied by this small, independently authored exact-sign witness.


\begin{booktwolab}[Predicate certification]
\paragraph{C++23 checkpoint: distinguish exact, certified, and unknown.}
The native experiment \texttt{book02\_workbench orientation} returns
an integer-grid sign and a binary64 filtered sign. These are distinct
contracts. For coordinates bounded in magnitude by \(10^9\), integer
differences have magnitude at most \(2\times10^9\) and the signed
two-product determinant has absolute value at most \(8\times10^{18}\);
a signed 64-bit integer can represent this result exactly. The
implementation checks that bound before multiplying. This is not
a promise of exact arithmetic for arbitrary 32-bit coordinates.

For floating-point coordinates, form
\[
 p=(a_x-c_x)(b_y-c_y),\quad
 q=(a_y-c_y)(b_x-c_x),\quad
 D=p-q.
\]
A sufficiently large signed determinant is easy to classify. A
near-cancellation, overflow, underflow, or nonfinite intermediate
invalidates that simple conclusion. The C++23 filter therefore
returns an explicit \emph{uncertain} state; it must not pretend that
uncertainty means collinearity. A complete production predicate needs
an adaptive exact-sign fallback for the intended input domain.

Check the following metamorphic relations: swapping two vertices
reverses a nonzero sign; adding the same exactly representable grid
translation to every vertex preserves the sign; exact collinearity
remains zero under translation within the supported grid; a coordinate
outside the documented range is rejected. Test the special case of
three exactly collinear floating-point positions separately: the
filter should remain uncertain, because its role is certification,
not guessing a zero.

\end{booktwolab}

\paragraph{Why the contract matters visually.}
A mistaken orientation decision can reverse a triangle's winding,
break a mesh adjacency rule, or leave a crack along a raster boundary.
Reporting the error state is part of geometry correctness, not an
optional debugging convenience.

\begin{booktwoproof}[From a floating-point number to an exact integer]
Every finite IEEE-754 binary64 number is a \emph{dyadic rational}:
\[
 x=(-1)^s\,m\,2^e,\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}.
\]
The unsigned fraction field is \(f\) and the biased exponent is \(E\).
For normal values, \(m=2^{52}+f\) and
\(e=E-1023-52\). For subnormal nonzero values,
\(m=f\) and \(e=-1074\). Either signed zero has \(m=0\).
This is an \emph{exact statement about stored bits}, not an
approximation to a decimal measurement.

For two dyadics \(u=m_u2^{e_u}\) and
\(v=m_v2^{e_v}\), let \(e_0=\min(e_u,e_v)\).
Then their difference is the integer
\[
 u-v=\left(m_u2^{e_u-e_0}-m_v2^{e_v-e_0}\right)2^{e_0},
\]
with signed integer mantissas as needed. A product multiplies
the integer mantissas and adds their powers of two. Thus
the orientation determinant can be evaluated with
\emph{integer addition, subtraction, shifts and multiplication}
without ever rounding the intermediate geometry.

The C++23 \texttt{src/exact\_predicates.hpp} implements
this derivation using educational variable-length unsigned
base-\(2^{32}\) limbs and a signed dyadic wrapper.
The result is the \emph{exact sign of the determinant of
the six supplied finite binary64 coordinates}. There is
no fixed \(\pm10^9\) grid restriction: subnormals and the
largest finite binary64 inputs are within its stated domain.
The program rejects infinities and NaNs rather than
inventing a winding.
\end{booktwoproof}

\begin{booktwolab}[Exact C++23 orientation versus an approximate filter]
Run \texttt{book02\_workbench orientation-exact} for
six declared geometric cases, then
\texttt{book02\_workbench orientation-audit} for
those cases and 500 additional bit-pattern-generated,
finite binary64 triples. Coordinates are exported in
lossless hexadecimal floating-point notation. The
\emph{computed} exact sign is independent of the
approximate filter result.

The separate verifier \texttt{predicate\_oracle.py}
reconstructs each hexadecimal value as Python's exact
rational \texttt{Fraction} and independently evaluates
the determinant. Python is the \emph{reference checker
only}: the geometry algorithm and its CSV generation
are C++23. Verify every sign, not merely the plotted
winding of one large triangle.

Three important cases are
\[
\begin{aligned}
a&=(0,0),&b&=(1,\epsilon),&
c&=(2,\operatorname{nextafter}(2\epsilon,+\infty));\\
a&=(0,0),&b&=(\eta,0),&
c&=(0,\eta);\\
a&=(M,M),&b&=(-M,M),&
c&=(0,-M).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(\epsilon=2^{-52}\), \(\eta=2^{-1074}\)
is the smallest positive subnormal, and
\(M\) is the largest finite binary64 value.
The exact signs of these stored triangles are positive.
The first has nearly cancelling terms; the second has
an area smaller than a representable nonzero binary64;
the last has differences/products that can overflow
an ordinary floating-point determinant.
Their mathematical signs remain well-defined.
\end{booktwolab}

\begin{booktwopitfall}[Exact input bits are not exact measurements]
Even a perfectly exact arithmetic oracle cannot restore digits
lost when a decimal input, camera measurement or geometric
construction was rounded to binary64 before the call.
The sign is exact \emph{for those six stored numbers}.
Furthermore, a small empirical filter threshold is not,
by itself, a formally certified error bound for all
IEEE-754 inputs. In this educational comparison the
standalone exact oracle is authoritative; the older
approximate filter is displayed for diagnostics, not
trusted as the final sign on every input.

This simple variable-length integer implementation is
not an adaptive, SIMD or production-speed replacement
for Shewchuk's expansion arithmetic, and its memory/time
behavior has not been benchmarked. Next design exercises:
compare with a formally bounded adaptive filter,
implement robust incircle tests, and use exact orientation
to protect triangle winding and mesh adjacency decisions.
\end{booktwopitfall}
