\section*{From probability to pixels: when a triangle covers a sample}
\noindent Imagine a triangle whose vertices have already been transformed
through object, world, camera, and projection coordinates. The renderer
still has a question to answer: which pixels can this triangle affect?
That sounds visual, but it is built from the mathematics of this volume.
Coordinate transformations determine the vertices, an orientation
predicate decides which side of an edge a point belongs to, polygon
intersection determines exact continuous coverage, and probability
predicts the error when the coverage is estimated from samples.

The word \emph{coverage} has several different meanings. For an ideal
unit-area pixel \(P=[0,1]\times[0,1]\) and projected triangle \(T\),
the \emph{continuous area fraction} is
\[
c=\frac{\operatorname{area}(T\cap P)}
        {\operatorname{area}(P)}.
\]
This number belongs to \([0,1]\). It is an area ratio, not the
integer number of sample points or the result of depth testing.
A single point sample gives either zero or one. An array of
subpixel samples yields a discrete estimate. The physical image
produced by a camera and the finite pixel-filter response are
different again: a real pixel integrates radiance through an
optical/sensor response, not merely triangle area.
Before writing a rasterizer we must say which of these quantities
the implementation promises.

\paragraph{First: an edge test is a signed area.}
For \(A=(a_x,a_y)\), \(B=(b_x,b_y)\) and a candidate point \(Q\),
define
\[
E_{AB}(Q)
=(b_x-a_x)(q_y-a_y)-(b_y-a_y)(q_x-a_x).
\]
This determinant is twice the oriented area of triangle \(ABQ\).
With counterclockwise triangle \(ABC\), a point lies inside or on
the boundary precisely when
\[
E_{AB}(Q)\ge0,\quad
E_{BC}(Q)\ge0,\quad
E_{CA}(Q)\ge0.
\]
Reverse the triangle orientation and the inequalities reverse.
This explains why the robust orientation lesson earlier in this
book was not an isolated computational-geometry trick: an
unreliable sign can change pixel membership, triangle adjacency
or apparent gaps. A production triangle rasterizer also needs
an \emph{edge-ownership} convention so two adjacent triangles do
not both claim the exact same shared-boundary sample. The
inclusive test shown here is sufficient for continuous area of
a single triangle because a line segment has zero area.
It is not, by itself, a complete top-left fill-rule implementation.

\paragraph{Second: clip the polygon before computing its area.}
Choose a small, exactly representable triangle in pixel units:
\[
A=(-\tfrac14,\tfrac14),\quad
B=(\tfrac54,\tfrac14),\quad
C=(\tfrac12,\tfrac54).
\]
The triangle crosses the left, right and top sides of the pixel,
so returning the triangle's original area is incorrect.
Intersect the triangle against the four halfplanes
\(x\ge0\), \(x\le1\), \(y\ge0\), \(y\le1\).
The resulting polygon vertices, in boundary order, are
\[
\begin{aligned}
&(\tfrac5{16},1),\quad (0,\tfrac7{12}),\quad
(0,\tfrac14),\\
&(1,\tfrac14),\quad (1,\tfrac7{12}),\quad
(\tfrac{11}{16},1).
\end{aligned}
\]
A reader can verify each intersection by parameterizing the
corresponding triangle edge. Clipping against an axis-aligned
halfplane does not require an approximate floating-point
intersection: for these rational input coordinates every
intersection remains rational.

Now compute the area \emph{twice}, using independent reasoning.
From \(y=\tfrac14\) to \(y=\tfrac7{12}\), the covered horizontal
width is exactly one. That rectangular contribution is
\(\tfrac7{12}-\tfrac14=\tfrac13\).
Above \(y=\tfrac7{12}\), the left and right boundaries are
\[
x_L(y)=\tfrac34y-\tfrac7{16},\qquad
x_R(y)=\tfrac{23}{16}-\tfrac34y.
\]
Their separation is
\[
w(y)=x_R-x_L=\tfrac{15}{8}-\tfrac32y.
\]
It drops linearly from width one at \(y=\tfrac7{12}\) to
width \(\tfrac38\) at \(y=1\). The upper piece is a trapezoid
with height \(\tfrac5{12}\) and area
\[
A_{\rm upper}
=\tfrac12(1+\tfrac38)\tfrac5{12}
=\tfrac{55}{192}.
\]
The exact pixel coverage is therefore
\[
\boxed{c=\tfrac13+\tfrac{55}{192}
=\tfrac{119}{192}\approx0.6197916667.}
\]
The shoelace formula applied to the six clipped vertices
produces the same rational number. This independent calculation
is an \emph{oracle}: a test compares two algorithms/derivations,
not one implementation with an answer copied out of itself.
The program \nolinkurl{tools/labs/book02_coverage_oracle.py}
implements rational Sutherland--Hodgman clipping and the
shoelace calculation. Its tests pin all six polygon vertices
and the fraction \(119/192\), check triangle-winding
invariance, and deliberately submit invalid triangles.

\paragraph{Third: sampling estimates area under explicit assumptions.}
Draw \(N\) independent samples \(Q_i\) uniformly over the square
and define \(H_i=1\) if \(Q_i\in T\) and \(H_i=0\) otherwise.
The estimator is
\[
\widehat c_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^NH_i.
\]
Since \(Q_i\) is uniform, \(\mathbb E[H_i]=c\), so
\(\mathbb E[\widehat c_N]=c\).
For independent samples,
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat c_N)
=\frac{c(1-c)}{N}.
\]
In our exact geometry,
\(c(1-c)=8687/36864\).
At \(N=256\), the variance is approximately
\(0.00092051\) and the standard deviation is about
\(0.03034\) in coverage-fraction units. This is a prediction
for \emph{independent uniform random subpixel samples}.
It is not automatically the variance of an MSAA sample pattern,
a stratified or low-discrepancy sequence, reused samples
across neighboring pixels, or a temporally accumulated image.

The deterministic pseudorandom seed in the companion program
makes one experiment reproducible. It does not make a single
sequence statistically representative. We therefore run
distinct seeded replications, record the sample count and
independent replication unit, then compare the measured
sample variance against the declared theoretical model.
If empirical variance differs from prediction, inspect the
sample generator, independence assumptions, estimator
implementation and available replication count before
declaring either the software or theorem wrong.

\paragraph{Fourth: what a renderer must still decide.}
The exact area fraction says nothing about triangle depth,
perspective-correct interpolation of vertex attributes,
shading of the covered surface, alpha blending, or whether
two surfaces occlude one another. In perspective rendering,
clip-space coordinates must first satisfy the camera's
homogeneous clipping rules. Dividing by \(w\) before the
appropriate clipping step can create unbounded or sign-flipped
screen coordinates. A conventional hardware rasterizer usually
evaluates edge functions at fixed sample locations and applies
a specified fill convention; it is not required to compute
the rational polygon-overlap area used as our analytic reference.
The exact rational algorithm also becomes computationally
expensive for arbitrary high-precision data and is not proposed
as a GPU inner-loop implementation.

If the triangle appears jagged, one must distinguish genuine
geometric discontinuity, finite sampling, a deficient sample
filter, precision loss, and incorrect edge ownership. All five
can look similar in a screenshot but require different repairs.
Likewise, a filter that smooths the final picture may hide,
rather than correct, missing or multiply-owned coverage.

\paragraph{Reader reconstruction and negative tests.}
First derive the six clipped polygon vertices by hand and
reconstruct \(119/192\) using both horizontal integration
and shoelace area. Reverse triangle winding and prove that
the unsigned clipped area remains unchanged. Then compare
the 256-sample estimates over at least 40 independent seeds;
report the empirical mean, variance, and theoretical
Bernoulli variance. Draw the triangle and exact clipped
polygon, and plot the distribution of signed estimator error.

Next test an entirely outside triangle, a triangle containing
the entire pixel, an exact half-pixel triangle, a degenerate
collinear triangle, and a polygon touching only an edge.
Repeat with two triangles sharing an edge and demonstrate the
need for a consistent ownership convention at discrete
sample positions. Explicitly reject the inference that
correct continuous coverage automatically proves
crack-free pixel rasterization.

\paragraph{The research bridge.}
This one worked pixel forces together much of the mathematics
of this volume: affine coordinates, determinants, exact
predicates, convex polygon intersection, measure,
expectation, estimator variance, numerical representation,
independent verification, and experimental design. The next
graphics volume can now introduce camera clipping,
interpolation, sample filters, depth and production
rasterization without pretending that those mechanisms
were proved by an attractive triangle diagram.
