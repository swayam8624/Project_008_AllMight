Image sampling, filtering, and efficient reconstruction

Sampling fixes the measurements available; filtering reconstructs a usable image and efficient image operators make those filters practical at scale.

Animate a pixel by respecting the buffer’s coordinates and lifetime

A CPU pixel buffer is a linear sequence even when the image it represents has two dimensions. For an image with width W and height H , the pixel at integer coordinate ( x , y ) has row-major index

i = y W + x .

This formula assumes tightly packed rows and counts pixels, not bytes. The general layout question is Concepts/Matrix storage indexing and strides (Mathematics for Visual Computing, Part 1, Reference notes: Matrix storage indexing and strides). A padded image instead needs its row stride and pixel format; a four-channel byte buffer is not indexed like an array of complete pixel records. The coordinate check must come before this calculation: require 0 ≤ x < W and 0 ≤ y < H . Checking only that the final index is below W H is insufficient: an x value past the right edge can spill into the next row while still producing an in-range index. Conversely, comparing x to the image height is wrong on a non-square image. The buffer has W H slots, but horizontal and vertical coordinates have different limits.

Sampling, integral images, and the limits of a box average

Sampling can preserve a continuous signal only under a strong condition: its frequency content must be band-limited below half the sampling frequency. At the Nyquist boundary, phase can make a sinusoid’s samples all equal or alternate in ways that cannot distinguish it from other frequencies; therefore content must be strictly below f s / 2 . For sample spacing Δ , a sinusoid at frequency f 0 has the same samples as frequencies f 0 − k f s for integer k , because the phase shift per sample changes by 2 π k . Those indistinguishable alternatives are aliases. Once sampled, they have collapsed to the same numbers; no later digital filter can determine which original frequency produced them. Anti-alias filtering must happen before sampling. MITV-16 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (video 00:01:39–00:09:35)

Pixel-area integration is already a weak low-pass filter. A one-dimensional box average of width Δ , normalized to preserve constant brightness, has frequency response

H b o x ( ω ) = sin ⁡ ( ω Δ / 2 ) ω Δ / 2 .

It attenuates high frequencies only as 1 / ω and its first zero occurs at 2 π / Δ , twice the Nyquist angular frequency π / Δ . Repeating the average squares the response, improving the high-frequency decay to 1 / ω 2 but also attenuating useful passband frequencies. Each additional box average raises the sinc response to another power. This explains why coarse block averaging is computationally attractive but can still alias, especially in images with stone/table boundaries or fine stripes. The Fourier transform also imposes a boundary condition that is easy to overlook. A discrete Fourier transform treats a finite image as periodically repeated. Unless opposite borders match, the wraparound creates an artificial step, injecting a 1 / f tail into the spectrum. A smooth apodizing window can fade image edges toward zero before analysis; mirror extension avoids a brightness jump but may still introduce a slope discontinuity, with faster spectral decay. Thus a measured frequency spectrum can reveal the windowing and boundary rule as much as the natural image itself. MITV-16 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (video 00:27:24–00:31:49)

Texture mapping is filtering over a changing footprint

A texture is a function over a parameter domain, often the unit square. Each surface point maps to coordinates ( u , v ) that index the image. A surface may need multiple charts or seams because one planar parameterization cannot cover every shape without distortion. At a receding angle, adjacent screen pixels correspond to unevenly sized and shaped patches of texture. This makes texture lookup another sampling problem. CMU-07 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Nearest lookup selects one texel. Bilinear filtering blends four neighbors and smooths magnification. But when one screen pixel covers many texels, a single lookup aliases. A mipmap stores prefiltered levels at successively lower resolutions. The renderer selects a level based on the projected footprint. Trilinear filtering blends neighboring levels. Anisotropic filtering handles an elongated footprint, such as a floor receding toward the horizon; isotropic mipmapping can overblur because one square level must approximate a long thin region. A checkerboard that alternates faster than the screen can resolve should average toward gray, rather than flicker black and white as the camera moves. Filter before reducing resolution. CMU-07 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Pixel footprints and texture derivatives

Texture lookup is a mapping from screen coordinates ( x , y ) to texture coordinates ( u , v ) . Locally, derivatives ∂ u / ∂ x , ∂ v / ∂ x , ∂ u / ∂ y , and ∂ v / ∂ y describe how a pixel footprint stretches into texture space. The Jacobian of this map transforms an approximately square pixel into a parallelogram in texture coordinates. If its singular values are similar, an isotropic mip level is adequate. If one singular value is much larger, the footprint is elongated; anisotropic filtering samples along the long axis. This local Jacobian also explains why perspective changes texture density so quickly near a horizon.

Nearest filtering can preserve crisp texel blocks during magnification but flickers in minification. Bilinear filtering smooths between texels but still uses a tiny support region. Trilinear filtering first bilinearly filters two mip levels and blends between them according to a fractional level. Anisotropic methods take additional samples along the footprint. Each step spends more computation to better approximate an integral over the pixel footprint. The right filter depends on the projected scale and orientation of texture detail, not on an arbitrary preference for soft images. CMU-07 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Antialiasing is controlled filtering before and after sampling

A pixel grid samples an underlying continuous image. A ray tracer can answer a query such as “what radiance arrives at image coordinate ( x , y ) ?”, but it does not know where to ask, and unqueried detail is lost. Sampling chooses query locations; reconstruction turns those finite values into a displayed image. Quantization is a separate operation: it rounds each real-valued channel to a finite set of codes. Keeping these concepts separate helps diagnose whether an artifact comes from spatial undersampling or limited channel precision. MITG-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:25:11–00:28:52)

When a signal changes faster than the sample grid can distinguish, distinct patterns can produce the same samples. A high-frequency sinusoid sampled at regular intervals may look like a lower-frequency wave. This “alias” explains moiré in brick patterns and the apparent backward rotation of a wheel whose rotation period nearly matches the camera frame interval. At 30 frames per second, a wheel making one revolution in 29 frames moves only one frame’s worth of apparent phase between captures, so the sampled sequence can suggest slow reverse motion. Rolling-shutter cameras add row-by-row time offsets and can shear moving geometry. MITG-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:09:43–00:13:01; 00:29:00–00:30:38)

A 1D function can be treated as a vector with one component per location. Its inner product with another function is an integral: ⟨ f , g ⟩ = ∫ f ( x ) g ( x ) d x . Fourier analysis measures how strongly a signal aligns with sine and cosine waves at each frequency; the complex exponential e i ω x = cos ⁡ ( ω x ) + i sin ⁡ ( ω x ) packages both measurements. Low frequencies describe broad changes, while high frequencies describe rapid edges and fine texture. Filtering can preserve low frequencies for a blur, preserve high frequencies for edge detail, or isolate a band. MITG-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:36:36–00:50:27)

Sampling repeats a signal’s spectrum at regular frequency intervals. If the original spectrum is band-limited to maximum frequency f m a x , samples spaced at rate f s > 2 f m a x keep those shifted copies from overlapping. The threshold 2 f m a x is the Nyquist rate. With no overlap, an ideal low-pass filter can isolate one copy and invert the transform. In the spatial domain, that ideal reconstruction uses the sinc kernel sinc ( x ) = sin ⁡ ( π x ) / ( π x ) , whose infinite extent and negative lobes make exact numerical reconstruction expensive and prone to ringing. Real images contain sharp discontinuities and therefore arbitrarily high-frequency content, so practical graphics uses finite approximations and accepts a tradeoff. Supersampling renders multiple subpixel locations and combines their colors. Uniform samples averaged in a box are simple, but the box filter is not an ideal low-pass reconstruction and can leave aliasing. A finite bicubic Mitchell filter includes a useful negative lobe and often reduces visible moiré at comparable effort. Filter choice affects sharpness and ringing as well as error magnitude. For a texture tilted in perspective, one screen pixel may cover a long, narrow footprint in texture space; a square mip level can average the wrong proportions. Resolution, blur, and display

A sensor pitch of p micrometers corresponds to a one-dimensional sampling frequency of 1 / p samples per micrometer in the image plane, with Nyquist at half that frequency. But scene detail maps to sensor detail through magnification and focal length. A fine fabric weave may alias in a distant wide shot yet resolve in a close macro image; changing distance, focus, aperture, and output size changes the pattern’s relationship to the pixel grid. An optical low-pass filter blurs before sampling, while post-capture downsampling should apply an appropriate low-pass filter before decimation. Simply throwing away every other row creates new aliasing. PHOTO-07 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) · PHOTO-08 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Motion blur can be treated as a point-spread function. If a camera moves linearly during exposure, an idealized distant scene may be convolved with a line segment whose length corresponds to image displacement. A deblurring algorithm can attempt an inverse filter when blur is known, but the inverse amplifies noise where the blur transfer function is weak. Real handheld shake is two-dimensional and may vary across depth; rolling shutter makes each row’s exposure begin at a different time. Optical stabilization shifts a lens group, sensor stabilization moves the sensor, and electronic stabilization crops or warps frames. Each addresses some motion, but none recovers detail lost to severe blur or subject movement. PHOTO-10 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Pyramids trade pixel count against aliasing and feature visibility

If image width and height are each reduced by factor r , a level costs roughly r 2 N pixels for an original N -pixel image. A half-scale pyramid therefore costs N ( 1 + 1 / 4 + 1 / 16 + ⋯ ) = 4 N / 3 , only one third more pixels than a single full-resolution level, while providing progressively cheaper coarse search. Yet halving abruptly can alias high frequencies, and box-averaging 2 × 2 cells loses detail aggressively. The checkerboard (red-black) subsampling pattern keeps one of two pixel colors and forms a regular grid rotated by 45 ∘ ; its spacing grows by 2 while sample count halves. One level with that pitch change costs half the previous level, for total pyramid cost approaching 2 N . It reduces scale more gently and cycles back into alignment after another such step. SIFT is a contrasting example: it samples multiple intermediate scales within an octave because a feature may be clearest at one blur level and disappear at another. Scale should be selected by both computational cost and the stability of the visual evidence. MITV-14 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (video 01:17:34–01:25:31)

The processing pipeline reconstructs a usable image

A raw file contains minimally processed sensor values, usually before demosaicing and final color rendering. A JPEG is a rendered image: the camera has demosaiced, white-balanced, denoised, sharpened, tone-mapped, converted to a color space, quantized, and compressed. Different raw converters can produce different results because their algorithms and defaults differ. Camera metadata such as focal length, exposure, orientation, and sometimes location can help the converter choose distortion, vignetting, and chromatic-aberration corrections. Re-editing a JPEG repeatedly compounds irreversible quantization and compression; raw or a high-bit-depth intermediate preserves more room for later decisions. PHOTO-17 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Gamma-like encoding allocates code values in a way that better matches display behavior and human sensitivity. If display luminance follows approximately V 2.5 , a camera or image encoding can apply a roughly reciprocal power so that the combined system is closer to the desired response. Equal code steps in a nonlinear space approximate more uniform perceptual changes than equal steps in linear radiance, because vision is more sensitive to ratios than to absolute increments. A gamma transform is not itself a complete high-dynamic-range tone mapper. Strong global compression can wash out midtones or desaturate colors. Historical CRT and modern LCD workflows differ in where the encoding and decoding occur, but the system must still account for display characteristics and viewing conditions. HDR capture extends the measurable range. Exposure bracketing uses different shutter times to gather long exposures for shadows and short exposures for highlights. For a static scene, align the frames, scale them to a common radiometric basis, reject saturated measurements, and weight longer clean exposures more strongly because they often have better SNR. The method fails when objects move between frames, when camera shake changes alignment, or when noise makes a region appear structurally different. Alternating short- and long-exposure pixels can capture range in one frame but creates demosaicing and spatial-resolution problems. Per-pixel neutral-density filters trade away photons; non-destructive readout can measure bright pixels before saturation but does not automatically improve low-light SNR. A robust mobile strategy captures a burst of underexposed frames at the same exposure time. The shared exposure keeps the frames visually similar, making alignment easier; underexposure protects highlights; averaging reduces noise in shadows. Tone mapping compresses an HDR image for a limited display. A global curve maps every pixel of the same luminance the same way. Local tone mapping adapts to neighborhoods, often compressing broad illumination changes while retaining local edges and texture. Human vision also adapts spatially and over time; it does not view all scene luminances simultaneously with one fixed response. Artists have long used high-key and low-key organization to imply dazzling brightness or deep darkness on low-range paper. Local tone mapping can reproduce that cue by preserving local contrast while reducing global contrast, but can create halos and a cartoon-like appearance when pushed too far. A bilateral filter helps by blurring nearby pixels that are similar in intensity while respecting large intensity edges. PHOTO-17 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

The bilateral filter weights a neighbor according to both spatial distance and intensity difference. In simplified form,

I ′ ( p ) = 1 W p ∑ q ∈ N ( p ) I ( q ) w s ( ‖ p − q ‖ ) w r ( | I ( p ) − I ( q ) | ) ,

where w s decreases with spatial distance, w r decreases with tonal difference, and W p normalizes the weights. A Gaussian blur has only spatial weights and therefore crosses edges; a bilateral filter can denoise within a region while preserving the boundary. Unsharp masking sharpens by subtracting a blurred version to isolate edge detail, thresholding that detail to avoid amplifying noise, then adding a controlled amount back to the original. JPEG compression exploits spatial frequency and visual sensitivity. It converts RGB into a luma-like channel and two chroma channels, often subsampling chroma because people are more sensitive to fine luminance detail than fine color variation. The DCT itself is reversible; quantization discards detail and makes the compression lossy. Aligning repeated frames

Image alignment often begins with features: corners, textured patches, or gradients that can be matched between images. If an exposure changes, raw intensity comparisons become less reliable because the same surface can have different pixel values. If a region is saturated, its true brightness is unknown and it cannot provide a stable correspondence. Motion estimation may use optical flow to infer per-pixel displacement, but large motion, occlusion, textureless regions, rolling shutter, and noise can break it. Robust estimators reject inconsistent matches and fit a transform to the rest, but they cannot make a single transform describe a scene with strong parallax. Image processing changes pixels, neighborhoods, and exposure

An image is a sampled grid of color values, often RGB plus alpha. Alpha records foreground coverage or opacity for compositing. For foreground color C f , background C b , and opacity α ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] , the “over” operation is C = α C f + ( 1 − α ) C b . If storing premultiplied color C ~ f = α C f , the operation becomes C = C ~ f + ( 1 − α ) C b . Premultiplication avoids repeatedly multiplying RGB by alpha and reduces colored fringes when partially transparent pixels are filtered. It does not solve a bad matte: in the green-screen example, green light reflected from the screen onto a person’s shoulder makes foreground and background colors overlap, so a hard green threshold either clips the shoulder or leaves a green halo. Layers also need lighting and shadow consistency to look physically integrated. MITG-21 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (00:16:19–00:24:25; 00:20:20–00:23:16)

A pointwise filter maps each pixel independently, I ′ ( x , y ) = f ( I ( x , y ) ) . A tone curve remaps brightness values to fit a display’s limited dynamic range or to spread a crowded histogram over more output levels. Contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) computes local contrast mappings over neighborhoods and limits histogram peaks so local enhancement does not over-amplify noise. High-dynamic-range capture has the opposite limitation: a camera exposure cannot simultaneously preserve a sunlit window and dark church interior when their luminances span far more range than the sensor. Fast image operations: sampling, convolution, and integral images

Image pyramids and multiscale inspection require resampling. If high spatial frequencies are sampled below their Nyquist rate, they fold into lower frequencies as aliasing. A low-pass prefilter suppresses frequencies that cannot be represented at the coarser scale. Repeated block averaging is computationally simple, but its frequency response and warping artifacts differ from an ideal rotationally symmetric filter. The course discusses low-pass approximations, Fourier analysis, and the relationship between discrete impulses and convolution. Convolution of image I with kernel K is

A separable kernel can be applied as two one-dimensional passes, reducing work from roughly O ( w h k 2 ) to O ( w h k ) for an image of width w , height h , and a k × k kernel. Sparse differences make wide spline filters cheap

A later Cognex patent asks how to low-pass filter large images cheaply before subsampling. Direct convolution of an N -sample signal with an M -sample kernel costs roughly O ( N M ) ; large support makes repeated multiscale filtering expensive. The trick is to approximate the desired smooth kernel by a piecewise polynomial spline and apply its ( n + 1 ) st finite difference, where each spline segment has degree n . Within a segment, the ( n + 1 ) st difference is zero. Only the knots and endpoints survive, so the transformed kernel is sparse. Convolve the signal with those few nonzero coefficients, then apply ( n + 1 ) cumulative sums to reconstruct the original smoothing response. Differencing and summation are inverse operations (up to boundary/initialization conventions), and their convolution operators commute and associate, which lets the filter be rearranged into an efficient pipeline. MITV-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) (video 00:58:49–01:07:42)

For a piecewise quadratic kernel, the third difference is zero across every smooth segment and nonzero only around the joined pieces. In the lecture’s worked discrete example, four taps remain; their magnitudes are 1 , 3 , 3 , 1 with alternating signs. The expensive broad weighted sum becomes a few additions and subtractions followed by three running-sum passes. Expanding the spline support by a scale parameter S changes the tap spacing, not the tap count, so the arithmetic cost remains nearly constant as the low-pass bandwidth changes. This makes the method useful for approximating broader Gaussian-like smoothers or a practical finite approximation to the ideal sinc low-pass before downsampling. An integral image S ( x , y ) stores cumulative intensity over the rectangle from the origin through ( x , y ) :

S ( x , y ) = ∑ i ≤ x , j ≤ y I ( i , j ) .

For a rectangle with corners ( x 1 , y 1 ) and ( x 2 , y 2 ) , its pixel sum is obtained by inclusion-exclusion:

Q = S ( x 2 , y 2 ) − S ( x 1 − 1 , y 2 ) − S ( x 2 , y 1 − 1 ) + S ( x 1 − 1 , y 1 − 1 ) .

After a single O ( w h ) construction pass, each rectangular sum takes constant time. This is powerful for repeated window statistics and box-like filters, but an integral image does not make arbitrary kernels constant-time, and finite integer storage can overflow. Worked example: four-corner rectangle sum

Suppose a 4 × 4 image has values

I = [ 1 2 0 1 0 1 3 1 2 1 0 0 1 0 2 2 ] .

For the rectangle covering rows 2–3 and columns 2–3 (one-based indexing), the direct sum is 1 + 3 + 1 + 0 = 5 . The integral image’s four-corner subtraction returns the same sum: cumulative total through the lower-right corner, minus the rectangle above, minus the rectangle left, plus their overlap. The latter plus term is necessary because the upper-left region was removed twice. This inclusion-exclusion pattern generalizes to higher-dimensional summed-area tables.

An integral image makes every rectangular sum constant-time

For a one-dimensional grayscale sequence g [ i ] , define a cumulative sum G [ i ] = ∑ k ≤ i g [ k ] . Then an interval sum is a difference, ∑ k = i j g [ k ] = G [ j ] − G [ i − 1 ] , independent of interval length. In two dimensions, the summed-area table is

S ( x , y ) = ∑ i ≤ x , j ≤ y I ( i , j ) .

For a rectangle with lower-right corner ( x 2 , y 2 ) and upper-left corner ( x 1 , y 1 ) , inclusion–exclusion yields

Q = S ( x 2 , y 2 ) − S ( x 1 − 1 , y 2 ) − S ( x 2 , y 1 − 1 ) + S ( x 1 − 1 , y 1 − 1 ) .

The top and left exterior regions are subtracted, then their overlap is restored because it was subtracted twice. Derivatives and integrals are convolution operators

The impulse δ ( x ) is best understood by its sifting property, ∫ f ( ξ ) δ ( x − ξ ) d ξ = f ( x ) . It is a distribution, not an ordinary finite-valued function; a narrow unit-area box or Gaussian can approximate its action in a limiting sense. A finite-difference derivative is convolution with shifted impulses of opposite sign. For step ϵ , one sign convention is

D ϵ ( x ) = δ ( x + ϵ / 2 ) − δ ( x − ϵ / 2 ) ϵ ,

which gives a difference quotient when convolved with a signal (the exact sign depends on convolution’s kernel reversal). Convolving this kernel with itself gives a second derivative stencil proportional to [ 1 , − 2 , 1 ] / ϵ 2 . Repeated differences and cumulative sums are inverse operators when boundary conditions and initial values are handled consistently. Their Fourier responses multiply, so a first derivative contributes j ω , a second derivative contributes − ω 2 , and integration contributes the reciprocal away from zero frequency. Color perception, encoding, and display

Make every color number carry a meaning

The workshop’s lamp emits a spectrum: a distribution of energy across wavelength. RGB is a chosen three-number representation of perceived color, not a complete spectrum. Different spectra can produce the same RGB response. Such spectra are called metamers. This is why an RGB renderer cannot reproduce every spectral phenomenon merely by increasing the precision of its three channels.

Radiance measures directional light per projected area and solid angle. Solid angle measures the apparent angular size of a region in three dimensions; its unit is the steradian. Irradiance is incident power per surface area, integrated over incoming directions. The surface cosine appears because an oblique beam spreads over more surface area. With normal N , incoming radiance L i and incoming direction ω , irradiance is

E = ∫ Ω + L i ( ω ) max ( 0 , N ⋅ ω ) d ω .

Now separate a physical sum from a storage encoding. A linear value is proportional to the represented signal; adding two linear light contributions has the intended physical meaning. sRGB uses a piecewise nonlinear encoding for storage and display. Decode color textures before lighting and filtering in a linear working representation. Encode only when the destination requires it. Texture coordinates, roughness, IDs and normals are data with their own meanings; applying an sRGB decode to them changes the intended numbers.

Suppose half the sampled area is black and half is unit white. Its linear average is 0.5 . Encoding that average in sRGB produces approximately 0.735357 . Averaging the encoded endpoint values instead produces encoded 0.5 , which decodes to approximately 0.214041 . The dark result is an arithmetic error in the representation, not a material property. Exposure then scales scene-linear values; tone mapping compresses their range for a chosen display. Tone mapping each contribution before summing generally changes the result because the mapping is nonlinear.

Texture sampling is another integration problem. A distant pixel can cover many texels, which are stored texture samples. Bilinear filtering reconstructs from four nearby texels at one level; it does not average an arbitrarily large footprint. Mip levels supply prefiltered scales, while anisotropic filtering handles footprints stretched much more strongly along one direction. Normal maps add a further complication: averaging encoded normals and renormalizing loses information about variation within the footprint. At small scales that variation can behave like additional roughness. This is why normal filtering and specular antialiasing must agree with the material model.

Real light is electromagnetic radiation.

At a point, light can be described spectrally by wavelength:

Visible wavelengths occupy only part of the electromagnetic spectrum.

A physically complete renderer would therefore reason about something like

L ( λ )

Most real-time renderers do not.

They approximate spectral light with three numbers:

( R , G , B ) .

This is a drastic compression.

It works because human color vision itself approximately reduces spectral information through three cone-response families.

RGB is not light itself

That phenomenon is metamerism.

Radiance invariance along a vacuum ray

One of the most powerful properties in rendering is:

  • apparent angular size shrinks,
  • geometric projected area relationship changes,

Human color perception

The retina contains several photoreceptor classes.

For photopic color vision, three cone types dominate:

L , M , S

with different spectral response curves.

Therefore many different spectra map to the same three responses.

Hence metamers.

Color values depend on physical spectra and encoding conventions, so operations must respect measurement and display stages.

Color as spectral measurement

Light is electromagnetic radiation described by wavelength and power. A spectrum can be emitted, absorbed, reflected, or transmitted by materials. The eye’s rods and cones and a camera’s sensors respond differently across wavelengths. The perceived or recorded color is a measurement of the spectrum by those responses, not an intrinsic label attached to a surface. CMU-14 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Standard color-matching functions convert a spectrum S ( λ ) into tristimulus values:

X = ∫ S ( λ ) x ¯ ( λ ) d λ , Y = ∫ S ( λ ) y ¯ ( λ ) d λ , Z = ∫ S ( λ ) z ¯ ( λ ) d λ .

Different spectra can have the same three integrals and therefore look alike to a standard observer under those conditions. They are metamers; changing the illuminant or camera response can reveal their difference. Chromaticity removes overall intensity, with coordinates x = X / ( X + Y + Z ) and y = Y / ( X + Y + Z ) . A display gamut is the set of colors obtainable from its primaries. CIE diagrams describe relationships among visible colors, while sRGB defines a practical display encoding. CMU-14 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

RGB is convenient for displays, CMYK for subtractive printing, HSV-like models for intuitive editing, and Y’CbCr for separating luma-like and chroma channels. Chroma can often be sampled more coarsely because visual acuity for color detail is lower than for brightness detail. These models solve different tasks; none is the original spectrum. Gamma encoding stores display values nonlinearly. Physical addition, interpolation, filtering, and compositing should use linear-light values, then encode for display. Averaging encoded black and white values directly yields a darker result than averaging their physical light. CMU-14 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Additive and subtractive mixtures

An additive display emits red, green, and blue light. Increasing all three toward maximum produces white. Printing is subtractive: cyan, magenta, and yellow inks absorb portions of incident white light; combining them ideally absorbs most visible wavelengths and produces dark output. In practice, black ink is added for deeper blacks and efficient neutral printing, giving CMYK. These models describe different physical mechanisms. A color slider that looks intuitive to a designer is not necessarily a model in which arithmetic represents physical mixing. CMU-14 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Chromaticity, gamut, and color conversion

A chromaticity plot can show the visible spectral locus and the triangle spanned by a display’s primaries. Colors outside that triangle are out of gamut. Color conversion between RGB spaces is a linear matrix transform only after values are in the appropriate linear-light representation and white-point assumptions are handled. A nonlinear encoding such as sRGB applies a transfer curve after the matrix conversion.

An image can store sRGB code values for display. If two stored values c 1 , c 2 are averaged directly, the result does not represent the mean radiance unless the encoding is linear. The correct process is decode, compute in linear light, then re-encode. This applies to texture filtering, physically based light accumulation, image resampling, and alpha compositing. Y’CbCr uses a luma-like channel and color-difference channels. Since human vision resolves brightness detail more finely than chroma detail, chroma subsampling can reduce storage while preserving apparent sharpness. The apostrophe on Y ′ signals that the quantity is formed from nonlinear encoded components, rather than strict linear luminance. CMU-14 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Color: device values are not perceived color

The human visual system has overlapping cone responses rather than one receptor for each display primary. A screen uses a small set of primaries whose combinations stimulate those cones; many distinct spectra can produce the same cone responses. Those spectra are metamers: perceptually matching colors under a given viewing condition despite different physical spectral distributions. RGB is therefore a device-oriented coordinate system, not a universal description of color. CIE spaces use standard observer response functions to describe color more independently of one particular display. Color pipelines also need a transfer function. Most display RGB values are nonlinear encodings. If encoded channel values are averaged directly, the result is not generally the average light energy. Output devices turn samples into emitted or reflected light

The graphics pipeline ends in hardware that transforms a numeric pixel into light reaching an observer. A CRT scans an electron beam across phosphors; magnetic fields deflect the electrons and therefore shift the beam to the wrong phosphor. A color LCD overlays RGB filters on three spatially offset subpixel grids. This geometry explains why text benefits from subpixel antialiasing: ClearType-style rendering adjusts red, green, and blue subpixel coverage separately to make apparent horizontal edge placement finer than one full pixel, provided the display’s RGB order is known. From scene spectrum to display color

Use a simplified three-channel camera to follow color through the pipeline. At each wavelength, scene illumination and surface reflectance determine the light arriving at the sensor. Each color filter transmits part of that spectrum, then the photosensitive material converts some arriving photons into charge. The raw mosaic therefore gives sparse, differently filtered samples. Demosaicing estimates a red, green, and blue value at every pixel; white balance scales channels; a camera color matrix maps the camera’s sensor responses toward a standard RGB space; tone mapping and a nonlinear transfer curve make the values suitable for a display; JPEG may then subsample chroma and quantize transform coefficients. Errors at one stage can be hidden or magnified later.

Take an example with a white card under a warm indoor lamp. The card reflects broadly, but the lamp’s spectrum is weighted toward longer wavelengths. The raw red channel may be high relative to blue. If the photographer selects the card as neutral, white balance reduces red relative to blue so that the card appears near the chosen reference white. That operation also changes every other object’s channels. If the scene contains a deliberately warm candle flame, full neutralization can erase the warmth the viewer expects. Therefore the algorithm can estimate a neutral illuminant, but choosing how much to compensate is partly a scene interpretation and aesthetic decision. Color matrices and nonlinear encodings

A color space conversion between two linear three-channel representations can often be written as a matrix multiplication:

Color photographs are a sequence of measurements and reconstructions

Silicon sensors respond broadly across visible wavelengths; an electron count alone does not identify the photon’s wavelength. Color capture must make separate spectral measurements. Radiometry and BRDF

For incoming radiance L i , irradiance at surface point x is

E ( x ) = ∫ Ω L i ( x , ω ) | n ⋅ ω | d ω .

The projected-area cosine and the solid-angle measure use the quantities defined in the preceding radiometry section. The bidirectional reflectance distribution function, or BRDF, f r ( x , ω i , ω o ) describes how incoming light from direction ω i is reflected toward outgoing direction ω o . d ω = | n y ⋅ ( − ω ) | ‖ y − x ‖ 2 d A y .

Measure, units, and conservation

Radiometric quantities answer different questions. Radiant energy measures an amount of light energy and is measured in joules. Radiant flux is energy per time and is measured in watts. Irradiance is flux arriving on a surface per area. Radiant intensity is flux emitted per solid angle by a point-like source. Radiance is flux per projected area per solid angle. The units track what is being integrated. Integrating radiance over solid angle with a projected-area cosine yields irradiance. Integrating flux over time yields energy.

Solid angle subtends area on a unit sphere. A cone that cuts out spherical area A has solid angle Ω = A / r 2 on a sphere of radius r . The full sphere has 4 π steradians; a hemisphere has 2 π . A small disk far away subtends approximately its area divided by squared distance, multiplied by the cosine between its normal and the direction of view. This approximation is the same geometry behind inverse-square illumination and the area-to-solid-angle Jacobian.

Radiance is useful because, in a vacuum and absent participating media, it stays constant along a ray. Energy does not disappear merely because a ray changes direction. A surface can redirect radiance, absorb it, or emit it. A BRDF cannot create more outgoing energy than arrives when integrated over directions. These conservation principles catch physically impossible formulas. A diffuse BRDF constant over the hemisphere must be ρ / π , not just ρ , because integrating the cosine over a hemisphere yields π . Directional, hemispherical, and area lights

A directional light approximates a source infinitely far away: all rays share a direction and intensity does not vary with distance across a local scene. A point light emits from one location and obeys inverse-square falloff. A hemispherical source distributes light across a broad range of directions and can illuminate a surface more uniformly. An area source spans physical area, so different points on a receiver see different directions and distances. Area lights generally produce softer shadows because each point on the source creates a slightly different shadow boundary. These distinctions are consequences of source geometry and angular measure, not just different shading presets. CMU-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Light in a scene: source size, reflection, and shape

Radiometry measures electromagnetic energy without weighting for human vision. Photometry weights light by the visual system’s spectral sensitivity. Radiant power is measured in watts; luminous flux in lumens. Luminous intensity is flux per solid angle, measured in candelas, where one candela is one lumen per steradian. A steradian is the solid angle that subtends an area of r 2 on a sphere of radius r ; the full sphere is 4 π steradians. Luminance describes light per projected area and direction, in candelas per square meter. Illuminance is luminous flux arriving per area, in lux, where one lux is one lumen per square meter. These quantities are not interchangeable: a source’s intensity, a surface’s luminance, and the illumination falling on a subject answer different questions. PHOTO-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

A point source illuminates a surface according to the inverse-square law. Doubling distance spreads the same energy across four times the area, so illuminance falls to one quarter. An extended area source behaves differently at close range. If a camera views a large, uniform surface with the same lens and field of view, doubling distance makes each pixel see four times as much surface area while the solid angle of each patch falls by four; the effects can approximately cancel. This distinction explains why moving a small flash back changes exposure dramatically, while moving away from a large uniformly luminous wall may change little. Real rooms add reflections, boundaries, and finite-source geometry. PHOTO-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Light-source size and angular spread shape shadows. A small, distant source produces a crisp umbra with a narrow penumbra. A large source sends rays from a wider range of positions, so occlusion changes gradually and shadows soften. Leonardo da Vinci reasoned about umbra and penumbra as a painter’s tool: a region may be completely blocked, fully illuminated, or only partly covered by an extended source. A useful photographic model classifies a light by spatial extent (point, line, or area) and by the directions it emits from each part. A fluorescent tube is spatially long and diffuse; sunlight through a narrow crack is line-like but directional; a softbox is a large source with a controlled angular spread. PHOTO-16 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Surfaces also distribute reflected light differently. A Lambertian diffuse surface appears similar from many viewing directions, although its emitted radiance and projected area must be distinguished carefully. A mirror sends light in one direction according to equal incident and reflected angles. Many objects combine diffuse and specular reflection. A microfacet model treats a surface as many tiny facets with slightly different normals; roughness spreads the specular lobe, while a polished surface concentrates it. A goniometric diagram plots reflected intensity as a function of viewing and illumination angles. Brushed metal is anisotropic: the reflection depends on direction along versus across the brushing. PHOTO-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

The BRDF (bidirectional reflectance distribution function) describes how an opaque surface reflects light from an incoming direction to an outgoing direction. A BSSRDF adds the possibility that light enters at one surface point, scatters beneath the surface, and exits at another. Skin, wax, fruit, and translucent materials need subsurface scattering to look natural; a purely surface-based model can make faces look hard and plastic. Fresnel reflection describes how the reflected fraction changes with angle and material. Dielectric highlights tend to take the light source’s color; conductor highlights can carry the metal’s color. At grazing angles, even a diffuse-looking dielectric can show a stronger specular reflection. Powdering translucent colored glass can make it appear pale because many small interfaces scatter light before it is absorbed through a long path. PHOTO-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

The luminance of a sunlit cloud can be several stops above the blue sky; clouds and snow can both be highly reflective, which helps create whiteouts with few reliable horizon cues. A bright scene can still be difficult to photograph if its luminance range exceeds what one exposure can represent. Light measurement is therefore not bookkeeping alone. It predicts shadow strength, required aperture and shutter, flash reach, and how highlights will compress in a finished image. Lighting for a photograph

A portrait can be designed around a key light, the brightest or shaping light; a fill light, which controls shadow depth; a rim or accent light, which separates hair or shoulders from the background; and a background light. Moving a lamp changes its direction, apparent size, and shadow. Modifiers—softboxes, umbrellas, reflectors, grids, barn doors, and flags—change source size or the portion of the scene that receives light. A ratio close to one-to-one between key and fill makes a flatter, gentler portrait; a stronger key-to-fill difference creates more dramatic shadows. Broad lighting illuminates the face side turned toward the camera; short lighting emphasizes the less camera-facing side. Rembrandt lighting creates a small triangle of light on the shadowed cheek. Butterfly lighting places a central light above the camera and produces a symmetric glamour pattern. These names are practical shorthand for geometry and mood, not rigid recipes. PHOTO-16 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Available light can be shaped without adding a lamp. A wall can bounce light into the shadow side of a face; a white card can fill a hand or cheek. Window light can make a directional key. A large white background can isolate a portrait while leaving texture in the subject. Light can also act as a pictorial or narrative element: a diagonal shaft may structure the composition or imply revelation, danger, or attention. A photographer can read a scene by inspecting shadow direction and softness, specular highlights, catchlights, and brightness ratios before deciding whether to supplement it. PHOTO-16 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Special subjects need special control. Shiny objects reflect the room and photographer, so a tent or large diffuse enclosure can create a clean continuous reflection. Food highlights and moisture may need careful arrangement. Grazing light reveals shallow relief and embossed writing because it creates small directional shadows. Architectural interiors often require balancing a dim interior with a bright window; the photographer may make a long exposure for the outside while controlling interior lamps, accepting or managing clipped flames. These are lighting problems intertwined with exposure and composition. PHOTO-16 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Flash adds a brief pulse. It can freeze motion if the flash duration is shorter than the subject’s movement interval, fill shadows while exposing for the background, or balance an ambient key with controlled fill. A guide number estimates flash exposure: for a given flash, aperture is approximately the guide number divided by subject distance, with units and zoom setting held consistent. A guide number of 58 at 10 feet suggests about f / 5.8 , near f / 5.6 . The guide number falls with flash zoom because the beam is spread over a wider angle. Flash on the camera axis can flatten shadows and create red-eye; off-camera, bounced, or umbrella-modified flash gives more dimensional control. A focal-plane shutter has a maximum sync speed when the sensor is fully uncovered. High-speed sync emits a rapid pulse train while the shutter slit travels, but reduces effective flash power. PHOTO-16 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Flash and ambient light can have different color temperatures. A daylight-balanced flash used at sunset may appear blue unless warmed with an orange gel; a tungsten white balance may make an unfiltered flash blue, while a flash white balance can make tungsten lamps orange. Second-curtain sync fires near the end of a long exposure so motion trails follow a moving subject. Combining ambient and flash can preserve a room’s context while freezing a nearby subject. Computational flash/no-flash methods compare paired images to borrow color or detail, but reflections, shadows, scene motion, and alignment make robust fusion difficult. A burst of same-exposure underexposed frames is often more reliable for static or modest-motion scenes. Read the shadows to estimate a light source

A shadow contains information about source angular size. Consider a small object casting a shadow on a nearby wall. A point source gives a crisp boundary because rays come from nearly one direction. A large softbox gives a penumbra because some source positions are blocked while others remain visible. Move the softbox closer while keeping its physical size unchanged and it occupies a larger angular extent; the shadow softens and the subject-to-background falloff changes. Move it farther away and it appears smaller, giving harder shadows. A photographer can therefore alter both shadow softness and illuminance by moving a source, even before changing power. PHOTO-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5) · PHOTO-16 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

The direction of a specular highlight tells a different story from diffuse shading. On a matte face, turning toward the key light changes broad tonal regions. On a glossy eye, the catchlight reflects the shape and position of the source. On a polished car, the highlight can show a stripbox, ceiling, or flag even if that modifier is outside the frame. On brushed metal, highlight shape elongates along the surface texture because the BRDF is anisotropic. In a tent-lit trumpet, the photographer chooses what the metal should reflect, effectively using the room itself as a painted illumination pattern. A reflected-light meter reading from an outdoor scene also folds source, surface, and geometry together. Direct sun is strong, while the blue sky fills shadows from a broad hemisphere. The lit side and the shadow side may differ by only a few stops, less than a viewer intuitively guesses because dark shadows feel dramatic. Under cloud cover, the whole visible sky becomes a giant source; shadows nearly disappear, but local contrast and color saturation change. On snow beneath a bright cloudy sky, sky and ground can have similar luminance and visual cues to the horizon weaken, producing a whiteout. These are consequences of source extent and reflectance, not just exposure. PHOTO-15 (Vision, XR, and Playable Worlds, Part 1, Ch. 5)

Environment illumination and differential compositing

Connect to: light measurement and radiometry; reflectance and BRDFs; the rendering equation and light transport; image formation and compositing.

Debevec’s 1998 method begins with a useful division of the scene. Put faraway illumination in an environment: the distant world is represented by a high-dynamic-range (HDR) radiance map. Put nearby surfaces in a local scene: they need geometry and reflectance estimates because the inserted object can cast shadows on them, reflect from them, or light them. This division is physical, not merely organizational. A distant light source is not blocked by a small inserted object in the same way a nearby wall is. If the new object can substantially change a surface’s illumination, that surface belongs in the local model.

A mirror-ball photograph records directions around the probe as reflected directions on the ball. The method places the ball near the object insertion point, captures several exposures, and combines them into an HDR environment map. The map must represent radiance, not the display values in one ordinary exposure. Camera response, exposure, probe location, camera pose, and the synthetic scene’s coordinate frame must be aligned well enough that a rendered pixel refers to the same direction and place as the photograph. The probe is a compact measurement of incoming light; it does not by itself describe local walls, their reflectance, or occlusion.

The key compositing step is to predict a change. Let L b be the observed background pixel. Let L 0 be the rendered local scene without the inserted object, and let L 1 be the same render with it. Then add the modeled change to the photographed pixel:

L f i n a l = L b + ( L 1 − L 0 ) .

The difference L 1 − L 0 includes effects caused by the new object: its visible radiance, its shadow on modeled surfaces, and changes in interreflection that the renderer captures. Subtracting the no-object render removes the model’s estimate of the old local contribution; adding the difference preserves the photographed background’s original detail. This is differential rendering. It is especially useful when the photograph is more detailed than the scene model. The model only has to predict how the insertion changes the image, rather than recreate every pixel in the photograph.

A tiny numerical example makes the bookkeeping concrete. Suppose a photograph has pixel value L b = 0.40 in linear radiance units. The local-scene render predicts L 0 = 0.10 before insertion and L 1 = 0.22 after insertion. The modeled change is 0.12 , so the composite becomes 0.52 . The method does not replace the photographed value by 0.22 ; that would discard the rest of the photograph’s contribution at that pixel. In practice, values must be in compatible linear radiometric units. Applying this arithmetic directly to gamma-encoded display values is not physically correct.

The subtraction can expose model errors. If L 1 − L 0 is too negative, the composite can fall below zero even though radiance should not. The paper describes a relative alternative, L f i n a l = L b ( L 1 / L 0 ) , which scales the photographed contribution by the modeled ratio. This avoids some negative results, but it becomes unstable when L 0 is near zero and still depends on the model getting the ratio right. It is a compensation strategy, not a substitute for a good local model.

Reflectance estimation is also tied to the same measurement logic. For a diffuse patch with known incident illumination, compare a photograph to a render of the same patch assigned unit diffuse reflectance. In the paper’s Lambertian update, if L r 1 is the observed patch radiance and L r 2 is the rendered unit-reflectance radiance, update the diffuse reflectance estimate by

ρ d ′ = L r 1 L r 2 .

The render includes indirect illumination, so the ratio can be iterated as the scene’s reflectance estimates change. This is a model-fitting loop: render a prediction, compare it with a measurement, and adjust the parameter that scales the diffuse response. It relies on a sufficiently accurate lighting estimate and on the patch being adequately described by a diffuse model.

Limits and assumptions. The method needs a registered camera, probe, and local scene. A mirror ball has finite resolution and may include the camera, support, seams, or other artifacts. A probe far from the insertion point may measure the wrong illumination. Incorrect geometry or reflectance produces incorrect shadows and differences; differential compositing can preserve the plate but cannot make the predicted change correct. The paper’s demonstrations used the Radiance renderer and two diffuse bounces. Treat the results as evidence for this measured-light compositing setup, not proof that a sparse local model captures every modern material or transport effect.
