# PART 516 — STANDARD BASIS IN \(\mathbb R^2\)

## 516.1 What problem does a standard basis solve?

From Part 514, we know that coordinates only make sense relative to a basis.

A basis in a 2D vector space requires two independent vectors that span the whole space.

There are infinitely many possible choices.

For example:

\[
(1,0),(0,1)
\]

or:

\[
(1,1),(1,-1)
\]

or:

\[
(2,0),(1,3)
\]

can all form bases of \(\mathbb R^2\).

So why do we overwhelmingly use:

\[
\boxed{
\vec e_1=(1,0),\qquad \vec e_2=(0,1)
}
\]

?

Because this choice makes coordinate representation maximally direct.

These are the **standard basis vectors** of \(\mathbb R^2\).

---

# 516.2 Why the notation \(\vec e_1,\vec e_2\)?

The symbol:

\[
\vec e_i
\]

usually denotes the basis vector whose \(i\)-th coordinate is \(1\) and all others are \(0\).

In \(\mathbb R^2\):

\[
\vec e_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
\]

and:

\[
\vec e_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

You may also see graphics-oriented names:

\[
\hat x,\quad \hat y
\]

or:

```cpp
X_AXIS
Y_AXIS
```

The exact symbol varies.

The role is the same.

---

# 516.3 Proving they form a basis

A basis must be:

1. linearly independent;
2. spanning.

### Independence

Suppose:

\[
a\vec e_1+b\vec e_2=\vec0.
\]

Then:

\[
a(1,0)+b(0,1)=(0,0).
\]

So:

\[
(a,b)=(0,0).
\]

Therefore:

\[
a=0,\qquad b=0.
\]

Only the trivial solution exists.

Hence:

\[
\vec e_1,\vec e_2
\]

are linearly independent.

### Spanning

Take arbitrary vector:

\[
\vec v=(x,y).
\]

Then:

\[
x\vec e_1+y\vec e_2
=
x(1,0)+y(0,1)
\]

which is:

\[
(x,0)+(0,y)
\]

therefore:

\[
(x,y).
\]

So every vector in \(\mathbb R^2\) can be constructed.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\{\vec e_1,\vec e_2\}
\text{ is a basis of }\mathbb R^2.
}
\]

---

# 516.4 Why coordinates become visually obvious

If:

\[
\vec v=(3,2)
\]

in the standard basis, then:

\[
\vec v=3\vec e_1+2\vec e_2.
\]

This means:

```text
3 units along standard X
+
2 units along standard Y
```

Graphically:

```text
          y
          ↑
      2   |        ● (3,2)
          |       /
          |      /
          |     /
          |    /
----------+----------------→ x
          0       3
```

This is why Cartesian coordinates feel so natural.

The standard basis aligns directly with the coordinate axes.

---

# 516.5 Why \((1,0)\) is not universally "right"

This is a subtle but essential graphics point.

Inside an abstract \(\mathbb R^2\) coordinate system, \((1,0)\) is the first standard basis vector.

But its physical/geometric interpretation depends on the chosen coordinate convention.

It might mean:

```text
screen right
world east
texture U direction
local object X
camera right
```

or something else.

So:

\[
(1,0)
\]

is only "right" after the coordinate frame tells you what the first basis direction means.

The standard basis is **standard relative to the coordinate representation**, not universal physical space.

---

# 516.6 Screen-space caveat

In textbook mathematics:

```text
+x → right
+y → up
```

But many screen coordinate systems use:

```text
+x → right
+y → down
```

So:

\[
\vec e_2=(0,1)
\]

may visually point downward on screen.

This does not change the algebra.

It changes the geometric interpretation of the coordinate system.

This is why engine code must never casually assume:

```text
positive Y = up
```

without knowing the API or space.

---

# 516.7 Implementation

A simple constant representation:

```cpp
struct Vec2 {
    float x;
    float y;
};

inline constexpr Vec2 X_AXIS{1.0f, 0.0f};
inline constexpr Vec2 Y_AXIS{0.0f, 1.0f};
```

Then:

```cpp
Vec2 v{3.0f, 2.0f};
```

implicitly means:

\[
3X_{\text{axis}}+2Y_{\text{axis}}.
\]

The memory contains only:

```text
3.0f
2.0f
```

The basis is usually not stored because the standard basis is implicit in the type's coordinate convention.

That is an important optimization of representation:

\[
\boxed{
\text{shared conventional basis need not be stored per vector}
}
\]

---

# 516.8 Why this matters for engine math

A million world-space positions may be stored as:

```cpp
Vec2 positions[1'000'000];
```

There is no basis stored beside each position.

The engine assumes all these values are interpreted using the same world-space coordinate convention.

That assumption is powerful—but dangerous if violated.

When data crosses spaces, the implicit basis changes.

That is why later transform matrices exist.

---

# PART 517 — STANDARD BASIS IN \(\mathbb R^3\)

Now extend exactly the same reasoning to three dimensions.

The standard basis is:

\[
\boxed{
\vec e_1=(1,0,0)
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\vec e_2=(0,1,0)
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\vec e_3=(0,0,1)
}
\]

often called:

\[
\hat x,\quad\hat y,\quad\hat z.
\]

---

# 517.1 Why three basis vectors?

Because:

\[
\dim\mathbb R^3=3.
\]

Therefore any basis contains exactly three vectors.

We want three independent directions that span the entire space.

The standard basis isolates one coordinate at a time.

---

# 517.2 Independence proof

Suppose:

\[
a\vec e_1+b\vec e_2+c\vec e_3=\vec0.
\]

Then:

\[
a(1,0,0)
+
b(0,1,0)
+
c(0,0,1)
=
(0,0,0).
\]

So:

\[
(a,b,c)=(0,0,0).
\]

Therefore:

\[
a=b=c=0.
\]

Hence the three vectors are linearly independent.

---

# 517.3 Spanning proof

Take arbitrary:

\[
\vec v=(x,y,z).
\]

Then:

\[
x\vec e_1+y\vec e_2+z\vec e_3
\]

equals:

\[
(x,0,0)+(0,y,0)+(0,0,z)
\]

which is:

\[
(x,y,z).
\]

Thus they span all of \(\mathbb R^3\).

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\{\vec e_1,\vec e_2,\vec e_3\}
}
\]

is the standard basis.

---

# 517.4 The basis directions form the coordinate frame

Geometrically:

```text
                  +Y
                  ↑
                  |
                  |
                  |
                  O────────→ +X
                 /
                /
               /
             +Z
```

or perhaps:

```text
                  +Z
                  ↑
                  |
                  |
                  O────────→ +X
                 /
                /
               /
             +Y
```

depending on convention.

This is already a warning:

**there is no universal graphics meaning of X/Y/Z orientation.**

Different engines and APIs choose different world-up and forward directions.

---

# 517.5 Forward is not universally \(-Z\)

You may see:

```text
OpenGL-style camera convention:
forward ≈ -Z
```

but other systems may use:

```text
+Z forward
```

or:

```text
+X forward
```

The standard basis only gives three independent coordinate directions.

Semantic labels such as:

```text
right
up
forward
```

are extra conventions.

Do not merge them unconsciously.

---

# 517.6 Right-handed versus left-handed systems preview

The ordered basis:

\[
(\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z)
\]

also has an orientation or **handedness**.

That topic is explicitly later in Parts 563–565.

For now, just know:

> choosing which direction the third axis points relative to the first two affects orientation conventions.

This later influences:

- cross products;
- camera matrices;
- rotations;
- winding;
- projection conventions;
- coordinate conversion.

---

# 517.7 Standard basis in memory

Again, engines usually do not store these values per vector.

They are implicit.

Conceptually:

```cpp
inline constexpr Vec3 X_AXIS{1,0,0};
inline constexpr Vec3 Y_AXIS{0,1,0};
inline constexpr Vec3 Z_AXIS{0,0,1};
```

But a `Vec3` like:

```cpp
Vec3 v{5, -2, 7};
```

means:

\[
5\vec e_x-2\vec e_y+7\vec e_z.
\]

This is the linear-combination interpretation of coordinates.

---

# 517.8 World, local, and view standard bases

This deserves precision.

Suppose object local coordinates use:

```text
local basis:
(1,0,0)local
(0,1,0)local
(0,0,1)local
```

and world coordinates also use:

```text
world basis:
(1,0,0)world
(0,1,0)world
(0,0,1)world
```

These coordinate tuples look numerically identical.

But the actual geometric basis directions may differ because the object's local frame is rotated in world space.

So:

\[
(1,0,0)_{\text{local}}
\]

need not equal:

\[
(1,0,0)_{\text{world}}
\]

geometrically.

This is Part 506's distinction in practical engine form.

# PART 518 — VECTOR ADDITION FROM THE PARALLELOGRAM RULE

We introduced addition earlier because vector spaces need it.

Now we isolate the geometric reason why vector addition works the way it does.

---

# 518.1 The problem

Suppose one displacement is:

\[
\vec u.
\]

Another displacement is:

\[
\vec v.
\]

We want one vector representing the **combined displacement**.

The operation must answer:

> if I move by \(\vec u\), then by \(\vec v\), what single displacement has the same overall effect?

That result is:

\[
\vec u+\vec v.
\]

---

# 518.2 Head-to-tail derivation

Let:

```text
A --u--> B --v--> C
```

Then total movement from \(A\) to \(C\) is:

\[
\vec u+\vec v.
\]

Graphically:

```text
A ●──────→● B
  \   u     \
   \         \ v
    \         \
     \         ▼
      ────────→● C
         u+v
```

The resulting vector joins the starting point to the final point.

This is the **triangle rule**.

---

# 518.3 Parallelogram rule

Because free vectors can be translated, place \(\vec u\) and \(\vec v\) at the same origin.

Then construct a parallelogram:

```text
          ●
         /|
        / |
      v/  |v
      /   |
     ●----●
       u
```

The diagonal from the shared origin is:

\[
\boxed{
\vec u+\vec v
}
\]

This is the parallelogram rule.

---

# 518.4 Why parallelogram and head-to-tail are the same rule

Start both vectors at origin \(O\).

Then translate \(\vec v\) so its tail begins at the head of \(\vec u\).

Because translation does not change a free vector, it remains the same \(\vec v\).

The endpoint reached is exactly the opposite parallelogram corner.

Therefore the diagonal equals the head-to-tail result.

So these are not two different addition definitions.

They are two visualizations of the same operation.

---

# 518.5 Why addition becomes component-wise

Let:

\[
\vec u=(u_x,u_y)
\]

and:

\[
\vec v=(v_x,v_y).
\]

The first displacement changes X by:

\[
u_x.
\]

The second changes X by:

\[
v_x.
\]

Therefore total X change is:

\[
u_x+v_x.
\]

Likewise total Y change:

\[
u_y+v_y.
\]

Hence:

\[
\boxed{
\vec u+\vec v
=
(u_x+v_x,\;u_y+v_y)
}
\]

and in 3D:

\[
\boxed{
(u_x,u_y,u_z)
+
(v_x,v_y,v_z)
=
(u_x+v_x,\;
u_y+v_y,\;
u_z+v_z)
}
\]

This formula is a coordinate manifestation of displacement composition.

---

# 518.6 Worked example

Let:

\[
\vec u=(3,1)
\]

and:

\[
\vec v=(-2,4).
\]

Then:

\[
\vec u+\vec v
=
(3-2,\;1+4)
\]

so:

\[
\boxed{
(1,5).
}
\]

Interpretation:

First move:

```text
+3 X
+1 Y
```

then:

```text
-2 X
+4 Y
```

total:

```text
+1 X
+5 Y
```

---

# 518.7 Why addition is commutative

Parallelogram geometry immediately gives:

\[
\vec u+\vec v
=
\vec v+\vec u.
\]

Whichever edge you traverse first, the opposite corner is the same.

Coordinate proof:

\[
(u_x+v_x,\;u_y+v_y)
\]

equals:

\[
(v_x+u_x,\;v_y+u_y)
\]

because scalar addition is commutative.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\vec u+\vec v=\vec v+\vec u.
}
\]

---

# 518.8 Why addition is associative

Take three vectors:

\[
\vec u,\vec v,\vec w.
\]

The final displacement after all three is independent of how we group them:

\[
(\vec u+\vec v)+\vec w
=
\vec u+(\vec v+\vec w).
\]

Coordinate-wise this follows from scalar associativity.

But remember:

\[
\boxed{
\text{mathematically associative}
}
\]

does not imply:

\[
\boxed{
\text{bit-exactly associative in floating point}
}
\]

This matters for physics and accumulation.

---

# 518.9 Implementation

```cpp
struct Vec3 {
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
};

constexpr Vec3 operator+(Vec3 a, Vec3 b) noexcept {
    return {
        a.x + b.x,
        a.y + b.y,
        a.z + b.z
    };
}
```

Each line maps directly to displacement composition along one basis direction.

For one 3D addition:

```text
3 scalar additions
```

No allocation is necessary.

Time is fixed:

\[
O(1)
\]

for 3D.

For general \(n\)-dimensional vectors:

\[
O(n).
\]

---

# 518.10 Hot-loop behavior

Suppose an engine updates positions:

```cpp
position += velocity * dt;
```

The vector addition itself is cheap.

At large scale, performance may instead depend on:

- memory layout;
- cache locality;
- SIMD width;
- data fetch;
- number of objects;
- whether positions/velocities are SoA or AoS.

So even though vector addition is mathematically trivial, a million additions can become a memory-throughput problem.

---

# 518.11 Invalid semantic addition

The type system may permit:

```cpp
Vec3 color;
Vec3 velocity;

auto nonsense = color + velocity;
```

Mathematically, both may inhabit \(\mathbb R^3\) numerically.

Semantically, that addition may be meaningless.

So vector-space legality is not enough to guarantee domain meaning.

Engine types may need semantic wrappers.

---

# PART 519 — SCALAR MULTIPLICATION OF VECTORS

Now isolate the second primitive vector-space operation.

---

# 519.1 What problem does scalar multiplication solve?

Suppose:

\[
\vec v
\]

represents a displacement.

We want to express:

```text
twice that displacement
half that displacement
reverse it
erase it
```

using one operation.

That operation is scalar multiplication.

For scalar:

\[
a\in F
\]

and vector:

\[
\vec v\in V,
\]

we write:

\[
\boxed{
a\vec v.
}
\]

---

# 519.2 Geometric interpretation

For \(a>0\):

\[
a\vec v
\]

keeps the same general direction but scales the amount.

For:

\[
a>1,
\]

the vector becomes longer.

For:

\[
0<a<1,
\]

it becomes shorter.

For:

\[
a=0,
\]

it becomes the zero vector.

For:

\[
a<0,
\]

the direction reverses and magnitude is scaled by \(|a|\).

Conceptually:

```text
v       ─────→

2v      ──────────→

0.5v    ──→

-v      ←─────

-2v     ←──────────

0v      •
```

---

# 519.3 Component derivation

Let:

\[
\vec v
=
v_x\vec e_x+v_y\vec e_y.
\]

Multiply by scalar \(a\):

\[
a\vec v
=
a(v_x\vec e_x+v_y\vec e_y).
\]

Distribute:

\[
=
av_x\vec e_x+av_y\vec e_y.
\]

Therefore coordinates are:

\[
\boxed{
a(v_x,v_y)
=
(av_x,av_y).
}
\]

In 3D:

\[
\boxed{
a(x,y,z)
=
(ax,ay,az).
}
\]

Again, component-wise scaling is not arbitrary; it follows from distributivity.

---

# 519.4 Worked examples

Take:

\[
\vec v=(2,-3).
\]

### Scale by 2

\[
2\vec v=(4,-6).
\]

### Scale by \(\frac12\)

\[
\frac12\vec v
=
(1,-1.5).
\]

### Scale by -1

\[
-\vec v=(-2,3).
\]

### Scale by 0

\[
0\vec v=(0,0).
\]

Each is exactly what the geometric intuition predicts.

---

# 519.5 Why negative scaling reverses direction

Suppose:

\[
\vec v=(3,1).
\]

Then:

\[
-\vec v=(-3,-1).
\]

The new vector points exactly opposite in coordinate space.

Also:

\[
\vec v+(-\vec v)=\vec0.
\]

So \(-1\) scaling produces the additive inverse.

This matches the Part 507 proof:

\[
(-1)\vec v=-\vec v.
\]

---

# 519.6 Scaling twice

Suppose first:

\[
b\vec v
\]

then:

\[
a(b\vec v).
\]

Vector-space compatibility requires:

\[
a(b\vec v)
=
(ab)\vec v.
\]

Example:

\[
2(3\vec v)=6\vec v.
\]

So nested scaling collapses into scalar multiplication.

This matters in engine calculations where chains of unit conversion, object scale, animation scale, or interpolation weights may multiply together.

---

# 519.7 Units and scalar multiplication

Suppose:

\[
\vec v
\]

is velocity:

\[
\text{metres/second}.
\]

Multiply by timestep:

\[
\Delta t
\]

measured in seconds.

Then:

\[
\vec v\Delta t
\]

has units:

\[
\frac{\text m}{\text s}\cdot\text s
=
\text m.
\]

So it becomes a displacement.

This is why:

```cpp
position += velocity * dt;
```

is physically meaningful.

The scalar multiplication is not merely numerical scaling.

It can change physical units when the scalar itself has units.

This connects vector algebra to dimensional analysis.

---

# 519.8 `velocity * dt` execution

Suppose:

\[
\vec v=(10,2,0)\ \text{m/s}
\]

and:

\[
\Delta t=0.016\text{ s}.
\]

Then:

\[
\Delta\vec x
=
\vec v\Delta t.
\]

Compute:

\[
(10,2,0)\cdot0.016
\]

giving:

\[
(0.16,0.032,0)\text{ m}.
\]

Then:

\[
\vec x_{\text{new}}
=
\vec x_{\text{old}}+\Delta\vec x.
\]

This simple operation becomes the backbone of numerical motion integration later.

---

# 519.9 Scalar multiplication implementation

```cpp
constexpr Vec3 operator*(Vec3 v, float s) noexcept {
    return {
        v.x * s,
        v.y * s,
        v.z * s
    };
}

constexpr Vec3 operator*(float s, Vec3 v) noexcept {
    return v * s;
}
```

Supporting both orders:

```cpp
v * 2.0f
```

and:

```cpp
2.0f * v
```

is useful because real scalar-vector multiplication is conventionally written in either form.

---

# 519.10 Why vector-times-vector is not the same operation

Do not generalize:

```cpp
Vec3 * Vec3
```

as "scalar multiplication."

Scalar multiplication specifically means:

\[
F\times V\rightarrow V.
\]

One operand is from the scalar field.

Another is from the vector space.

Component-wise vector multiplication may be useful in graphics:

```cpp
colorA * colorB
```

but it is a different operation with different semantics.

Later dot and cross products are also different.

---

# 519.11 Floating-point numerical behavior

Consider huge component:

\[
v_x=10^{38}.
\]

Scale by:

\[
100.
\]

Mathematically:

\[
10^{40}.
\]

But `float` may overflow to:

\[
+\infty.
\]

Or consider:

\[
10^{-38}\cdot10^{-10}.
\]

The result may underflow severely.

So scalar multiplication can trigger:

- overflow;
- underflow;
- loss of precision;
- NaN propagation.

The algebra may be valid while the machine representation fails to preserve it exactly.

---

# PART 520 — VECTOR SUBTRACTION AS DISPLACEMENT DIFFERENCE

Now comes an operation graphics programmers use constantly:

\[
\vec a-\vec b.
\]

But the geometric meaning depends on what those operands represent.

---

# 520.1 Subtraction is not fundamentally primitive

Vector subtraction can be defined using addition and additive inverse:

\[
\boxed{
\vec u-\vec v
=
\vec u+(-\vec v).
}
\]

So subtraction needs no new vector-space axiom.

It is derived.

This mirrors scalar subtraction:

\[
a-b=a+(-b).
\]

---

# 520.2 Coordinate rule

Let:

\[
\vec u=(u_x,u_y,u_z)
\]

and:

\[
\vec v=(v_x,v_y,v_z).
\]

Then:

\[
\vec u-\vec v
=
\vec u+(-\vec v).
\]

Since:

\[
-\vec v=(-v_x,-v_y,-v_z),
\]

we get:

\[
\boxed{
\vec u-\vec v
=
(u_x-v_x,\;
u_y-v_y,\;
u_z-v_z).
}
\]

---

# 520.3 The most important geometric use: point difference

Suppose two points:

\[
A
\]

and:

\[
B
\]

have coordinates:

\[
A=(A_x,A_y)
\]

and:

\[
B=(B_x,B_y).
\]

The displacement from \(A\) to \(B\) is:

\[
\boxed{
\overrightarrow{AB}=B-A.
}
\]

This is one of the most important formulas in geometry.

Why?

Because we want a vector \(\vec d\) such that:

\[
A+\vec d=B.
\]

Solve:

\[
\vec d=B-A.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{destination} - \text{source}
=
\text{displacement from source to destination}.
}
\]

---

# 520.4 Worked example

Let:

\[
A=(2,3)
\]

and:

\[
B=(7,5).
\]

Then:

\[
B-A
=
(7-2,\;5-3)
\]

giving:

\[
\boxed{
(5,2).
}
\]

Interpretation:

To get from \(A\) to \(B\):

```text
move +5 along X
move +2 along Y
```

Check:

\[
A+(B-A)
\]

equals:

\[
(2,3)+(5,2)
=
(7,5)
=
B.
\]

So the displacement is correct.

---

# 520.5 Order matters

\[
B-A
\]

and:

\[
A-B
\]

point in opposite directions.

Since:

\[
A-B=-(B-A).
\]

Example:

\[
B-A=(5,2)
\]

but:

\[
A-B=(-5,-2).
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{subtraction is not commutative}
}
\]

because:

\[
\vec u-\vec v
\neq
\vec v-\vec u
\]

unless the vectors happen to be equal.

---

# 520.6 Why this is called displacement difference

Suppose:

\[
\vec u
\]

and:

\[
\vec v
\]

are themselves displacement vectors.

Then:

\[
\vec u-\vec v
\]

asks:

> what displacement must be added to \(\vec v\) to obtain \(\vec u\)?

Because:

\[
\vec v+(\vec u-\vec v)=\vec u.
\]

So subtraction gives the **difference** between two displacement states.

---

# 520.7 Velocity difference

Suppose two bodies have velocities:

\[
\vec v_A
\]

and:

\[
\vec v_B.
\]

Relative velocity often involves:

\[
\boxed{
\vec v_B-\vec v_A.
}
\]

This describes how B's motion differs from A's.

Later collision response depends heavily on relative velocity.

The same subtraction operation appears because both quantities inhabit the same vector space.

---

# 520.8 Ray direction from two points

Suppose:

\[
P_{\text{camera}}
\]

and:

\[
P_{\text{target}}.
\]

A direction toward the target begins with:

\[
\vec d=P_{\text{target}}-P_{\text{camera}}.
\]

Then later we may normalize \(\vec d\).

So ray generation fundamentally relies on point subtraction.

The sequence is:

```text
target point
-
origin point
=
displacement
↓
normalize later
=
direction
```

This distinction is critical.

---

# 520.9 Surface edge vector

Given triangle vertices:

\[
A,\quad B,\quad C,
\]

two edge vectors are commonly:

\[
\vec e_1=B-A
\]

and:

\[
\vec e_2=C-A.
\]

These are not points.

They are displacements along triangle edges.

Later:

\[
\vec e_1\times\vec e_2
\]

gives the geometric normal direction.

So even advanced triangle geometry starts with subtraction.

---

# 520.10 Why point + point is different

This preview matters.

If \(A\) and \(B\) are geometric points, then:

\[
B-A
\]

has natural meaning:

> displacement from A to B.

But:

\[
A+B
\]

does not have the same natural geometric meaning.

This is why points fundamentally belong to affine geometry rather than pure vector-space geometry.

Full treatment appears in Parts 521–522.

For now remember:

```text
point - point → vector
point + vector → point
vector + vector → vector
```

but:

```text
point + point → not a natural affine operation
```

# 520.11 C++ semantic implementation

A weak API might use:

```cpp
using Point3 = Vec3;
```

Then the compiler cannot distinguish:

```cpp
Point3 a;
Point3 b;

auto d = b - a;
```

from arbitrary vector subtraction.

A stronger API could write:

```cpp
struct Point3 {
    float x, y, z;
};

struct Vec3 {
    float x, y, z;
};

constexpr Vec3 operator-(Point3 b, Point3 a) noexcept {
    return {
        b.x - a.x,
        b.y - a.y,
        b.z - a.z
    };
}

constexpr Point3 operator+(Point3 p, Vec3 d) noexcept {
    return {
        p.x + d.x,
        p.y + d.y,
        p.z + d.z
    };
}
```

Now the type system expresses geometry.

This is a much stronger design.

---

# 520.12 Why this prevents real bugs

With distinct types:

```cpp
Point3 p1;
Point3 p2;
Vec3 velocity;
```

this operation:

```cpp
p2 - p1
```

returns a displacement.

But nonsense like:

```cpp
p1 + p2
```

can be forbidden entirely.

The type system now encodes affine semantics rather than relying on human discipline.

This can prevent coordinate-system and physics bugs.

---

# 520.13 Numerical cancellation

Vector subtraction has a major numerical danger.

Suppose:

\[
A=(10^8,10^8,10^8)
\]

and:

\[
B=(10^8+1,10^8,10^8).
\]

Mathematically:

\[
B-A=(1,0,0).
\]

But if `float` cannot represent the `+1` difference at that magnitude, both coordinates may round to the same stored number.

Then machine subtraction gives:

\[
(0,0,0)
\]

instead.

This is **catastrophic loss of local detail due to large coordinates**.

It is one reason large-world engines may use:

- double precision;
- camera-relative coordinates;
- origin rebasing.

Those later topics are Parts 1054–1057.

But the numerical root begins here: subtraction of nearly equal large values.

---

# 520.14 Implementation

```cpp
constexpr Vec3 operator-(Vec3 a, Vec3 b) noexcept {
    return {
        a.x - b.x,
        a.y - b.y,
        a.z - b.z
    };
}
```

Fixed 3D complexity:

\[
O(1).
\]

For \(n\)-dimensional vectors:

\[
O(n).
\]

The arithmetic is cheap, but numerical precision and semantic correctness can be far more important than raw operation count.

# 516–520: THE COMPLETE CONNECTION

These five Parts form a clean conceptual block.

The **standard basis** gives us canonical coordinate directions:

\[
\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z.
\]

A vector such as:

\[
(x,y,z)
\]

means:

\[
x\vec e_x+y\vec e_y+z\vec e_z.
\]

Now if we compose two displacements, we use:

\[
\boxed{
\vec u+\vec v
}
\]

which geometrically comes from the parallelogram/head-to-tail rule and computationally becomes component-wise addition.

To change the amount of one displacement, we use:

\[
\boxed{
a\vec v
}
\]

which scales every coordinate component.

To ask for the difference between two vectors—or especially the displacement between two points—we use:

\[
\boxed{
\vec u-\vec v
}
\]

which is addition of the additive inverse.

So:

```text
standard basis
      ↓
coordinates
      ↓
vector addition
      ↓
compose displacements

scalar multiplication
      ↓
scale/reverse/collapse displacement

vector subtraction
      ↓
difference / relative displacement
```

---

# COMPLETE WORKED ENGINE EXAMPLE

Suppose a character has world-space point:

\[
P=(10,3,5).
\]

Velocity:

\[
\vec v=(4,0,-2)\ \text{m/s}.
\]

Frame time:

\[
\Delta t=0.016\text{ s}.
\]

First compute displacement:

\[
\Delta\vec p
=
\vec v\Delta t.
\]

So:

\[
(4,0,-2)\cdot0.016
=
(0.064,0,-0.032).
\]

Now add displacement to point:

\[
P'
=
P+\Delta\vec p.
\]

Therefore:

\[
P'
=
(10.064,3,4.968).
\]

Now suppose a target is at:

\[
T=(13,5,9).
\]

The displacement from the character to target is:

\[
T-P'.
\]

Compute:

\[
13-10.064=2.936
\]

\[
5-3=2
\]

\[
9-4.968=4.032.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\vec d=(2.936,2,4.032).
}
\]

This simple chain uses three of today's Parts:

```text
velocity × dt
→ scalar multiplication

position + displacement
→ vector addition / affine point displacement

target - position
→ displacement difference
```

Nearly every motion system in an engine rests on these operations.

# COMMON CONFUSIONS YOU SHOULD NOW ELIMINATE

A standard basis is not "the only basis." It is merely the canonical axis-aligned basis used for ordinary coordinates.

A vector's components are coefficients relative to that basis.

Vector addition does not mean "add arbitrary structs with x/y/z fields"; it composes compatible vectors in the same space.

Scalar multiplication does not mean component-wise vector multiplication. One operand is a scalar.

Vector subtraction is not mysterious—it is addition of the negative vector.

And the expression:

\[
B-A
\]

is not merely arithmetic on coordinates. When \(A\) and \(B\) are points, it has the geometric meaning:

\[
\boxed{
\text{displacement from }A\text{ to }B.
}
\]

# INTERVIEW ATTACK TREE

### Level 1 — What is the standard basis of \(\mathbb R^2\)?

\[
(1,0),(0,1).
\]

They are independent and span all of \(\mathbb R^2\).

---

### Level 2 — Why does \((x,y)\) mean \(x\) and \(y\)?

Because:

\[
(x,y)
=
x(1,0)+y(0,1).
\]

The numbers are coefficients relative to the standard basis.

---

### Level 3 — What is the standard basis of \(\mathbb R^3\)?

\[
(1,0,0),
(0,1,0),
(0,0,1).
\]

---

### Level 4 — Why is vector addition component-wise?

Because total displacement along each basis direction is the sum of the individual displacements along that direction.

---

### Level 5 — Explain the parallelogram rule.

Place two free vectors at the same origin. Complete the parallelogram. The diagonal from the shared origin is their sum.

It is equivalent to head-to-tail composition.

---

### Level 6 — What does \(a\vec v\) mean geometrically?

Scale the vector's displacement magnitude by \(|a|\); reverse orientation when \(a<0\); obtain zero vector when \(a=0\).

---

### Level 7 — What is vector subtraction?

\[
\vec u-\vec v
=
\vec u+(-\vec v).
\]

It gives the vector that must be added to \(\vec v\) to obtain \(\vec u\).

---

### Level 8 — What does \(B-A\) mean for points?

The displacement from \(A\) to \(B\).

---

### Level 9 — Why can large-world subtraction fail in `float`?

If \(A\) and \(B\) are huge and differ only slightly, their stored floating-point representations may lose the small difference before subtraction happens.

---

### Level 10 — Why aren't world-space `(1,0,0)` and local-space `(1,0,0)` necessarily the same vector?

Because the numeric tuple is relative to different bases. Identical coefficients do not imply identical geometric directions across coordinate spaces.
