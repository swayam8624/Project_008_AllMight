# THE MAP OF 526–533

Before diving in, understand why these are eight separate Parts.

We currently know what a vector is:

\[
\vec v=(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n).
\]

But that tuple by itself doesn't answer:

> How large is this vector?

There are actually **multiple valid answers**, depending on what definition of "size" we choose.

The familiar geometric answer is Euclidean length:

\[
\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.
\]

That becomes the **L2 norm**.

But we can define other legitimate notions of size:

\[
|x|+|y|+|z|
\]

or:

\[
\max(|x|,|y|,|z|).
\]

Those are L1 and L∞.

Then once we know how to measure the size of a vector difference, we automatically obtain a way to measure distance between two points:

\[
d(P,Q)=\|Q-P\|.
\]

So the dependency chain is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{vector}
\rightarrow
\text{magnitude}
\rightarrow
\text{squared magnitude}
\rightarrow
\text{normalization}
\rightarrow
\text{robust normalization}
\rightarrow
\text{general norm}
\rightarrow
L_1,L_2,L_\infty
\rightarrow
\text{distance}
}
\]

---

# PART 526 — EUCLIDEAN VECTOR MAGNITUDE

# 526.1 The problem

Consider:

\[
\vec v=(3,4).
\]

The vector tells us:

```text
3 units along X
4 units along Y
```

But suppose I ask:

> What is the actual straight-line amount of displacement?

It is not:

\[
3+4=7.
\]

If you physically move three metres east and four metres north, your direct displacement from start to finish is five metres.

Why five?

We need geometry.

---

# 526.2 Start from a right triangle

Take:

\[
\vec v=(x,y).
\]

Draw its components:

```text
             ●
             |\
             | \
           y |  \  ?
             |   \
             |    \
             ●-----●
                x
```

The horizontal component and vertical component form perpendicular sides of a right triangle.

The vector itself forms the hypotenuse.

By the Pythagorean theorem:

\[
c^2=x^2+y^2.
\]

Therefore:

\[
c=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
\]

So the Euclidean magnitude of a two-dimensional vector is:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|
=
\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
}
\]

where the double bars

\[
\|\vec v\|
\]

mean the magnitude or norm of the vector.

---

# 526.3 Why the positive square root?

From:

\[
c^2=x^2+y^2
\]

algebraically we might write:

\[
c=\pm\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
\]

But length is defined to be nonnegative.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
c=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\ge0.
}
\]

A vector can point negatively along coordinates.

Its **length cannot be negative**.

For example:

\[
\vec v=(-3,-4).
\]

Magnitude:

\[
\sqrt{(-3)^2+(-4)^2}
=
\sqrt{9+16}
=
5.
\]

The signs describe orientation.

Length describes amount.

---

# 526.4 Deriving 3D magnitude

Now take:

\[
\vec v=(x,y,z).
\]

First combine the X and Y displacement.

Its projected length in the \(xy\)-plane is:

\[
r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
\]

Now \(r\) and \(z\) form another perpendicular right triangle.

Therefore:

\[
\|\vec v\|^2
=
r^2+z^2.
\]

Substitute:

\[
r^2=x^2+y^2.
\]

So:

\[
\|\vec v\|^2
=
x^2+y^2+z^2.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|
=
\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.
}
\]

---

# 526.5 Generalization to \(\mathbb R^n\)

For:

\[
\vec v=
(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n),
\]

the Euclidean magnitude is:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_2
=
\sqrt{
v_1^2+
v_2^2+
\cdots+
v_n^2
}.
}
\]

Equivalently:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_2
=
\sqrt{
\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_i^2
}.
}
\]

Here:

- \(n\) is dimension;
- \(i\) indexes components;
- \(v_i\) is component \(i\);
- \(\sum\) means add the indicated terms.

The subscript \(2\) will matter when we distinguish different norms later.

---

# 526.6 There is an extremely important hidden assumption

Why was:

\[
\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
\]

valid?

Because X and Y represented **perpendicular unit basis directions**.

We implicitly assumed an **orthonormal basis**.

"Orthonormal" means:

1. basis directions are mutually perpendicular;
2. each basis direction has unit length.

Detailed orthonormal-basis theory is Part 559, but the warning belongs here.

Suppose your basis is:

\[
\vec b_1=(1,0)
\]

and:

\[
\vec b_2=(1,1).
\]

Coordinates:

\[
(1,1)_B
\]

mean the geometric vector:

\[
\vec v
=
1\vec b_1+1\vec b_2
\]

so:

\[
\vec v
=
(1,0)+(1,1)
=
(2,1)
\]

in standard coordinates.

Its Euclidean length is:

\[
\sqrt{2^2+1^2}
=
\sqrt5.
\]

But naively applying:

\[
\sqrt{1^2+1^2}
\]

to its basis-\(B\) coordinates gives:

\[
\sqrt2.
\]

Wrong.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2}
}
\]

equals geometric Euclidean length only when those coordinates are expressed relative to an appropriate orthonormal Euclidean basis.

This point becomes extremely important once transformations and nonorthogonal coordinate systems appear.

---

# 526.7 Why squaring every component?

Suppose components are:

\[
(-3,4).
\]

The component \(-3\) means motion opposite the positive X basis direction.

Magnitude must not become smaller merely because the component points left instead of right.

Squaring removes sign:

\[
(-3)^2=9.
\]

More deeply, Pythagorean length measures contributions from mutually perpendicular directions using squares.

It is not arbitrary sign removal.

---

# 526.8 Magnitude properties

For Euclidean magnitude:

### Non-negativity

\[
\|\vec v\|\ge0.
\]

### Zero only for the zero vector

If:

\[
\|\vec v\|=0,
\]

then:

\[
v_1^2+\cdots+v_n^2=0.
\]

Every term is nonnegative.

For their sum to equal zero, every term must equal zero:

\[
v_i=0
\]

for every \(i\).

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|=0
\iff
\vec v=\vec0.
}
\]

---

# 526.9 Negation does not change magnitude

Take:

\[
-\vec v=(-v_1,\ldots,-v_n).
\]

Then:

\[
\|-\vec v\|
=
\sqrt{
(-v_1)^2+\cdots+(-v_n)^2
}.
\]

Since:

\[
(-v_i)^2=v_i^2,
\]

we get:

\[
\boxed{
\|-\vec v\|=\|\vec v\|.
}
\]

Opposite direction.

Same length.

---

# 526.10 Scaling and magnitude

For scalar \(a\):

\[
a\vec v
=
(av_1,\ldots,av_n).
\]

Magnitude:

\[
\|a\vec v\|
=
\sqrt{
a^2v_1^2+\cdots+a^2v_n^2
}.
\]

Factor:

\[
=
\sqrt{
a^2
(v_1^2+\cdots+v_n^2)
}.
\]

Since:

\[
\sqrt{a^2}=|a|,
\]

we obtain:

\[
\boxed{
\|a\vec v\|
=
|a|\|\vec v\|.
}
\]

Why absolute value?

If:

\[
a=-2,
\]

the vector reverses direction and doubles in length.

Its length is \(2\), not \(-2\), times the original.

---

# 526.11 Units

Suppose:

\[
x,y,z
\]

are measured in metres.

Then:

\[
x^2
\]

has units:

\[
m^2.
\]

Therefore:

\[
x^2+y^2+z^2
\]

has units:

\[
m^2.
\]

Square root returns:

\[
m.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|
}
\]

has the same physical unit as \(\vec v\).

This matters.

A displacement magnitude is metres.

A velocity magnitude is:

\[
m/s.
\]

Acceleration magnitude:

\[
m/s^2.
\]

---

# 526.12 Worked example: 3–4–12

Take:

\[
\vec v=(3,4,12).
\]

Calculate:

\[
\|\vec v\|
=
\sqrt{3^2+4^2+12^2}.
\]

Squares:

\[
9,\quad16,\quad144.
\]

Sum:

\[
9+16+144=169.
\]

Square root:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|=13.
}
\]

---

# 526.13 Canonical C++ implementation

A direct implementation:

```cpp
#include <cmath>

struct Vec3 {
    float x{};
    float y{};
    float z{};
};

[[nodiscard]]
float length(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return std::sqrt(
        v.x * v.x +
        v.y * v.y +
        v.z * v.z
    );
}
```

Execution for:

```cpp
Vec3 v{3.0f, 4.0f, 12.0f};
```

is conceptually:

```text
x*x       → 9
y*y       → 16
z*z       → 144

9+16      → 25
25+144    → 169

sqrt(169) → 13
```

---

# 526.14 A more numerically robust implementation

The direct formula has a numerical weakness.

Consider enormous values:

\[
x=10^{308},
\qquad
y=10^{308}
\]

in `double`.

The actual length is approximately:

\[
1.414\times10^{308},
\]

which still fits inside a finite `double`.

But computing:

\[
x^2
\]

requires approximately:

\[
10^{616},
\]

which overflows.

So the intermediate result becomes infinity even though the final answer is representable.

The standard library provides:

```cpp
std::hypot(...)
```

which implementations are designed to evaluate hypotenuse-like expressions more robustly.

For three dimensions:

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
double robustLength(double x,
                    double y,
                    double z) noexcept
{
    return std::hypot(x, y, z);
}
```

A common underlying principle is scaling.

Let:

\[
m=\max(|x|,|y|,|z|).
\]

Then:

\[
\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}
=
m
\sqrt{
(x/m)^2+
(y/m)^2+
(z/m)^2
}.
\]

After division by the largest component, every magnitude is at most one, greatly reducing overflow risk.

---

# 526.15 Why not always use `std::hypot`?

Because robustness has a potential cost.

A direct:

```cpp
sqrt(x*x + y*y + z*z)
```

is extremely simple.

A robust implementation may require:

- absolute values;
- comparisons;
- scaling;
- additional divisions;
- special-case handling.

If an engine already guarantees values remain safely scaled, direct arithmetic may be faster.

So production engineering asks:

\[
\boxed{
\text{What numeric range does this subsystem actually need?}
}
\]

before automatically choosing either implementation.

---

# PART 527 — SQUARED MAGNITUDE AND WHY GRAPHICS OFTEN AVOIDS SQUARE ROOTS

This Part is deceptively important.

A huge amount of geometry code does **not** need the actual length.

It only needs to compare lengths.

---

# 527.1 Start from magnitude

Euclidean magnitude:

\[
\|\vec v\|
=
\sqrt{
x^2+y^2+z^2
}.
\]

Square both sides:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|^2
=
x^2+y^2+z^2.
}
\]

This is the **squared magnitude**, often called:

```text
length squared
squared length
length2
norm squared
```

depending on codebase.

---

# 527.2 Why can we compare squared magnitudes?

Suppose:

\[
a\ge0,\qquad b\ge0.
\]

The square-root function is strictly increasing on nonnegative numbers.

Therefore:

\[
a<b
\]

if and only if:

\[
\sqrt a<\sqrt b.
\]

So if:

\[
\|\vec u\|^2
<
\|\vec v\|^2,
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec u\|
<
\|\vec v\|.
}
\]

Therefore if the only question is:

> which vector is shorter?

we do not need either square root.

---

# 527.3 Example

Take:

\[
\vec a=(3,4)
\]

and:

\[
\vec b=(6,1).
\]

Squared magnitudes:

\[
\|\vec a\|^2
=
9+16=25.
\]

\[
\|\vec b\|^2
=
36+1=37.
\]

Since:

\[
25<37,
\]

we immediately know:

\[
\|\vec a\|<\|\vec b\|.
\]

No need to calculate:

\[
5
\]

and:

\[
\sqrt{37}.
\]

---

# 527.4 Radius testing

Suppose an enemy triggers when player distance is less than:

\[
r=10.
\]

Naive code:

```cpp
if (length(enemy - player) < 10.0f) {
    trigger();
}
```

This computes a square root.

Instead:

\[
\|enemy-player\|<10.
\]

Both sides are nonnegative, so square:

\[
\|enemy-player\|^2<100.
\]

Code:

```cpp
if (lengthSquared(enemy - player) < 100.0f) {
    trigger();
}
```

No square root.

---

# 527.5 Correct API

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
constexpr float lengthSquared(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return
        v.x * v.x +
        v.y * v.y +
        v.z * v.z;
}
```

Then:

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
float length(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return std::sqrt(lengthSquared(v));
}
```

This is a good layering:

```text
component squares
       ↓
lengthSquared
       ↓
sqrt only when needed
       ↓
length
```

---

# 527.6 Hardware reason

Addition and multiplication are fundamental floating-point operations.

Square root is a more specialized operation and generally carries higher latency/throughput cost than an ordinary multiply or add, although exact behavior varies by CPU/GPU architecture.

So avoiding unnecessary square roots is a valid optimization when:

1. you genuinely need only ordering or a squared threshold;
2. profiling shows the code executes enough to matter.

Do not mechanically eliminate every `sqrt`.

---

# 527.7 When you CANNOT use squared magnitude as a substitute

Suppose velocity is:

\[
\vec v=(3,4)\ m/s.
\]

Speed is:

\[
5\ m/s.
\]

Squared speed is:

\[
25\ m^2/s^2.
\]

Those have different dimensions.

If UI asks:

```text
Player speed: ?
```

you cannot display 25.

The required quantity is 5.

Likewise:

```cpp
distanceTravelled += length(delta);
```

cannot be replaced by:

```cpp
distanceTravelled += lengthSquared(delta);
```

because that changes the mathematical quantity.

---

# 527.8 Squared magnitude is not itself a norm

Remember one norm property is:

\[
\|a\vec v\|
=
|a|\|\vec v\|.
\]

Squared magnitude instead satisfies:

\[
\|a\vec v\|^2
=
a^2\|\vec v\|^2.
\]

For example:

\[
\vec v=(1,0).
\]

Squared magnitude:

\[
1.
\]

Scale by 2:

\[
2\vec v=(2,0).
\]

Squared magnitude:

\[
4.
\]

If squared magnitude were a norm, scaling by 2 should produce:

\[
2\cdot1=2.
\]

It produces 4.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|^2
\text{ is not a norm}.
}
\]

---

# 527.9 Squared Euclidean distance is not a metric

Similarly:

\[
d^2(P,Q)=\|Q-P\|^2
\]

is extremely useful computationally.

But it is not a true mathematical distance metric because it fails the triangle inequality.

Take points on a line:

\[
A=0,\quad B=1,\quad C=2.
\]

Then:

\[
d^2(A,C)=4.
\]

But:

\[
d^2(A,B)+d^2(B,C)
=
1+1
=
2.
\]

A metric would require:

\[
4\le2,
\]

which is false.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{squared distance is useful for comparison but is not itself a metric}.
}
\]

---

# 527.10 Squaring can overflow

Avoiding the square root does **not** automatically make the computation numerically safer.

For huge:

\[
x,
\]

the term:

\[
x^2
\]

can overflow.

So:

```cpp
lengthSquared(v)
```

may return infinity even when:

```cpp
length(v)
```

implemented robustly with scaling could return a finite result.

This is an important production distinction.

---

# 527.11 Tiny values can underflow

For very small floating-point values:

\[
x^2
\]

can become too small to represent normally or even round to zero.

Then:

\[
lengthSquared(v)
\]

can incorrectly appear zero.

This becomes particularly dangerous when normalization code does:

```cpp
float invLength =
    1.0f / std::sqrt(lengthSquared(v));
```

because an underflowed zero becomes division by zero.

Part 529 will address this properly.

---

# PART 528 — NORMALIZING A VECTOR

Now we answer:

> Given a nonzero vector that contains both direction and magnitude, how do I remove the magnitude and retain only direction?

That operation is **normalization**.

---

# 528.1 Desired output

Given:

\[
\vec v\neq\vec0,
\]

we want a vector:

\[
\hat v
\]

that:

1. points in the same direction;
2. has magnitude 1.

So we want:

\[
\|\hat v\|=1.
\]

---

# 528.2 Derive the formula instead of memorizing it

We know scalar multiplication obeys:

\[
\|a\vec v\|
=
|a|\|\vec v\|.
\]

We want:

\[
\|a\vec v\|=1.
\]

Therefore:

\[
|a|\|\vec v\|=1.
\]

To preserve the same direction rather than reverse it, choose positive \(a\).

Then:

\[
a=\frac1{\|\vec v\|}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\hat v
=
\frac{\vec v}{\|\vec v\|}
}
\]

for:

\[
\vec v\neq\vec0.
\]

That is normalization.

---

# 528.3 Prove the output has unit length

Take:

\[
\hat v=
\frac{\vec v}{\|\vec v\|}.
\]

Magnitude:

\[
\|\hat v\|
=
\left\|
\frac1{\|\vec v\|}
\vec v
\right\|.
\]

Using scalar homogeneity:

\[
=
\left|
\frac1{\|\vec v\|}
\right|
\|\vec v\|.
\]

Since magnitude is positive for nonzero \(\vec v\):

\[
=
\frac1{\|\vec v\|}
\|\vec v\|.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\|\hat v\|=1.
}
\]

---

# 528.4 Example: \((3,4)\)

Magnitude:

\[
\|\vec v\|
=
5.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\hat v
=
\frac{(3,4)}{5}
\]

so:

\[
\boxed{
\hat v=(0.6,0.8).
}
\]

Check:

\[
\sqrt{0.6^2+0.8^2}
=
\sqrt{0.36+0.64}
=
1.
\]

---

# 528.5 Normalization loses information

This is important.

Both:

\[
(3,4)
\]

and:

\[
(30,40)
\]

normalize to:

\[
(0.6,0.8).
\]

Therefore normalization discards original magnitude.

It is **not invertible** unless you separately store the magnitude.

Conceptually:

```text
vector
├── magnitude
└── direction

normalize
    ↓

direction only
```

If you retain:

\[
m=\|\vec v\|
\]

and:

\[
\hat v=\vec v/m,
\]

then:

\[
\vec v=m\hat v.
\]

So magnitude + normalized direction reconstructs the original vector.

---

# 528.6 Normalization is not linear

A linear operation would need:

\[
N(\vec u+\vec v)
=
N(\vec u)+N(\vec v).
\]

Normalization does not satisfy this.

Take:

\[
\vec u=(1,0)
\]

and:

\[
\vec v=(0,1).
\]

Then:

\[
N(\vec u)=\vec u
\]

and:

\[
N(\vec v)=\vec v.
\]

So:

\[
N(\vec u)+N(\vec v)
=
(1,1).
\]

But:

\[
N(\vec u+\vec v)
=
N(1,1)
=
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2}
\right).
\]

Not equal.

So normalization is a **nonlinear operation**.

---

# 528.7 Positive and negative scaling

If:

\[
a>0,
\]

then:

\[
N(a\vec v)=N(\vec v).
\]

Positive scaling changes magnitude but not direction.

If:

\[
a<0,
\]

then:

\[
N(a\vec v)=-N(\vec v).
\]

Negative scaling reverses orientation.

---

# 528.8 Units disappear

Suppose:

\[
\vec v=(3,4)\text{ metres}.
\]

Magnitude:

\[
5\text{ metres}.
\]

Then:

\[
\frac{\vec v}{\|\vec v\|}
\]

has units:

\[
\frac{\text m}{\text m}=1.
\]

So a normalized direction is dimensionless.

That is exactly what we want when representing pure orientation.

---

# 528.9 Straight implementation

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
Vec3 normalize(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    const float len = std::sqrt(
        v.x * v.x +
        v.y * v.y +
        v.z * v.z
    );

    return {
        v.x / len,
        v.y / len,
        v.z / len
    };
}
```

Mathematically correct for nonzero finite values within safe numerical ranges.

But we can improve its arithmetic structure.

---

# 528.10 Reciprocal once, multiply three times

Instead of three divisions:

```cpp
v.x / len
v.y / len
v.z / len
```

compute:

\[
\frac1{len}
\]

once.

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
Vec3 normalizeUnchecked(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    const float lenSquared =
        v.x * v.x +
        v.y * v.y +
        v.z * v.z;

    const float invLen =
        1.0f / std::sqrt(lenSquared);

    return {
        v.x * invLen,
        v.y * invLen,
        v.z * invLen
    };
}
```

The conceptual sequence:

```text
x²+y²+z²
     ↓
sqrt
     ↓
length
     ↓
1 / length
     ↓
multiply every component
```

---

# 528.11 Why GPUs often expose inverse square root

Since:

\[
\frac1{\|\vec v\|}
=
\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}},
\]

normalization naturally involves inverse square root.

Shader languages and GPU hardware commonly support operations corresponding to `sqrt` and/or reciprocal square root.

This can make normalization efficient.

But do not assume an approximate inverse square root is always acceptable.

Accuracy requirements vary.

---

# 528.12 The famous fast inverse square root

You may encounter the old Quake-style "fast inverse square root."

Its historical importance is real.

But treating it as automatically superior on modern hardware is a mistake.

Modern CPUs/GPUs may have hardware-supported square root or reciprocal-square-root instructions, and compilers may optimize mathematical expressions appropriately.

Use approximations only when:

- error tolerance is understood;
- target hardware benefits;
- profiling demonstrates value.

Do not cargo-cult a historical trick.

---

# 528.13 Where normalization appears

Later you will normalize:

- camera directions;
- light directions;
- surface normals;
- ray directions in some formulations;
- reflection directions;
- tangent-frame axes;
- axes of rotation;
- constraint directions;
- vectors before measuring angular relationships.

Normalization is therefore an important **invariant-establishing operation**.

---

# PART 529 — ZERO-VECTOR NORMALIZATION AND ROBUST HANDLING

This Part is where textbook math becomes engine engineering.

The textbook writes:

\[
\hat v=\frac{\vec v}{\|\vec v\|}
\]

and then quietly says:

\[
\vec v\neq0.
\]

Production code cannot quietly assume that.

---

# 529.1 What happens for the zero vector?

Take:

\[
\vec v=(0,0,0).
\]

Magnitude:

\[
\|\vec v\|=0.
\]

Normalization asks for:

\[
\frac{\vec0}{0}.
\]

Every component requires:

\[
\frac00.
\]

That is undefined mathematically.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the zero vector has no normalized direction}.
}
\]

This makes conceptual sense.

Zero displacement points nowhere.

---

# 529.2 IEEE-754 consequence

With floating-point arithmetic:

```cpp
0.0f / 0.0f
```

typically yields NaN under normal IEEE-754 behavior.

So naive:

```cpp
normalize(Vec3{0,0,0})
```

can produce:

```text
(NaN, NaN, NaN)
```

Those NaNs can propagate:

```text
direction
   ↓
camera matrix
   ↓
world transform
   ↓
vertex transform
   ↓
entire scene becomes invalid
```

A single failed normalization can therefore create spectacular downstream corruption.

---

# 529.3 Where zero vectors arise naturally

This is not a pathological input.

Suppose:

```cpp
direction = target - position;
```

If:

```text
target == position
```

then:

\[
target-position=\vec0.
\]

Or:

```cpp
velocity
```

can legitimately become zero.

Or two identical contact points may produce zero displacement.

So robust normalization must deliberately define behavior.

---

# 529.4 Exact zero versus near zero

Mathematically only:

\[
\vec0
\]

is invalid.

A tiny nonzero vector such as:

\[
(10^{-30},0,0)
\]

has a perfectly valid mathematical direction:

\[
(1,0,0).
\]

But finite-precision arithmetic creates a practical question.

The length computation may:

- underflow;
- lose precision;
- produce unstable reciprocal;
- amplify noise.

So engines often define a **minimum usable length**.

That is an engineering policy, not a mathematical theorem.

---

# 529.5 Why `epsilon` needs thought

You might see:

```cpp
if (lengthSquared(v) < 1e-8f)
    ...
```

But ask:

> 1e-8 of what unit?

If \(\vec v\) is metres, then:

\[
\|\vec v\|^2
\]

has units:

\[
m^2.
\]

So the threshold must also represent squared metres.

If your world units are kilometres or micrometres, the same numerical epsilon means something completely different.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{epsilon is part of a domain policy, not a magical universal constant}.
}
\]

---

# 529.6 Comparing squared length to threshold

If your minimum acceptable length is:

\[
\varepsilon,
\]

then you can compare:

\[
\|\vec v\|^2
\le
\varepsilon^2
\]

rather than computing a square root.

Example:

```cpp
constexpr float minimumLength = 1e-5f;
constexpr float minimumLengthSquared =
    minimumLength * minimumLength;

if (lengthSquared(v) <= minimumLengthSquared) {
    // Too small to normalize under this policy.
}
```

This avoids `sqrt`, but remember the squared computation itself may overflow/underflow for extreme values.

---

# 529.7 Several valid API policies

There is no single correct answer to invalid normalization.

### Policy A: precondition / assertion

```cpp
Vec3 normalize(Vec3 v)
{
    assert(lengthSquared(v) > 0.0f);
    ...
}
```

Good when zero indicates a programmer bug.

Bad when zero is an expected runtime state.

---

### Policy B: return zero

```cpp
Vec3 normalizeOrZero(Vec3 v);
```

If input is unusable:

\[
\vec0\rightarrow\vec0.
\]

Convenient.

But mathematically dangerous because the output is not unit length.

A caller expecting a guaranteed direction can silently receive an invalid one.

---

### Policy C: return fallback

```cpp
Vec3 normalizeOr(Vec3 v, Vec3 fallback);
```

For example, camera direction might retain previous direction.

Useful when the application has a sensible fallback.

---

### Policy D: signal failure

```cpp
std::optional<UnitVec3> tryNormalize(Vec3 v);
```

This represents reality accurately:

> a direction either exists or does not.

For semantic APIs this is often excellent.

---

# 529.8 Robust finite checking

We should also reject NaN and infinity.

```cpp
#include <cmath>

[[nodiscard]]
bool isFinite(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return
        std::isfinite(v.x) &&
        std::isfinite(v.y) &&
        std::isfinite(v.z);
}
```

Why?

Consider:

\[
(+\infty,1,0).
\]

Normalization involves expressions such as:

\[
\frac{\infty}{\infty},
\]

which can become NaN.

A production direction type should usually not silently accept nonfinite components.

---

# 529.9 Robust scaling normalization

We can do even better than the naive square-sum approach.

Let:

\[
m=
\max(|x|,|y|,|z|).
\]

If:

\[
m=0,
\]

all components are zero.

Otherwise scale:

\[
\vec s=\frac{\vec v}{m}.
\]

Every component of \(\vec s\) now has magnitude at most 1.

Compute:

\[
\ell_s=
\sqrt{
s_x^2+s_y^2+s_z^2
}.
\]

Then normalize:

\[
\hat v=
\frac{\vec s}{\ell_s}.
\]

Notice we never need to construct:

\[
x^2
\]

for huge \(x\).

---

# 529.10 Robust implementation

```cpp
#include <algorithm>
#include <cmath>
#include <optional>

struct Vec3 {
    double x{};
    double y{};
    double z{};
};

[[nodiscard]]
std::optional<Vec3>
tryNormalize(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    if (!std::isfinite(v.x) ||
        !std::isfinite(v.y) ||
        !std::isfinite(v.z))
    {
        return std::nullopt;
    }

    const double scale =
        std::max({
            std::abs(v.x),
            std::abs(v.y),
            std::abs(v.z)
        });

    if (scale == 0.0) {
        return std::nullopt;
    }

    const Vec3 scaled{
        v.x / scale,
        v.y / scale,
        v.z / scale
    };

    const double scaledLength =
        std::sqrt(
            scaled.x * scaled.x +
            scaled.y * scaled.y +
            scaled.z * scaled.z
        );

    return Vec3{
        scaled.x / scaledLength,
        scaled.y / scaledLength,
        scaled.z / scaledLength
    };
}
```

---

# 529.11 Trace huge input

Suppose:

\[
\vec v=(10^{300},10^{300},0).
\]

Naive:

\[
(10^{300})^2=10^{600}
\]

overflows `double`.

Robust version chooses:

\[
scale=10^{300}.
\]

Then:

\[
scaled=(1,1,0).
\]

Length:

\[
\sqrt2.
\]

Output:

\[
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2},
0
\right).
\]

Correct direction, without ever calculating \(10^{600}\).

---

# 529.12 What about tiny input?

Suppose:

\[
\vec v=(10^{-300},0,0).
\]

Naive square:

\[
10^{-600}
\]

underflows.

Scaling method:

\[
scale=10^{-300}.
\]

So:

\[
scaled=(1,0,0).
\]

Length:

\[
1.
\]

Result:

\[
(1,0,0).
\]

Again, mathematically correct.

---

# 529.13 But production may still reject tiny input

Numerically *being able* to recover its direction doesn't mean the direction is semantically trustworthy.

Suppose the vector came from:

```text
measured position difference
```

and its magnitude is close to the system's error/noise scale.

Then its direction may be mostly numerical noise.

So an application may still say:

\[
\|\vec v\|<\varepsilon
\Rightarrow
\text{no reliable direction}.
\]

That is a semantic decision.

---

# PART 530 — L1 NORM

Now we broaden the concept.

Euclidean magnitude is one way of measuring vector size.

A **norm** is a mathematical abstraction specifying what a valid notion of vector size must satisfy.

---

# 530.1 General norm definition

A norm is a function:

\[
\|\cdot\|:V\rightarrow\mathbb R
\]

that assigns each vector a real number and satisfies four essential properties.

For vectors \(\vec u,\vec v\) and scalar \(a\):

### 1. Non-negativity

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|\ge0.
}
\]

### 2. Definiteness

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|=0
\iff
\vec v=\vec0.
}
\]

### 3. Absolute homogeneity

\[
\boxed{
\|a\vec v\|
=
|a|\|\vec v\|.
}
\]

### 4. Triangle inequality

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec u+\vec v\|
\le
\|\vec u\|+\|\vec v\|.
}
\]

These rules capture the structural behavior expected from "size."

---

# 530.2 Why triangle inequality?

Suppose:

\[
\vec u
\]

takes you from A to B, and:

\[
\vec v
\]

takes you from B to C.

Then:

\[
\vec u+\vec v
\]

takes you directly A to C.

A sane notion of length should not say the direct route is longer than the sum of the two legs:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec u+\vec v\|
\le
\|\vec u\|+\|\vec v\|.
}
\]

That is the triangle inequality.

---

# 530.3 Definition of the L1 norm

For:

\[
\vec v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n),
\]

define:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_1
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|v_i|.
}
\]

In 2D:

\[
\boxed{
\|(x,y)\|_1
=
|x|+|y|.
}
\]

In 3D:

\[
\boxed{
\|(x,y,z)\|_1
=
|x|+|y|+|z|.
}
\]

---

# 530.4 Example

Take:

\[
\vec v=(-3,4,-2).
\]

Then:

\[
\|\vec v\|_1
=
|-3|+|4|+|-2|.
\]

Therefore:

\[
3+4+2=9.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_1=9.
}
\]

---

# 530.5 Why call it L1?

The exponent "1" comes from a larger family:

\[
\|\vec v\|_p
=
\left(
\sum_i |v_i|^p
\right)^{1/p}.
\]

Setting:

\[
p=1
\]

gives:

\[
\sum_i |v_i|.
\]

We are not turning this into a separate \(L_p\) lesson; the expression only explains where the naming comes from.

---

# 530.6 Prove L1 is a norm

## Non-negativity

Every:

\[
|v_i|\ge0.
\]

A sum of nonnegative values is nonnegative:

\[
\|\vec v\|_1\ge0.
\]

---

## Definiteness

If:

\[
\sum_i|v_i|=0,
\]

then every nonnegative term must be zero.

So:

\[
|v_i|=0
\Rightarrow
v_i=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\vec v=\vec0.
\]

---

## Homogeneity

\[
\|a\vec v\|_1
=
\sum_i|av_i|.
\]

Since:

\[
|ab|=|a||b|,
\]

we get:

\[
=
\sum_i|a||v_i|
\]

\[
=
|a|\sum_i|v_i|.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\|a\vec v\|_1
=
|a|\|\vec v\|_1.
}
\]

---

# 530.7 Triangle inequality for L1

Start with:

\[
\|\vec u+\vec v\|_1
=
\sum_i|u_i+v_i|.
\]

Scalar absolute value obeys:

\[
|a+b|
\le
|a|+|b|.
\]

Therefore:

\[
|u_i+v_i|
\le
|u_i|+|v_i|.
\]

Sum all components:

\[
\sum_i|u_i+v_i|
\le
\sum_i|u_i|
+
\sum_i|v_i|.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec u+\vec v\|_1
\le
\|\vec u\|_1+\|\vec v\|_1.
}
\]

So L1 satisfies all norm axioms.

---

# 530.8 Geometry of the L1 unit ball

Consider every 2D vector satisfying:

\[
\|\vec v\|_1\le1.
\]

So:

\[
|x|+|y|\le1.
\]

The boundary goes through:

\[
(1,0),
\quad
(0,1),
\quad
(-1,0),
\quad
(0,-1).
\]

Shape:

```text
        (0,1)
          ●
         / \
        /   \
(-1,0)●     ●(1,0)
        \   /
         \ /
          ●
        (0,-1)
```

A diamond.

Compare that to the circular Euclidean unit ball.

Different norm → different geometry.

---

# 530.9 Why L1 is called Manhattan distance in coordinate geometry

Imagine city streets arranged in an orthogonal grid.

To move from:

\[
(0,0)
\]

to:

\[
(3,4),
\]

if you can move only horizontally and vertically:

\[
3+4=7.
\]

That corresponds to:

\[
\|(3,4)\|_1=7.
\]

Hence:

```text
Manhattan norm / Manhattan distance
taxicab geometry
```

---

# 530.10 L1 is not rotationally invariant

Take:

\[
\vec v=(1,0).
\]

Then:

\[
\|\vec v\|_1=1.
\]

Rotate by \(45^\circ\):

\[
\vec v'
=
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2}
\right).
\]

Its L1 norm is:

\[
\frac1{\sqrt2}
+
\frac1{\sqrt2}
=
\sqrt2.
\]

So rotating the vector changed its L1 norm.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
L_1
\text{ depends on the orientation of the coordinate axes}.
}
\]

This is a major difference from Euclidean length.

---

# 530.11 Implementation

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
constexpr float l1Norm(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return
        std::abs(v.x) +
        std::abs(v.y) +
        std::abs(v.z);
}
```

No square root.

Computational structure:

```text
3 absolute values
2 additions
```

for `Vec3`.

Highly straightforward to vectorize in batch processing.

---

# PART 531 — L2 NORM

Now we formally identify our familiar Euclidean magnitude as the **L2 norm**.

---

# 531.1 Definition

For:

\[
\vec v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n),
\]

define:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_2
=
\left(
\sum_i |v_i|^2
\right)^{1/2}.
}
\]

Because:

\[
|v_i|^2=v_i^2
\]

for real numbers:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_2
=
\sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2}.
}
\]

This is exactly Part 526's Euclidean magnitude.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{ordinary Euclidean magnitude}=L_2\text{ norm}.
}
\]

---

# 531.2 We already proved three norm properties

Non-negativity:

\[
\|\vec v\|_2\ge0.
\]

Definiteness:

\[
\|\vec v\|_2=0
\iff
\vec v=0.
\]

Homogeneity:

\[
\|a\vec v\|_2
=
|a|\|\vec v\|_2.
\]

The difficult property is the triangle inequality.

Let's actually prove it.

---

# 531.3 A scalar inequality we need

We need to show:

\[
\|\vec x+\vec y\|_2
\le
\|\vec x\|_2+\|\vec y\|_2.
\]

An important intermediate result is:

\[
\boxed{
\left|
\sum_i x_i y_i
\right|
\le
\sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}
\sqrt{\sum_i y_i^2}.
}
\]

Later you will recognize this as the **Cauchy–Schwarz inequality** expressed through what Part 534 will call the dot product.

We can derive it without assuming dot-product theory.

---

# 531.4 Deriving the scalar-sum inequality

Consider for any real \(t\):

\[
\sum_i(tx_i-y_i)^2.
\]

Every squared term is nonnegative, so:

\[
\sum_i(tx_i-y_i)^2\ge0.
\]

Expand:

\[
\sum_i
(t^2x_i^2-2tx_iy_i+y_i^2).
\]

Group:

\[
t^2\sum_i x_i^2
-
2t\sum_i x_iy_i
+
\sum_i y_i^2
\ge0.
\]

Define:

\[
A=\sum_i x_i^2,
\]

\[
B=\sum_i x_iy_i,
\]

\[
C=\sum_i y_i^2.
\]

Then:

\[
At^2-2Bt+C\ge0
\]

for every real \(t\).

A quadratic that is never negative cannot have two distinct real roots, so its discriminant must satisfy:

\[
(-2B)^2-4AC\le0.
\]

Thus:

\[
4B^2\le4AC.
\]

Divide by 4:

\[
B^2\le AC.
\]

Substitute definitions:

\[
\left(
\sum_i x_i y_i
\right)^2
\le
\left(
\sum_i x_i^2
\right)
\left(
\sum_i y_i^2
\right).
\]

Take nonnegative square roots:

\[
\boxed{
\left|
\sum_i x_i y_i
\right|
\le
\|\vec x\|_2\|\vec y\|_2.
}
\]

We now have what we need.

---

# 531.5 Prove L2 triangle inequality

Start:

\[
\|\vec x+\vec y\|_2^2
=
\sum_i(x_i+y_i)^2.
\]

Expand:

\[
=
\sum_i
(x_i^2+2x_iy_i+y_i^2).
\]

Regroup:

\[
=
\sum_i x_i^2
+
2\sum_i x_iy_i
+
\sum_i y_i^2.
\]

Using the inequality above:

\[
\sum_i x_i y_i
\le
\|\vec x\|_2\|\vec y\|_2.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\|\vec x+\vec y\|_2^2
\le
\|\vec x\|_2^2
+
2\|\vec x\|_2\|\vec y\|_2
+
\|\vec y\|_2^2.
\]

Right side factors:

\[
=
(
\|\vec x\|_2+
\|\vec y\|_2
)^2.
\]

Both sides are nonnegative, so taking square roots preserves ordering:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec x+\vec y\|_2
\le
\|\vec x\|_2+\|\vec y\|_2.
}
\]

Thus L2 satisfies the fourth norm axiom.

---

# 531.6 Geometry

In 2D:

\[
\|\vec v\|_2=1
\]

means:

\[
x^2+y^2=1.
\]

That is a circle.

In 3D:

\[
x^2+y^2+z^2=1
\]

is a sphere.

So the L2 unit ball is:

```text
2D → disk
3D → sphere interior
nD → hypersphere interior
```

---

# 531.7 Why Euclidean geometry prefers L2

L2 treats all orthonormal directions isotropically.

"Isotropic" here means:

> no preferred spatial direction.

A Euclidean rotation does not change Euclidean magnitude.

In 2D, Part 503 already showed that a proper rotation preserves:

\[
x^2+y^2.
\]

L1 did not have that property.

L∞ will not either.

That rotational symmetry is why L2 corresponds naturally to ordinary physical straight-line geometry.

---

# 531.8 Implementation

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
float l2Norm(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return std::sqrt(
        v.x * v.x +
        v.y * v.y +
        v.z * v.z
    );
}
```

Or robustly:

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
double l2Norm(Vec3d v) noexcept
{
    return std::hypot(v.x, v.y, v.z);
}
```

Part 526 and Part 531 therefore describe the same formula from two different levels:

**526:** geometric magnitude.  
**531:** that magnitude as one member of the general norm concept.

---

# PART 532 — L-INFINITY NORM

The third important norm asks:

> What is the largest absolute component?

---

# 532.1 Formal definition

For:

\[
\vec v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n),
\]

define:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_\infty
=
\max_i |v_i|.
}
\]

In 3D:

\[
\boxed{
\|(x,y,z)\|_\infty
=
\max(|x|,|y|,|z|).
}
\]

---

# 532.2 Example

Take:

\[
\vec v=(-3,8,-5).
\]

Absolute values:

\[
3,\quad8,\quad5.
\]

Largest:

\[
8.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_\infty=8.
}
\]

---

# 532.3 Why is it called infinity norm?

This name is not arbitrary.

Recall the \(p\)-norm expression:

\[
\|\vec v\|_p
=
\left(
\sum_i|v_i|^p
\right)^{1/p}.
\]

Let:

\[
M=\max_i|v_i|.
\]

At least one component has magnitude \(M\), so:

\[
M^p
\le
\sum_i|v_i|^p.
\]

Taking \(1/p\) power:

\[
M
\le
\|\vec v\|_p.
\]

Also every:

\[
|v_i|\le M.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\sum_i|v_i|^p
\le
nM^p.
\]

Take \(1/p\) power:

\[
\|\vec v\|_p
\le
n^{1/p}M.
\]

So:

\[
M
\le
\|\vec v\|_p
\le
n^{1/p}M.
\]

As:

\[
p\rightarrow\infty,
\]

we have:

\[
n^{1/p}\rightarrow1.
\]

The lower and upper bounds both approach \(M\).

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\lim_{p\to\infty}\|\vec v\|_p
=
M
=
\max_i|v_i|.
}
\]

That is why the notation is:

\[
L_\infty.
\]

---

# 532.4 Prove it is a norm

### Non-negativity

Absolute values are nonnegative, so their maximum is nonnegative.

### Definiteness

If:

\[
\max_i|v_i|=0,
\]

then every:

\[
|v_i|=0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\vec v=\vec0.
\]

### Homogeneity

\[
\|a\vec v\|_\infty
=
\max_i|av_i|
\]

\[
=
\max_i
(|a||v_i|)
\]

\[
=
|a|\max_i|v_i|.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\|a\vec v\|_\infty
=
|a|\|\vec v\|_\infty.
}
\]

---

# 532.5 Triangle inequality

For every component:

\[
|u_i+v_i|
\le
|u_i|+|v_i|.
\]

Also:

\[
|u_i|
\le
\|\vec u\|_\infty
\]

and:

\[
|v_i|
\le
\|\vec v\|_\infty.
\]

Therefore:

\[
|u_i+v_i|
\le
\|\vec u\|_\infty+
\|\vec v\|_\infty.
\]

Since this holds for every component, their maximum also satisfies:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec u+\vec v\|_\infty
\le
\|\vec u\|_\infty+
\|\vec v\|_\infty.
}
\]

Thus L∞ is a valid norm.

---

# 532.6 Geometry of the unit ball

In 2D:

\[
\|(x,y)\|_\infty\le1
\]

means:

\[
\max(|x|,|y|)\le1.
\]

Therefore:

\[
|x|\le1
\]

and:

\[
|y|\le1.
\]

That is the square:

```text
(-1,1) ┌─────────┐ (1,1)
       │         │
       │         │
       │         │
(-1,-1)└─────────┘ (1,-1)
```

In 3D the unit ball is an axis-aligned cube.

This is a beautiful connection to graphics:

\[
\boxed{
L_\infty\text{ balls are axis-aligned boxes}.
}
\]

---

# 532.7 Grid interpretation

Suppose a chess king can move one square horizontally, vertically, or diagonally per move.

From:

\[
(0,0)
\]

to:

\[
(3,5),
\]

the king can move diagonally for three steps:

\[
(0,0)\rightarrow(1,1)\rightarrow(2,2)\rightarrow(3,3)
\]

then two steps vertically:

\[
(3,4)\rightarrow(3,5).
\]

Total:

\[
5.
\]

And:

\[
\|(3,5)\|_\infty
=
\max(3,5)
=
5.
\]

That is why L∞ distance is also called **Chebyshev distance** in grid contexts.

---

# 532.8 L∞ is coordinate-axis dependent

As with L1, L∞ is not rotationally invariant.

Take:

\[
(1,0).
\]

Then:

\[
\|(1,0)\|_\infty=1.
\]

Rotate \(45^\circ\):

\[
\left(
\frac1{\sqrt2},
\frac1{\sqrt2}
\right).
\]

Then:

\[
\|\cdot\|_\infty
=
\frac1{\sqrt2}
\approx0.707.
\]

So the norm changed.

Again, the coordinate axes matter.

---

# 532.9 Implementation

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
constexpr float lInfinityNorm(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return std::max({
        std::abs(v.x),
        std::abs(v.y),
        std::abs(v.z)
    });
}
```

For three components:

```text
3 absolute values
2 max operations
```

No square root.

# PART 533 — DISTANCE INDUCED BY A NORM

Now the entire block comes together.

A norm measures the size of a **vector**.

Points are not vectors.

But Part 521 taught us that subtracting two points gives a displacement vector:

\[
Q-P.
\]

So define distance between points as the norm of their displacement.

---

# 533.1 Formal definition

Given norm:

\[
\|\cdot\|,
\]

define:

\[
\boxed{
d(P,Q)
=
\|Q-P\|.
}
\]

This is called the **distance induced by the norm**.

---

# 533.2 Why the subtraction appears

A point has location.

A norm expects a vector.

Therefore we cannot directly write:

\[
\|P\|
\]

as an intrinsic point operation unless an origin has been chosen.

But:

\[
Q-P
\]

is intrinsically the displacement from P to Q.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{point}
-
\text{point}
\rightarrow
\text{vector}
\rightarrow
\text{norm}
\rightarrow
\text{distance}.
}
\]

That is the correct semantic chain.

---

# 533.3 Euclidean distance

Using L2:

\[
d_2(P,Q)
=
\|Q-P\|_2.
\]

For:

\[
P=(p_x,p_y)
\]

and:

\[
Q=(q_x,q_y),
\]

displacement:

\[
Q-P
=
(q_x-p_x,q_y-p_y).
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
d_2(P,Q)
=
\sqrt{
(q_x-p_x)^2+
(q_y-p_y)^2
}.
}
\]

In 3D:

\[
\boxed{
d_2(P,Q)
=
\sqrt{
(q_x-p_x)^2+
(q_y-p_y)^2+
(q_z-p_z)^2
}.
}
\]

---

# 533.4 L1 distance

Using L1:

\[
\boxed{
d_1(P,Q)
=
\sum_i|q_i-p_i|.
}
\]

For:

\[
P=(1,2),
\quad
Q=(4,6),
\]

difference:

\[
(3,4).
\]

L1 distance:

\[
3+4=7.
\]

---

# 533.5 L∞ distance

Using L∞:

\[
\boxed{
d_\infty(P,Q)
=
\max_i|q_i-p_i|.
}
\]

Same example:

\[
Q-P=(3,4).
\]

Therefore:

\[
d_\infty(P,Q)=4.
\]

And Euclidean:

\[
d_2(P,Q)=5.
\]

So the same two points have:

\[
\boxed{
d_1=7,\qquad
d_2=5,\qquad
d_\infty=4.
}
\]

There is no contradiction.

We changed the geometry by changing the norm.

---

# 533.6 A proper metric

A mathematical distance function, called a **metric**, needs several properties.

For points \(P,Q,R\):

### Non-negativity

\[
d(P,Q)\ge0.
\]

### Identity of indiscernibles

\[
d(P,Q)=0
\iff
P=Q.
\]

### Symmetry

\[
d(P,Q)=d(Q,P).
\]

### Triangle inequality

\[
d(P,R)
\le
d(P,Q)+d(Q,R).
\]

Let's derive all four from the norm axioms.

---

# 533.7 Non-negativity

\[
d(P,Q)=\|Q-P\|.
\]

A norm is nonnegative.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
d(P,Q)\ge0.
}
\]

---

# 533.8 Distance zero iff points coincide

\[
d(P,Q)=0
\]

means:

\[
\|Q-P\|=0.
\]

Norm definiteness says:

\[
Q-P=\vec0.
\]

Therefore:

\[
Q=P.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
d(P,Q)=0
\iff
P=Q.
}
\]

---

# 533.9 Symmetry

\[
d(P,Q)
=
\|Q-P\|.
\]

But:

\[
P-Q=-(Q-P).
\]

A norm satisfies:

\[
\|-\vec v\|
=
|-1|\|\vec v\|
=
\|\vec v\|.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\|Q-P\|
=
\|P-Q\|.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
d(P,Q)=d(Q,P).
}
\]

---

# 533.10 Triangle inequality

Point differences obey:

\[
R-P
=
(R-Q)+(Q-P).
\]

Take norm:

\[
\|R-P\|
=
\|(R-Q)+(Q-P)\|.
\]

Norm triangle inequality gives:

\[
\le
\|R-Q\|+\|Q-P\|.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
d(P,R)
\le
d(P,Q)+d(Q,R).
}
\]

So every norm automatically induces a valid metric through vector differences.

---

# 533.11 Translation invariance

Shift every point by the same displacement:

\[
P'=P+\vec t
\]

and:

\[
Q'=Q+\vec t.
\]

Then:

\[
Q'-P'
=
(Q+\vec t)-(P+\vec t)
=
Q-P.
\]

Therefore:

\[
d(P',Q')
=
\|Q-P\|
=
d(P,Q).
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{norm-induced distances are translation invariant}.
}
\]

This connects directly back to affine geometry.

---

# 533.12 Different norms can change which object is "nearest"

This matters tremendously.

Let query point:

\[
O=(0,0).
\]

Candidate A:

\[
A=(3,0).
\]

Candidate B:

\[
B=(2,2).
\]

### L2

\[
d_2(O,A)=3.
\]

\[
d_2(O,B)=\sqrt8\approx2.828.
\]

So under Euclidean distance:

\[
B\text{ is closer}.
\]

### L1

\[
d_1(O,A)=3.
\]

\[
d_1(O,B)=4.
\]

So under L1:

\[
A\text{ is closer}.
\]

### L∞

\[
d_\infty(O,A)=3.
\]

\[
d_\infty(O,B)=2.
\]

So under L∞:

\[
B\text{ is closer}.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{"nearest" has no meaning until the distance metric is specified}.
}
\]

---

# 533.13 Distance balls

A radius-\(r\) neighborhood around point \(P\) means:

\[
\{Q:d(P,Q)\le r\}.
\]

With L2 in 2D:

```text
circle
```

With L1:

```text
diamond
```

With L∞:

```text
axis-aligned square
```

In 3D:

```text
L2 → sphere
L1 → octahedral shape
L∞ → cube
```

So selecting a norm changes the shape of "within radius."

---

# 533.14 This directly affects algorithms

Suppose your code says:

```cpp
if (distance(player, object) < radius)
```

You must ask:

> Which distance?

For ordinary physical proximity:

\[
L_2
\]

is usually intended.

For grid movement with horizontal/vertical cost:

\[
L_1
\]

may model the problem better.

For maximum coordinate error or axis-aligned neighborhoods:

\[
L_\infty
\]

may be natural.

The algorithm should use a metric matching the domain geometry.

---

# 533.15 Coordinate spaces must match

This is invalid:

```cpp
distance(worldPoint, localPoint);
```

unless one point is first transformed into the other's coordinate space.

Why?

Because:

\[
Q-P
\]

only has geometric meaning when the coordinates describe positions relative to compatible frames.

So before distance:

```text
same semantic quantity?
        ↓
same coordinate space?
        ↓
same units?
        ↓
subtract
        ↓
norm
```

---

# 533.16 Units must match

Imagine vector:

\[
(x,y)
\]

where:

```text
x = metres
y = seconds
```

Then:

\[
\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
\]

attempts to add:

\[
m^2+s^2,
\]

which is physically meaningless.

This can occur in optimization/state vectors where components represent different physical quantities.

Such systems often need:

- nondimensionalization;
- scaling;
- weighted norms;
- domain-specific metrics.

Never blindly apply Euclidean norm to heterogeneous units.

---

# 533.17 Canonical C++ implementation

```cpp
struct Vec3 {
    float x{};
    float y{};
    float z{};
};

[[nodiscard]]
constexpr Vec3 operator-(Vec3 a, Vec3 b) noexcept
{
    return {
        a.x - b.x,
        a.y - b.y,
        a.z - b.z
    };
}

[[nodiscard]]
constexpr float l1Norm(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return
        std::abs(v.x) +
        std::abs(v.y) +
        std::abs(v.z);
}

[[nodiscard]]
float l2Norm(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return std::sqrt(
        v.x * v.x +
        v.y * v.y +
        v.z * v.z
    );
}

[[nodiscard]]
constexpr float lInfinityNorm(Vec3 v) noexcept
{
    return std::max({
        std::abs(v.x),
        std::abs(v.y),
        std::abs(v.z)
    });
}
```

Then:

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
float euclideanDistance(Vec3 a, Vec3 b) noexcept
{
    return l2Norm(b - a);
}
```

Likewise:

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
float manhattanDistance(Vec3 a, Vec3 b) noexcept
{
    return l1Norm(b - a);
}
```

and:

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
float chebyshevDistance(Vec3 a, Vec3 b) noexcept
{
    return lInfinityNorm(b - a);
}
```

# HOW THE THREE NORMS RELATE

For any finite-dimensional vector:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_\infty
\le
\|\vec v\|_2
\le
\|\vec v\|_1.
}
\]

Let's understand why.

---

## Why \(L_\infty\le L_2\)

Let:

\[
M=\max_i|v_i|.
\]

One component has squared magnitude:

\[
M^2.
\]

And:

\[
\sum_i v_i^2
\]

contains that term plus other nonnegative terms.

So:

\[
M^2
\le
\sum_i v_i^2.
\]

Taking square roots:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_\infty
\le
\|\vec v\|_2.
}
\]

---

## Why \(L_2\le L_1\)

We have:

\[
\left(
\sum_i|v_i|
\right)^2
\]

which expands into:

\[
\sum_i v_i^2
+
2\sum_{i<j}|v_i||v_j|.
\]

Every cross term is nonnegative.

Therefore:

\[
\left(
\sum_i|v_i|
\right)^2
\ge
\sum_i v_i^2.
\]

Take square roots:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_1
\ge
\|\vec v\|_2.
}
\]

---

# Stronger finite-dimensional bounds

For dimension \(n\):

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_1
\le
\sqrt n\,\|\vec v\|_2
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_2
\le
\sqrt n\,\|\vec v\|_\infty.
}
\]

Also:

\[
\boxed{
\|\vec v\|_1
\le
n\|\vec v\|_\infty.
}
\]

These relationships tell us that while the norms define different geometry, in fixed finite dimension they bound one another.

---

# ONE VECTOR UNDER ALL THREE NORMS

Take:

\[
\vec v=(3,-4,12).
\]

### L1

\[
\|\vec v\|_1
=
3+4+12
=
\boxed{19}.
\]

### L2

\[
\|\vec v\|_2
=
\sqrt{9+16+144}
=
\boxed{13}.
\]

### L∞

\[
\|\vec v\|_\infty
=
\max(3,4,12)
=
\boxed{12}.
\]

And indeed:

\[
12\le13\le19.
\]

Exactly as expected.

---

# PERFORMANCE COMPARISON

For a `Vec3`, ignoring loads:

| Operation | Typical scalar work |
|---|---|
| L1 | 3 abs + 2 adds |
| L2 squared | 3 mul + 2 adds |
| L2 | 3 mul + 2 adds + sqrt |
| L∞ | 3 abs + 2 max |
| normalize | L2 work + reciprocal + 3 mul |
| L2 distance | 3 subtract + L2 |
| L2 squared distance | 3 subtract + L2 squared |

This does **not** automatically imply L∞ is always faster than L2 in meaningful application code.

Real performance depends on:

- instruction throughput;
- compiler optimization;
- vectorization;
- memory bandwidth;
- batching;
- surrounding algorithm;
- target CPU/GPU.

At millions of vectors, memory layout can dominate the arithmetic itself.

---

# SIMD / GPU VIEW

Suppose we have millions of vectors:

```text
v0
v1
v2
v3
...
```

Each norm operation is independent.

That means batch evaluation is highly parallelizable.

CPU SIMD can process multiple components/vectors together.

GPU threads/invocations can each process separate vectors.

There are generally no:

- heap allocations;
- synchronization requirements;
- pointer chasing;
- branches in the happy path.

This is ideal data-parallel arithmetic.

For large arrays, however, moving vector data from memory may cost as much or more than the few arithmetic instructions.

---

# MEMORY MODEL

None of these norms require permanent extra memory.

A vector remains:

```text
Vec3
┌────────────┐
│ x : float  │
├────────────┤
│ y : float  │
├────────────┤
│ z : float  │
└────────────┘
```

The norm is derived transiently:

```text
load x
load y
load z
    ↓
arithmetic
    ↓
one scalar output
```

Normalization produces another vector:

```text
input Vec3
    ↓
calculate norm
    ↓
calculate inverse length
    ↓
scale components
    ↓
output Vec3
```

No heap ownership is involved.

---

# TEXTBOOK VERSUS PRODUCTION IMPLEMENTATION

A textbook implementation might be:

```cpp
return v / std::sqrt(v.x*v.x + v.y*v.y + v.z*v.z);
```

A production engine may need decisions about:

```text
What happens for zero?
What happens for almost-zero?
What happens for NaN?
What happens for infinity?
Is direct length or robust hypot used?
Are vectors float or double?
Are directions required to stay normalized?
Should invalid input assert?
Should failure return optional?
Should we normalize every frame?
Can squared length avoid sqrt?
Are values bounded enough that overflow cannot occur?
Does coordinate scale make the epsilon sensible?
```

Those are not "extra details."

Those are what separate mathematical literacy from implementation ownership.

---

# A STRONG SEMANTIC DIRECTION TYPE

A canonical/reference design could enforce unit length:

```cpp
#include <optional>

struct UnitVec3 {
private:
    Vec3 value_;

    explicit UnitVec3(Vec3 v) noexcept
        : value_(v)
    {}

public:
    [[nodiscard]]
    static std::optional<UnitVec3>
    from(Vec3 v) noexcept
    {
        auto normalized = tryNormalize(v);

        if (!normalized) {
            return std::nullopt;
        }

        return UnitVec3{*normalized};
    }

    [[nodiscard]]
    Vec3 value() const noexcept
    {
        return value_;
    }
};
```

Then functions requiring a direction can state their invariant through the type:

```cpp
void setCameraForward(UnitVec3 forward);
```

instead of:

```cpp
void setCameraForward(Vec3 forward);
```

The latter cannot tell the caller whether zero or non-unit input is legal.

This is a **canonical design pattern**, not a claim about any specific project implementation.

---

# TESTING THIS MATH LIBRARY

A serious vector math library should test much more than:

```cpp
length({3,4,0}) == 5
```

You want structured cases.

For example:

```cpp
// Axis vectors.
assert(length(Vec3{1,0,0}) == 1.0f);

// 3-4-5.
assert(length(Vec3{3,4,0}) == 5.0f);

// Negation preserves magnitude.
assert(length(Vec3{-3,-4,0}) == 5.0f);

// Squared magnitude.
assert(lengthSquared(Vec3{3,4,0}) == 25.0f);

// L1.
assert(l1Norm(Vec3{-3,4,-2}) == 9.0f);

// L-infinity.
assert(lInfinityNorm(Vec3{-3,8,-5}) == 8.0f);
```

Normalization:

```cpp
auto u = tryNormalize(Vec3{3,4,0});

assert(u.has_value());

const float ls = lengthSquared(*u);

// Approximate check required because of floating point.
assert(std::abs(ls - 1.0f) < tolerance);
```

Zero:

```cpp
assert(!tryNormalize(Vec3{0,0,0}));
```

Nonfinite:

```cpp
assert(!tryNormalize(
    Vec3{
        std::numeric_limits<double>::infinity(),
        0,
        0
    }
));
```

And robust implementations should be tested with extremely large and tiny finite inputs.

---

# DEBUGGING ATTACK TREE

Suppose a direction becomes NaN.

Do not start randomly inserting epsilons.

Trace:

```text
Where was direction created?
        ↓
Was it produced by normalization?
        ↓
What was the input vector?
        ↓
Was it zero?
        ↓
Was it nearly zero?
        ↓
Were any components already NaN/Inf?
        ↓
Did lengthSquared overflow?
        ↓
Did lengthSquared underflow?
        ↓
Did sqrt receive invalid data?
        ↓
Was division by zero performed?
        ↓
What policy should the caller actually use?
```

That is systematic debugging.

# WHY THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN MAGNITUDE, NORM, AND DISTANCE MATTERS

These three words are related but not identical.

A **norm** is a function satisfying the norm axioms:

\[
\|\cdot\|.
\]

The **magnitude** of a vector usually refers to its size under the chosen norm, and in ordinary graphics almost always means Euclidean/L2 magnitude unless otherwise specified.

A **distance** compares two points:

\[
d(P,Q)=\|Q-P\|.
\]

So:

```text
vector
    ↓ norm
scalar magnitude

point - point
    ↓
vector displacement
    ↓ norm
scalar distance
```

That chain should now be automatic.

---

# WHAT THE SHAPES SHOULD LOOK LIKE IN YOUR HEAD

For radius \(1\) around the origin in 2D:

```text
L1

      /\
     /  \
    /    \
    \    /
     \  /
      \/


L2

     ****
   **    **
  *        *
  *        *
   **    **
     ****


L∞

  ┌────────┐
  │        │
  │        │
  │        │
  └────────┘
```

They are three legitimate definitions of "one unit away."

This is not cosmetic.

It changes:

- neighborhood shape;
- nearest point;
- path cost;
- optimization behavior;
- algorithm semantics.

---

# DO NOT CONFUSE THESE EIGHT IDEAS

\[
\|\vec v\|
\]

is magnitude.

\[
\|\vec v\|^2
\]

is squared magnitude.

\[
\frac{\vec v}{\|\vec v\|}
\]

is normalization.

\[
\vec0/\|\vec0\|
\]

is undefined.

\[
\|\vec v\|_1
\]

is sum of absolute components.

\[
\|\vec v\|_2
\]

is Euclidean magnitude.

\[
\|\vec v\|_\infty
\]

is maximum absolute component.

And:

\[
\|Q-P\|
\]

is distance induced by a norm.

If these collapse into "some vector length stuff," Parts 534 onward become fragile.
