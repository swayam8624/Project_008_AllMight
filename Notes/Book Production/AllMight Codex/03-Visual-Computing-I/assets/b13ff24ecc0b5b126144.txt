# PART 564 — RIGHT-HANDED COORDINATE SYSTEMS

## 564.1 What "right-handed" actually means

From Part 563, take an ordered independent basis:

\[
B=(b_1,b_2,b_3).
\]

Its orientation can be tested through the scalar triple product:

\[
H=(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3.
\]

Using the standard right-hand-oriented cross product, if:

\[
\boxed{H>0}
\]

the ordered basis is **right-handed**.

For an orthonormal basis, the magnitude must be exactly one mathematically:

\[
H=+1.
\]

For the standard Cartesian basis:

\[
e_x=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
e_y=(0,1,0),
\]

\[
e_z=(0,0,1),
\]

we have:

\[
e_x\times e_y=e_z.
\]

Therefore:

\[
(e_x\times e_y)\cdot e_z
=
e_z\cdot e_z
=
1.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{(e_x,e_y,e_z)\text{ is right-handed}.}
\]

---

# 564.2 The hand mnemonic is not the definition

You can use your right hand:

```text
index  → +X
middle → +Y
thumb  → +Z
```

depending on the physical mnemonic you prefer.

But the actual mathematical test is stronger:

\[
\boxed{
b_1\times b_2
\text{ points toward }b_3
}
\]

or equivalently:

\[
\boxed{
(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3>0.
}
\]

The hand gesture helps you remember the orientation relation.

It does not replace the relation.

---

# 564.3 Handedness belongs to an ordered basis

This matters.

Take the set:

\[
\{e_x,e_y,e_z\}.
\]

Now order it:

\[
(e_x,e_y,e_z).
\]

Right-handed.

Swap the first two:

\[
(e_y,e_x,e_z).
\]

Then:

\[
e_y\times e_x=-e_z.
\]

So:

\[
(e_y\times e_x)\cdot e_z=-1.
\]

That ordered basis is left-handed.

Same three geometric axes.

Different ordering.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{handedness is not merely a property of three unlabeled lines}.
}
\]

It depends on their ordered orientation.

---

# 564.4 Cyclic permutations preserve handedness

Start:

\[
(e_x,e_y,e_z).
\]

Cycle:

\[
(e_y,e_z,e_x).
\]

Check:

\[
e_y\times e_z=e_x.
\]

Still positive.

Cycle again:

\[
(e_z,e_x,e_y).
\]

And:

\[
e_z\times e_x=e_y.
\]

Still positive.

Thus cyclic permutations preserve orientation.

But swapping any single pair reverses it.

This mirrors the scalar triple product properties from Parts 548–549.

---

# 564.5 Right-handed does not mean "Y is up"

This misconception causes endless confusion.

Consider one right-handed system where:

\[
+Y
\]

is called "up."

You can also construct a perfectly valid right-handed system where:

\[
+Z
\]

is called "up."

Handedness says nothing by itself about which semantic role is assigned to an axis.

You need separate answers to questions such as:

\[
\text{Which axis is up?}
\]

\[
\text{Which axis is forward?}
\]

\[
\text{Which axis is right?}
\]

and:

\[
\text{What is their orientation order?}
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{up-axis convention}
\neq
\text{handedness}.
}
\]

---

# 564.6 Right-handed does not uniquely determine "forward"

Suppose world basis is standard:

\[
X=\text{right},
\qquad
Y=\text{up},
\qquad
Z=\text{some third axis}.
\]

The basis may be right-handed.

A camera system can nevertheless choose its *viewing direction* to be either \(+Z\) or \(-Z\), provided all camera-space conventions are built consistently.

So statements such as:

> right-handed means forward must be \(-Z\)

are too crude.

What may be true is that a **specific camera convention** uses:

\[
+X=\text{camera right},
\quad
+Y=\text{camera up},
\quad
-Z=\text{camera forward}.
\]

Then the positive third coordinate axis is actually camera **backward**:

\[
+Z=\text{backward}.
\]

The underlying ordered coordinate basis:

\[
(+X,+Y,+Z)
\]

can still be right-handed.

That distinction is essential.

---

# 564.7 Coordinates are not the physical arrows themselves

Suppose a point has coordinates:

\[
(2,3,5).
\]

Those numbers mean:

\[
P-O
=
2b_x+3b_y+5b_z.
\]

If the basis changes handedness, the same tuple can represent a different geometric displacement.

For example, define:

\[
b_x'=b_x,
\]

\[
b_y'=b_y,
\]

\[
b_z'=-b_z.
\]

Then tuple:

\[
(2,3,5)_{B'}
\]

represents:

\[
2b_x+3b_y-5b_z
\]

in the original basis.

Thus converting handedness is not:

> relabel the same numbers and hope.

You must understand which basis direction was reflected.

---

# 564.8 A handedness change requires orientation reversal

Can a pure rotation convert a right-handed frame into a left-handed frame?

No.

A rotation preserves orientation.

Imagine physically rotating your coordinate tripod.

No matter how you turn it, the X-Y-Z handed relationship remains the same.

To reverse handedness, you need an **orientation-reversing operation**, such as reflecting one axis:

\[
(x,y,z)
\mapsto
(x,y,-z).
\]

This corresponds to scaling one basis direction by \(-1\).

Its geometric effect is a mirror reflection.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{right-handed}\rightarrow\text{left-handed}
}
\]

cannot be accomplished by a proper rotation alone.

---

# 564.9 What happens to the cross product under a mirrored basis?

This is subtle.

Suppose original basis is right-handed:

\[
e_x\times e_y=e_z.
\]

Now define mirrored basis:

\[
e_x'=e_x,
\]

\[
e_y'=e_y,
\]

\[
e_z'=-e_z.
\]

Then physically:

\[
e_x'\times e_y'
=
e_z
=
-e_z'.
\]

So relative to the new ordered basis:

\[
\boxed{
e_x'\times e_y'=-e_z'.
}
\]

That is exactly the signature of a left-handed basis.

The geometric cross product did not "break."

The orientation of the basis changed.

---

# 564.10 Cross-product component formulas contain an orientation convention

The formula:

\[
a\times b
=
(
a_yb_z-a_zb_y,\;
a_zb_x-a_xb_z,\;
a_xb_y-a_yb_x
)
\]

is normally written relative to a positively oriented/right-handed orthonormal basis.

This matters enormously.

If you simply move the same symbolic component formula into a coordinate basis of opposite orientation while pretending it represents the same geometric cross product, you have silently changed an orientation convention.

In engine code, people often avoid this philosophical distinction by establishing one internal cross-product convention and explicitly converting coordinate spaces at boundaries.

That is usually saner.

---

# 564.11 Mesh normals and a right-handed frame

Suppose triangle vertices are:

\[
A,B,C.
\]

Edges:

\[
e_1=B-A,
\]

\[
e_2=C-A.
\]

Normal:

\[
N=e_1\times e_2.
\]

The output orientation depends on:

1. the vertex order;
2. the cross-product convention;
3. the coordinate-basis orientation.

Flip one of these orientation choices and the normal may reverse.

This is why "my normals point inward" can arise from several different mismatches.

---

# 564.12 Right-handed TBN example

Suppose:

\[
T=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
B=(0,1,0),
\]

\[
N=(0,0,1).
\]

Then:

\[
T\times B=N.
\]

So ordered tangent basis:

\[
(T,B,N)
\]

is right-handed.

If your shader reconstructs:

\[
B=N\times T,
\]

then:

\[
T\times(N\times T)=N
\]

for unit perpendicular \(T,N\), as shown in Part 563.

So this convention produces a positively oriented TBN frame.

Mirrored UVs may deliberately require a sign flip.

---

# PART 565 — LEFT-HANDED COORDINATE SYSTEMS

A left-handed basis is not "wrong geometry."

It is simply the opposite orientation class.

---

# 565.1 Mathematical definition

For ordered independent basis:

\[
B=(b_1,b_2,b_3),
\]

under our standard right-handed geometric cross product, define:

\[
H=(b_1\times b_2)\cdot b_3.
\]

If:

\[
\boxed{H<0},
\]

the basis is left-handed.

For an orthonormal left-handed frame:

\[
\boxed{H=-1.}
\]

---

# 565.2 Concrete construction

Start from standard right-handed:

\[
e_x=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
e_y=(0,1,0),
\]

\[
e_z=(0,0,1).
\]

Flip the third direction:

\[
f_x=e_x,
\]

\[
f_y=e_y,
\]

\[
f_z=-e_z.
\]

Then:

\[
f_x\times f_y
=
e_x\times e_y
=
e_z.
\]

But:

\[
f_z=-e_z.
\]

Therefore:

\[
f_x\times f_y=-f_z.
\]

And:

\[
(f_x\times f_y)\cdot f_z
=
e_z\cdot(-e_z)
=
-1.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
(f_x,f_y,f_z)
\text{ is left-handed}.
}
\]

---

# 565.3 The mirror analogy

Put your right hand and left hand in front of you.

They are mirror images.

You can rotate your right hand however you want, but you cannot make it coincide perfectly with your left hand without reflecting it.

That is the geometric intuition behind opposite handedness.

They are congruent under reflection but not under proper rotation.

This is also why the word **chirality** sometimes appears in mathematics and physics.

---

# 565.4 Metric geometry survives a handedness flip

Suppose transform:

\[
M(x,y,z)=(x,y,-z).
\]

Take vectors:

\[
a=(a_x,a_y,a_z),
\]

\[
b=(b_x,b_y,b_z).
\]

After reflection:

\[
a'=(a_x,a_y,-a_z),
\]

\[
b'=(b_x,b_y,-b_z).
\]

Dot:

\[
a'\cdot b'
=
a_xb_x+a_yb_y+(-a_z)(-b_z).
\]

Thus:

\[
=
a_xb_x+a_yb_y+a_zb_z.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
a'\cdot b'=a\cdot b.
}
\]

Therefore lengths are preserved:

\[
\|a'\|=\|a\|.
\]

Angles are preserved.

Distances are preserved.

What changes is **orientation**, not ordinary Euclidean metric structure.

That distinction is extremely important.

---

# 565.5 Why a mirrored object still has the same shape

A reflection preserves:

\[
\text{distance},
\]

\[
\text{angle},
\]

\[
\text{area magnitude},
\]

\[
\text{volume magnitude}.
\]

But it reverses signed orientation quantities.

For example, signed volume:

\[
(a\times b)\cdot c
\]

changes sign under an odd number of axis reflections.

So a mirrored mesh looks geometrically congruent, but its winding/orientation may reverse.

---

# 565.6 Converting coordinate tuples between a simple RH/LH pair

Suppose the two spaces differ only by Z reflection.

Then a point:

\[
P_R=(x,y,z)
\]

in right-handed coordinates corresponds to:

\[
\boxed{
P_L=(x,y,-z)
}
\]

in the reflected convention, assuming origins/scales and axis meanings otherwise match.

Similarly for ordinary direction vectors:

\[
D_L=(d_x,d_y,-d_z).
\]

But beware: normals, cross-derived orientations, triangle winding and transform matrices may require more thought because orientation-sensitive objects do not behave like arbitrary vectors under reflection.

---

# 565.7 Why triangle winding flips under a mirror

Consider triangle in XY plane:

\[
A=(0,0,0),
\]

\[
B=(1,0,1),
\]

\[
C=(0,1,0).
\]

Reflect Z.

Geometric orientation of the embedded triangle relative to the 3D basis changes.

More generally, any orientation-reversing transform changes the sign of oriented volume/area tests associated with the transformed frame.

This is why a handedness conversion may require reversing triangle index order if you want to preserve the intended concept of "front face."

For one triangle:

```cpp
i0, i1, i2
```

reversal can be:

```cpp
i0, i2, i1
```

which flips cross-product normal orientation.

---

# 565.8 Do not memorize graphics APIs as "the handedness"

A graphics API can support data and matrices built under many conventions.

An application, math library, camera system, asset format and projection setup may each adopt particular coordinate conventions.

So it is safer to document concrete facts:

```text
world +Y is up
world +Z is forward
basis orientation is left-handed
camera looks along ...
front-face winding is ...
matrix convention is ...
```

rather than using one vague label and assuming everything else follows from it.

That will save you enormous debugging time later.

---

# RIGHT VS LEFT: WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGES?

| Property | Handedness flip |
|---|---|
| Vector length | preserved |
| Dot product | preserved under reflection |
| Angle magnitude | preserved |
| Distance | preserved |
| Orthogonality | preserved |
| Triangle area magnitude | preserved |
| Volume magnitude | preserved |
| Signed orientation | reversed |
| Scalar triple-product sign | reversed |
| Winding interpretation | may reverse |
| Cross-product orientation relative to basis | reversed |

This is the distinction to own.

# PART 566 — ORIENTATION TESTS IN 2D USING SIGNED AREA

Now we turn handedness into a practical geometric predicate.

Given three 2D points:

\[
A,\quad B,\quad C,
\]

we want to answer:

> Does \(A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C\) make a left turn, right turn, or remain collinear?

This primitive is everywhere in computational geometry.

---

# 566.1 Build two edge vectors

Let:

\[
u=B-A
\]

and:

\[
v=C-A.
\]

In coordinates:

\[
u=(u_x,u_y),
\]

\[
v=(v_x,v_y).
\]

Embed them conceptually in the XY plane of 3D:

\[
(u_x,u_y,0),
\]

\[
(v_x,v_y,0).
\]

Their cross product has only Z component:

\[
u\times v
=
(0,0,u_xv_y-u_yv_x).
\]

Define the 2D cross scalar:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{cross2}(u,v)
=
u_xv_y-u_yv_x.
}
\]

This is the fundamental 2D orientation determinant.

---

# 566.2 Signed doubled triangle area

The magnitude of the 3D cross is parallelogram area:

\[
|u_xv_y-u_yv_x|.
\]

Triangle area is half:

\[
\boxed{
A_\triangle
=
\frac12
\left|
u_xv_y-u_yv_x
\right|.
}
\]

Therefore define signed doubled area:

\[
\boxed{
A_2(A,B,C)
=
(B_x-A_x)(C_y-A_y)
-
(B_y-A_y)(C_x-A_x).
}
\]

Then:

\[
\boxed{
A_{\triangle,\text{signed}}
=
\frac12 A_2.
}
\]

The factor \(1/2\) is irrelevant if we only care about sign, so orientation algorithms often use the doubled area directly.

---

# 566.3 Interpreting the sign

Under the conventional 2D orientation where \(+X\) points right and \(+Y\) points upward:

\[
A_2>0
\]

means:

\[
A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C
\]

is counterclockwise.

\[
A_2<0
\]

means clockwise.

\[
A_2=0
\]

means collinear.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,C)
=
\operatorname{sign}(A_2).
}
\]

---

# 566.4 Worked counterclockwise example

Take:

\[
A=(0,0),
\]

\[
B=(4,0),
\]

\[
C=(1,3).
\]

Then:

\[
B-A=(4,0),
\]

\[
C-A=(1,3).
\]

Compute:

\[
A_2
=
4(3)-0(1)
=
12.
\]

Positive.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
A,B,C\text{ are counterclockwise}.
}
\]

Actual triangle area:

\[
\frac{12}{2}=6.
\]

---

# 566.5 Reverse two vertices

Use:

\[
A,C,B.
\]

Now:

\[
C-A=(1,3),
\]

\[
B-A=(4,0).
\]

Cross scalar:

\[
1(0)-3(4)=-12.
\]

Same area magnitude.

Opposite orientation.

Exactly like cross anti-commutativity.

---

# 566.6 Collinearity

Take:

\[
A=(0,0),
\]

\[
B=(2,2),
\]

\[
C=(5,5).
\]

Then:

\[
B-A=(2,2),
\]

\[
C-A=(5,5).
\]

Compute:

\[
2(5)-2(5)=0.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
A,B,C\text{ are collinear}.
}
\]

The triangle has zero area.

---

# 566.7 Why orientation is translation invariant

Translate every point by vector \(t\):

\[
A'=A+t,
\]

\[
B'=B+t,
\]

\[
C'=C+t.
\]

Then:

\[
B'-A'
=
(B+t)-(A+t)
=
B-A.
\]

Similarly:

\[
C'-A'=C-A.
\]

So signed area does not change.

Thus orientation is a property of relative geometry, not absolute position.

---

# 566.8 Positive scaling versus reflection

Uniform scale \(s>0\):

\[
u\rightarrow su,
\qquad
v\rightarrow sv.
\]

Then:

\[
\operatorname{cross2}(su,sv)
=
s^2\operatorname{cross2}(u,v).
\]

Sign stays same.

But reflect X:

\[
(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y).
\]

The determinant changes sign.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{2D reflection reverses orientation}.
}
\]

This is the 2D analogue of handedness reversal in 3D.

---

# 566.9 Screen coordinates can reverse your intuition

Many screen-space coordinate conventions use Y increasing downward.

If:

\[
+X=\text{right},
\qquad
+Y=\text{down},
\]

then the screen basis has opposite orientation from conventional mathematical XY with Y up.

A triangle that *looks clockwise on the screen* can produce the sign you mentally associate with counterclockwise mathematical coordinates, depending on the convention.

So never say:

> positive determinant means visually counterclockwise

without specifying coordinate orientation.

The formula is objective.

The English label "clockwise" depends on how axes are drawn.

---

# 566.10 C++ implementation

```cpp
struct Vec2 {
    double x;
    double y;
};

[[nodiscard]]
constexpr double orient2D(
    Vec2 a,
    Vec2 b,
    Vec2 c) noexcept
{
    return
        (b.x - a.x) * (c.y - a.y)
      - (b.y - a.y) * (c.x - a.x);
}
```

Then conceptual classification:

```cpp
const double s = orient2D(a, b, c);

if (s > 0.0) {
    // Positive orientation.
}
else if (s < 0.0) {
    // Negative orientation.
}
else {
    // Collinear in exact arithmetic.
}
```

---

# 566.11 Why exact `== 0` becomes dangerous

For integer coordinates within a safe integer range, orientation can sometimes be evaluated exactly using sufficiently wide integer arithmetic.

For floating-point coordinates, near-collinear points may produce a tiny residual:

\[
1.2\times10^{-15}
\]

instead of zero.

But a universal epsilon is also problematic because the determinant scales like area.

If coordinates scale by \(s\):

\[
A_2\rightarrow s^2A_2.
\]

A tolerance meaningful for coordinates around \(1\) may be nonsense around \(10^9\).

Robust computational geometry often uses adaptive/exact orientation predicates when correctness of the sign is critical.

This becomes especially important in:

- convex hulls;
- triangulation;
- intersection algorithms;
- topology construction.

For gameplay approximations, a scale-aware tolerance may be sufficient.

---

# 566.12 Why orientation tests are predicates, not measurements

Sometimes we care about area magnitude.

Sometimes only sign matters.

If the algorithm asks:

> which side of directed edge \(AB\) does \(C\) lie on?

then:

\[
\operatorname{sign}
\left(
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,C)
\right)
\]

is the required information.

Computing:

\[
\sqrt{\cdot}
\]

or normalized distances would be unnecessary.

Good geometry algorithms compute the quantity actually needed.

---

# PART 567 — ORIENTATION TESTS IN 3D USING TRIPLE PRODUCTS

2D orientation used signed area.

In 3D, the corresponding primitive is **signed volume**.

---

# 567.1 Four points are needed

In 2D, three points can define orientation because:

\[
B-A,\ C-A
\]

are two vectors that can span the plane.

In 3D, three points define only a triangle plane.

To determine which side of that plane a fourth point occupies, we need:

\[
A,B,C,D.
\]

Construct:

\[
u=B-A,
\]

\[
v=C-A,
\]

\[
w=D-A.
\]

Then evaluate scalar triple product:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{orient3D}(A,B,C,D)
=
[(B-A)\times(C-A)]\cdot(D-A).
}
\]

Equivalent cyclic arrangement:

\[
(B-A)\cdot[(C-A)\times(D-A)].
\]

The first form makes the plane-side interpretation clearer.

---

# 567.2 Interpret it geometrically

First:

\[
N=(B-A)\times(C-A).
\]

This is an oriented normal to triangle plane \(ABC\).

Then:

\[
N\cdot(D-A)
\]

measures the signed component of \(D-A\) along that normal, multiplied by the base's parallelogram area.

Therefore:

\[
\operatorname{orient3D}>0
\]

means \(D\) lies on the positive-normal side of oriented plane \(ABC\).

Negative means the opposite side.

Zero means:

\[
D
\]

lies in the same plane as \(A,B,C\), or the base triangle itself is degenerate.

---

# 567.3 Signed tetrahedral volume

The scalar triple product equals parallelepiped signed volume.

The tetrahedron with vertices:

\[
A,B,C,D
\]

has one-sixth that volume.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
V_{\text{tet,signed}}
=
\frac16
\operatorname{orient3D}(A,B,C,D).
}
\]

The factor \(1/6\) is irrelevant for a sign predicate.

---

# 567.4 Worked example

Take:

\[
A=(0,0,0),
\]

\[
B=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
C=(0,1,0),
\]

\[
D=(0,0,2).
\]

Then:

\[
B-A=e_x,
\]

\[
C-A=e_y.
\]

Cross:

\[
e_x\times e_y=e_z.
\]

Dot with:

\[
D-A=(0,0,2).
\]

Result:

\[
2.
\]

Therefore \(D\) lies on the positive side of oriented triangle \(ABC\).

Tetrahedron volume:

\[
\frac{2}{6}
=
\frac13.
\]

---

# 567.5 Move \(D\) below the plane

Take:

\[
D=(0,0,-2).
\]

Then:

\[
e_z\cdot(0,0,-2)=-2.
\]

Same volume magnitude.

Opposite orientation.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{orient3D sign tells plane side}.
}
\]

---

# 567.6 Reverse triangle winding

Swap:

\[
B,C.
\]

Then base normal becomes:

\[
(C-A)\times(B-A)
=
-
[(B-A)\times(C-A)].
\]

So every orient3D result changes sign.

The geometric position of \(D\) did not change.

The **orientation of the reference plane** changed.

This is exactly the same logic as reversing triangle normals.

---

# 567.7 Coplanarity

If:

\[
\operatorname{orient3D}=0,
\]

then the four points have zero signed tetrahedron volume.

For nondegenerate \(ABC\), this means \(D\) lies in their plane.

So orient3D is also a coplanarity predicate.

---

# 567.8 Scale behavior

If all coordinates are scaled uniformly by \(s\), each displacement scales by \(s\).

Cross:

\[
s^2.
\]

Dot with third displacement:

\[
s^3.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{orient3D}
\text{ scales cubically}.
}
\]

Again, this makes fixed absolute epsilons dangerous.

---

# 567.9 C++ implementation

```cpp
[[nodiscard]]
double orient3D(
    Vec3d a,
    Vec3d b,
    Vec3d c,
    Vec3d d) noexcept
{
    return dot(
        cross(b - a, c - a),
        d - a
    );
}
```

Conceptually:

```text
triangle ABC
      ↓
oriented normal-area vector
      ↓
dot against D-A
      ↓
signed volume / side test
```

---

# 567.10 Why robust orientation predicates matter so much

Imagine building a tetrahedralization or deciding whether triangles intersect.

A wrong sign can change algorithmic topology:

```text
point classified above plane
instead of below plane
```

may cause an edge to be inserted on the wrong side.

Unlike a small shading error, this can corrupt the entire combinatorial structure.

This is why computational geometry distinguishes between approximate numeric quantities and robust **predicate signs**.

---

# ORIENTATION UNIFICATION

At this point 2D and 3D should look like the same idea at different dimensions.

In 2D:

\[
\boxed{
\det(B-A,C-A)
}
\]

gives signed area and turn orientation.

In 3D:

\[
\boxed{
\det(B-A,C-A,D-A)
}
\]

gives signed volume and side orientation.

This pattern generalizes through determinants.

The determinant Part later will make that structure explicit.

# PART 568 — BARYCENTRIC COORDINATES AS AFFINE WEIGHTS

Now we enter barycentric coordinates.

Do not begin by memorizing a formula with \(d_{00},d_{01}\) or triangle areas.

First understand what problem barycentrics solve.

---

# 568.1 Ordinary Cartesian coordinates depend on an external origin/basis

Suppose point:

\[
P=(3,4).
\]

Those coordinates tell you where \(P\) lies relative to a selected Cartesian frame.

But suppose what we actually care about is a triangle:

\[
A,B,C.
\]

Can we describe \(P\) **using the triangle itself as the reference object**?

Yes.

Write:

\[
\boxed{
P=
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
}
\]

with:

\[
\boxed{
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1.
}
\]

The scalars:

\[
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
\]

are the barycentric coordinates of \(P\) relative to triangle \(ABC\).

---

# 568.2 Why must the weights sum to one?

This is not arbitrary.

Remember points live in an affine space.

Suppose all points are translated by vector \(t\):

\[
A'=A+t,
\]

\[
B'=B+t,
\]

\[
C'=C+t.
\]

Use the same weights:

\[
P'
=
\alpha A'+\beta B'+\gamma C'.
\]

Substitute:

\[
=
\alpha(A+t)
+
\beta(B+t)
+
\gamma(C+t).
\]

Expand:

\[
=
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
+
(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)t.
\]

If:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1,
\]

then:

\[
P'=P+t.
\]

Perfect.

The represented point translates exactly with the triangle.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\sum\text{weights}=1
}
\]

is what makes the combination **affine** rather than origin-dependent.

---

# 568.3 What goes wrong if the weights do not sum to one?

Suppose:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=2.
\]

Translate all vertices by \(t\).

Then represented point moves by:

\[
2t
\]

instead of:

\[
t.
\]

So its meaning depends incorrectly on the chosen origin.

That is why barycentric coordinates use affine weights.

Part 571 will formally compare affine and linear combinations; here we need the barycentric consequence.

---

# 568.4 Vertex coordinates are trivial

At vertex \(A\):

\[
\boxed{
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
=
(1,0,0).
}
\]

Because:

\[
1A+0B+0C=A.
\]

At \(B\):

\[
\boxed{(0,1,0)}.
\]

At \(C\):

\[
\boxed{(0,0,1)}.
\]

This should already give you intuition:

> each weight tells how strongly that vertex contributes.

---

# 568.5 Along an edge

Suppose point lies halfway between \(A\) and \(B\):

\[
P=\frac12A+\frac12B.
\]

Then:

\[
\boxed{
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
=
\left(
\frac12,\frac12,0
\right).
}
\]

The coordinate opposite vertex \(C\) is zero.

In general:

\[
\boxed{
P\in AB
\Rightarrow
\gamma=0.
}
\]

Similarly:

\[
P\in BC
\Rightarrow
\alpha=0,
\]

and:

\[
P\in CA
\Rightarrow
\beta=0.
\]

This property is enormously useful.

---

# 568.6 Interior points

If triangle is nondegenerate and:

\[
\alpha>0,
\quad
\beta>0,
\quad
\gamma>0,
\]

with:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1,
\]

then \(P\) lies strictly inside the triangle.

If zero values are allowed:

\[
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\ge0,
\]

then \(P\) lies inside **or on the boundary**.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
P\in\triangle ABC
\iff
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\ge0
}
\]

subject to numerical tolerance and a nondegenerate triangle.

This gives a point-in-triangle test.

---

# 568.7 Outside points require negative weights

Example in 1D first.

Take points:

\[
A=0,
\qquad
B=1.
\]

A point beyond \(B\), say:

\[
P=2,
\]

can be represented as:

\[
P=-1A+2B.
\]

Weights:

\[
(-1,2)
\]

sum to one.

One is negative.

The same phenomenon occurs for triangles.

Negative barycentric weights indicate the point lies outside the triangle's convex region.

---

# 568.8 Barycentric weights are not distances

This is important.

If:

\[
\alpha=0.8,
\]

that does not generally mean:

> P is 80% of some Euclidean distance from A.

It means:

> A contributes affine weight 0.8 to the point.

For a particular edge interpolation, weights correspond directly to one-dimensional interpolation fractions.

Inside a 2D triangle, the geometry involves all three vertices simultaneously.

---

# 568.9 The centroid / barycenter

The triangle's centroid is:

\[
G=
\frac{A+B+C}{3}.
\]

So its barycentric coordinates are:

\[
\boxed{
\left(
\frac13,\frac13,\frac13
\right).
}
\]

This is where the word "barycentric" gets its center-of-mass flavor.

If equal masses are placed at the three vertices, the balance point is their average.

More generally, normalized positive masses act like barycentric weights.

---

# 568.10 Mass interpretation

Imagine masses:

\[
m_A,m_B,m_C
\]

at vertices.

Center of mass:

\[
P=
\frac{
m_AA+m_BB+m_CC
}{
m_A+m_B+m_C
}.
\]

Define:

\[
\alpha=
\frac{m_A}{M},
\]

\[
\beta=
\frac{m_B}{M},
\]

\[
\gamma=
\frac{m_C}{M},
\]

where:

\[
M=m_A+m_B+m_C.
\]

Then:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1
\]

and:

\[
P=\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C.
\]

So barycentric coordinates can be viewed as normalized balance weights.

---

# 568.11 Uniqueness requires a nondegenerate triangle

Suppose:

\[
A,B,C
\]

are not collinear.

Then they are **affinely independent**.

A point in their plane has unique barycentric coordinates.

But if the triangle collapses:

\[
A,B,C
\]

collinear, the same point may admit multiple barycentric triples.

Why?

Because the three vertices no longer provide two independent affine directions.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{nonzero triangle area is required for unique triangle barycentrics}.
}
\]

---

# PART 569 — DERIVING BARYCENTRIC COORDINATES FOR A TRIANGLE

Now we compute the weights.

We will derive them in several ways because each method exposes a different geometric fact.

---

# 569.1 Start from the affine equation

We want:

\[
P=
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
\]

with:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1.
\]

Use:

\[
\alpha=1-\beta-\gamma.
\]

Then:

\[
P
=
(1-\beta-\gamma)A
+
\beta B+
\gamma C.
\]

Expand:

\[
P
=
A
+
\beta(B-A)
+
\gamma(C-A).
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
P-A
=
\beta(B-A)
+
\gamma(C-A).
}
\]

This is the key reduction.

We transformed a point/affine problem into a vector-basis problem.

Define:

\[
v_0=B-A,
\]

\[
v_1=C-A,
\]

\[
v_2=P-A.
\]

Then solve:

\[
\boxed{
v_2=\beta v_0+\gamma v_1.
}
\]

Once \(\beta,\gamma\) are known:

\[
\boxed{
\alpha=1-\beta-\gamma.
}
\]

---

# 569.2 Derivation using dot products

Take:

\[
v_2=\beta v_0+\gamma v_1.
\]

Dot both sides with \(v_0\):

\[
v_2\cdot v_0
=
\beta(v_0\cdot v_0)
+
\gamma(v_1\cdot v_0).
\]

Dot with \(v_1\):

\[
v_2\cdot v_1
=
\beta(v_0\cdot v_1)
+
\gamma(v_1\cdot v_1).
\]

Define:

\[
d_{00}=v_0\cdot v_0,
\]

\[
d_{01}=v_0\cdot v_1,
\]

\[
d_{11}=v_1\cdot v_1,
\]

\[
d_{20}=v_2\cdot v_0,
\]

\[
d_{21}=v_2\cdot v_1.
\]

Then:

\[
d_{20}
=
\beta d_{00}
+
\gamma d_{01},
\]

\[
d_{21}
=
\beta d_{01}
+
\gamma d_{11}.
\]

This is a \(2\times2\) linear system.

---

# 569.3 Solve the system

Write:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
d_{00}&d_{01}\\
d_{01}&d_{11}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\beta\\
\gamma
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
d_{20}\\
d_{21}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Notice the matrix:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
v_0\cdot v_0&v_0\cdot v_1\\
v_1\cdot v_0&v_1\cdot v_1
\end{bmatrix}
\]

is exactly the **Gram matrix** from Part 541.

So barycentric solving is directly connected to nonorthogonal basis coordinates.

Its determinant-like denominator is:

\[
\boxed{
D=d_{00}d_{11}-d_{01}^2.
}
\]

Then:

\[
\boxed{
\beta=
\frac{
d_{11}d_{20}-d_{01}d_{21}
}{
D
}
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
\gamma=
\frac{
d_{00}d_{21}-d_{01}d_{20}
}{
D
}.
}
\]

Finally:

\[
\boxed{
\alpha=1-\beta-\gamma.
}
\]

---

# 569.4 What is the denominator geometrically?

Recall the Gram determinant identity:

\[
\|v_0\times v_1\|^2
=
\|v_0\|^2\|v_1\|^2
-
(v_0\cdot v_1)^2.
\]

But:

\[
d_{00}=\|v_0\|^2,
\]

\[
d_{11}=\|v_1\|^2,
\]

\[
d_{01}=v_0\cdot v_1.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
D
=
\|v_0\times v_1\|^2.
}
\]

That is **parallelogram area squared**.

So when:

\[
D=0,
\]

triangle area is zero.

The barycentric solve fails exactly when the triangle is degenerate.

Again, algebraic singularity corresponds directly to geometric degeneracy.

---

# 569.5 Worked dot-product example

Take:

\[
A=(0,0),
\]

\[
B=(4,0),
\]

\[
C=(0,2),
\]

and:

\[
P=(1,0.5).
\]

Embed in 2D directly.

Then:

\[
v_0=B-A=(4,0),
\]

\[
v_1=C-A=(0,2),
\]

\[
v_2=P-A=(1,0.5).
\]

Dots:

\[
d_{00}=16,
\]

\[
d_{01}=0,
\]

\[
d_{11}=4,
\]

\[
d_{20}=4,
\]

\[
d_{21}=1.
\]

Denominator:

\[
D=16(4)-0=64.
\]

Then:

\[
\beta
=
\frac{4(4)}{64}
=
\frac14.
\]

And:

\[
\gamma
=
\frac{16(1)}{64}
=
\frac14.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\alpha
=
1-\frac14-\frac14
=
\frac12.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
=
\left(
\frac12,\frac14,\frac14
\right).
}
\]

Verify:

\[
\frac12(0,0)
+
\frac14(4,0)
+
\frac14(0,2)
=
(1,0.5).
\]

Correct.

---

# 569.6 Area-coordinate derivation

There is a beautiful geometric method.

For point \(P\) in the plane of triangle \(ABC\), define signed subtriangle areas.

Let:

\[
A_{ABC}
\]

be signed area of the whole triangle.

Then:

\[
\boxed{
\alpha
=
\frac{
A_{PBC}
}{
A_{ABC}
}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\beta
=
\frac{
A_{APC}
}{
A_{ABC}
}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\gamma
=
\frac{
A_{ABP}
}{
A_{ABC}
}.
}
\]

The orientation/order in each signed-area expression must be chosen consistently.

---

# 569.7 Why area ratio equals barycentric weight

Focus on \(\alpha\), the coefficient of \(A\).

Consider edge \(BC\) as a fixed base.

Triangles:

\[
ABC
\]

and:

\[
PBC
\]

share the same base \(BC\).

Their area ratio is therefore the ratio of their signed heights above line \(BC\).

At \(P=A\), the ratio is 1.

At any point \(P\) on \(BC\), the height is zero, so the ratio is 0.

And the signed height varies linearly as \(P\) moves perpendicular to \(BC\).

That is exactly the affine behavior required of the coordinate associated with vertex \(A\).

Similarly for the other vertices.

So barycentric coordinates are literally normalized opposite-subtriangle areas.

---

# 569.8 Verify with our example

Triangle:

\[
A=(0,0),
\quad
B=(4,0),
\quad
C=(0,2).
\]

Whole doubled area:

\[
A_2(A,B,C)
=
4(2)=8.
\]

Actual area:

\[
4.
\]

Point:

\[
P=(1,0.5).
\]

For \(\gamma\), triangle \(ABP\):

base \(AB=4\), height \(0.5\).

Area:

\[
1.
\]

Whole area:

\[
4.
\]

Thus:

\[
\gamma=\frac14.
\]

Similarly \(\beta=\frac14\) and \(\alpha=\frac12\).

Perfect.

---

# 569.9 2D signed-area implementation

Define:

```cpp
double orient2D(Vec2 a, Vec2 b, Vec2 c);
```

Then for nondegenerate triangle:

\[
D=\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,C).
\]

A consistent barycentric form is:

\[
\boxed{
\alpha=
\frac{
\operatorname{orient2D}(P,B,C)
}{
D
}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\beta=
\frac{
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,P,C)
}{
D
}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\gamma=
\frac{
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,P)
}{
D
}.
}
\]

If you reverse all orientation conventions consistently, numerator and denominator signs both reverse, leaving the weights unchanged.

---

# 569.10 Why the weights sum to one geometrically

The oriented subtriangles partition the oriented whole triangle algebraically:

\[
A_{PBC}
+
A_{APC}
+
A_{ABP}
=
A_{ABC}.
\]

Divide by:

\[
A_{ABC}.
\]

Then:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1.
\]

This remains true algebraically even when \(P\) is outside and some signed subareas become negative.

That is why signed areas are superior to taking absolute values during barycentric computation.

If you use absolute areas, outside-point relationships break.

---

# 569.11 Point-in-triangle test via barycentrics

For a nondegenerate triangle:

\[
P\text{ inside or on boundary}
\]

iff:

\[
\boxed{
\alpha\ge0,\quad
\beta\ge0,\quad
\gamma\ge0
}
\]

with:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1.
\]

Strictly inside requires:

\[
\alpha>0,\quad
\beta>0,\quad
\gamma>0.
\]

On edge \(BC\):

\[
\alpha=0.
\]

At vertex \(A\):

\[
\alpha=1,\quad\beta=\gamma=0.
\]

So a single coordinate system gives classification information almost for free.

---

# 569.12 Outside classification

Suppose:

\[
\alpha<0.
\]

Then \(P\) lies outside the triangle across the edge opposite \(A\), namely edge \(BC\), under consistent orientation.

Likewise:

\[
\beta<0
\]

indicates outside across edge \(CA\), and:

\[
\gamma<0
\]

across edge \(AB\).

This becomes useful for closest-point algorithms and collision routines later.

---

# 569.13 Degenerate triangles

If:

\[
A,B,C
\]

are collinear, then:

\[
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,C)=0
\]

and:

\[
D=0.
\]

The barycentric coordinate map loses uniqueness.

This is unavoidable.

Any API computing triangle barycentrics must therefore have an explicit degenerate-triangle policy:

```text
return failure
assert precondition
fallback to segment logic
use application-specific tolerance
```

Silently dividing by tiny area is not robust geometry.

---

# 569.14 A 3D triangle does not require projecting to XY if using the dot method

If:

\[
A,B,C,P\in\mathbb R^3
\]

and \(P\) lies in the triangle plane, the Gram/dot derivation works directly in 3D.

You do not need to decide whether to drop X, Y or Z.

That is a major advantage of the dot-product formulation.

The area-coordinate version can also be performed in 3D using oriented normals/areas, but the dot form is often convenient.

---

# 569.15 What if P is not exactly in the triangle plane?

The equation:

\[
P-A
=
\beta(B-A)+\gamma(C-A)
\]

has no exact solution if \(P\) has a nonzero normal component.

The dot/Gram equations effectively recover the coefficients of the **orthogonal projection of \(P\)** onto the triangle's span.

Why?

Because solving via inner products finds the least-squares in-plane component.

This is useful, but the semantics must be understood.

If you require true point-on-triangle membership, also test distance to the triangle plane.

---

# PART 570 — INTERPOLATING ATTRIBUTES WITH BARYCENTRIC COORDINATES

This is where barycentrics become foundational graphics machinery.

Suppose each triangle vertex has an attribute:

\[
q_A,
\quad
q_B,
\quad
q_C.
\]

At a point with barycentric coordinates:

\[
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma),
\]

define interpolated attribute:

\[
\boxed{
q(P)
=
\alpha q_A+
\beta q_B+
\gamma q_C.
}
\]

This works for any attribute living in a space where affine/linear interpolation makes sense.

---

# 570.1 Scalar example

Suppose vertex temperatures:

\[
t_A=10,
\]

\[
t_B=30,
\]

\[
t_C=50.
\]

At barycentrics:

\[
\left(
\frac12,\frac14,\frac14
\right),
\]

we get:

\[
t(P)
=
\frac12(10)
+
\frac14(30)
+
\frac14(50).
\]

Calculate:

\[
5+7.5+12.5=25.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{t(P)=25.}
\]

---

# 570.2 Color interpolation

Let:

\[
C_A=(1,0,0)
\]

red,

\[
C_B=(0,1,0)
\]

green,

\[
C_C=(0,0,1)
\]

blue.

At:

\[
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
=
\left(
\frac12,\frac14,\frac14
\right),
\]

interpolated color:

\[
C(P)
=
\frac12C_A+
\frac14C_B+
\frac14C_C.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
C(P)=
\left(
0.5,0.25,0.25
\right).
}
\]

The exact same weights that locate the point interpolate its attributes.

That is the key graphics insight.

---

# 570.3 Why using the same weights is geometrically natural

Imagine attribute \(q\) is itself an affine function across the triangle.

At vertices it takes:

\[
q_A,q_B,q_C.
\]

Because:

\[
P=\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
\]

and affine maps preserve affine combinations, we expect:

\[
\boxed{
q(P)
=
\alpha q(A)
+
\beta q(B)
+
\gamma q(C).
}
\]

This is why linear variation over a triangle is represented exactly by barycentric interpolation.

---

# 570.4 Edge behavior is automatically correct

Suppose \(P\) lies on edge \(AB\).

Then:

\[
\gamma=0.
\]

So:

\[
q(P)
=
\alpha q_A+\beta q_B.
\]

Vertex \(C\) contributes nothing.

Thus the triangle interpolation reduces naturally to ordinary line interpolation on its edges.

This is crucial for continuity between adjacent triangles.

---

# 570.5 Vertex behavior is automatically correct

At \(A\):

\[
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1,0,0).
\]

Therefore:

\[
q(A)=q_A.
\]

Likewise at the other vertices.

No special-case code is necessary.

---

# 570.6 UV interpolation

If vertices have texture coordinates:

\[
UV_A,
\quad
UV_B,
\quad
UV_C,
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
UV(P)
=
\alpha UV_A+
\beta UV_B+
\gamma UV_C.
}
\]

This determines where on a texture the interior point corresponds.

At the affine-geometry level this is straightforward.

There is, however, an important rasterization complication involving perspective, which we will discuss shortly.

---

# 570.7 Normal interpolation

Suppose vertex normals:

\[
N_A,
N_B,
N_C.
\]

Compute:

\[
N_{\text{raw}}
=
\alpha N_A+
\beta N_B+\gamma N_C.
\]

But even if each vertex normal is unit length, the interpolated vector generally is not.

Therefore shading commonly uses:

\[
\boxed{
N(P)
=
\operatorname{normalize}
(
\alpha N_A+
\beta N_B+
\gamma N_C
).
}
\]

This is exactly the issue from Part 557.

Interpolation happens in vector coordinates.

The unit-normal invariant must be re-established afterward.

---

# 570.8 Tangents can have the same issue

If you interpolate tangent vectors:

\[
T_{\text{raw}}
=
\alpha T_A+
\beta T_B+\gamma T_C,
\]

the result may no longer be:

- unit length;
- exactly perpendicular to interpolated normal.

A shading frame may therefore need re-normalization and re-orthogonalization.

Interpolation preserves affine combinations.

It does not automatically preserve nonlinear constraints such as:

\[
\|T\|=1
\]

or:

\[
T\cdot N=0.
\]

This distinction is fundamental.

---

# 570.9 Interpolation preserves linear constraints, not nonlinear ones

Suppose all vertex values satisfy a linear equation:

\[
L(q)=c
\]

and weights sum to one.

Then:

\[
L(
\alpha q_A+\beta q_B+\gamma q_C
)
\]

becomes:

\[
\alpha L(q_A)+
\beta L(q_B)+
\gamma L(q_C).
\]

If every:

\[
L(q_i)=c,
\]

then:

\[
=
(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)c
=
c.
\]

So affine constraints survive.

But unit length is:

\[
q\cdot q=1,
\]

which is quadratic/nonlinear.

Therefore it does not survive ordinary interpolation.

This is why normals need renormalization.

---

# 570.10 Interpolating positions reconstructs P itself

If the attribute is position:

\[
q_A=A,
\]

\[
q_B=B,
\]

\[
q_C=C,
\]

then barycentric interpolation gives:

\[
q(P)
=
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C.
\]

But that equals:

\[
P.
\]

So the geometry itself is one instance of the same interpolation rule.

---

# 570.11 Interpolating arbitrary `VecN`

A generic implementation might look like:

```cpp
template<typename T>
[[nodiscard]]
constexpr T baryLerp(
    T const& a,
    T const& b,
    T const& c,
    double alpha,
    double beta,
    double gamma)
{
    return
        a * alpha
      + b * beta
      + c * gamma;
}
```

This assumes type `T` supports scalar multiplication and addition with appropriate semantics.

But that does **not** mean every type should be interpolated this way.

For example:

- raw unit quaternions need more careful rotational interpolation;
- categorical IDs should not be blended;
- booleans have no sensible affine interpolation;
- material indices usually should not be interpolated.

Type shape alone does not establish mathematical legitimacy.

---

# 570.12 Barycentric derivatives are constant over an affine triangle

This is a powerful fact.

For a nondegenerate triangle in a flat coordinate plane, each barycentric coordinate:

\[
\alpha(P),
\quad
\beta(P),
\quad
\gamma(P)
\]

is an affine-linear function of position.

Therefore its gradient is constant across the triangle.

That is why linearly interpolated vertex attributes have constant screen-space/object-space derivatives across a triangle before perspective correction.

This later connects to:

- rasterization;
- texture derivatives;
- gradients;
- finite elements;
- interpolation hardware.

We are not teaching derivative machinery fully here, but the structural fact matters.

---

# 570.13 The critical graphics caveat: perspective interpolation

Suppose 3D vertices are projected through a perspective camera.

Screen-space barycentric coordinates are affine in **screen coordinates**.

But a 3D attribute that was affine across the original triangle generally does **not** remain affine after perspective division.

So this naive procedure can be wrong:

```text
compute screen-space barycentrics
use them directly on original UVs
```

for perspective-projected geometry.

This produces the classic "affine texture mapping" distortion.

Modern rasterization uses **perspective-correct interpolation**.

The conceptual correction involves interpolating quantities weighted by reciprocal homogeneous depth, often expressed using \(1/w\), then dividing appropriately.

The full homogeneous/perspective machinery comes later, so do not memorize a correction formula out of context yet.

For Part 570, retain this distinction:

\[
\boxed{
\text{barycentric interpolation is affine;
perspective projection is not affine after division}.
}
\]

Therefore screen-space barycentric weights require perspective correction for most 3D vertex attributes.

That is not a failure of barycentrics.

It is a consequence of projective geometry.

# 570.14 Rasterization connection

A rasterizer conceptually has a triangle in screen coordinates.

For each candidate sample/pixel center:

\[
P=(x,y),
\]

it can evaluate oriented edge functions.

Those edge functions are closely related to the signed areas from Part 566.

Normalized appropriately, they become barycentric coordinates.

Then the rasterizer can answer both:

\[
\text{Is the sample inside the triangle?}
\]

and:

\[
\text{What are its interpolation weights?}
\]

with closely related arithmetic.

This is why orientation tests, signed areas and barycentrics appear next to each other in the curriculum.

They are literally the mathematical core of triangle rasterization.

# 570.15 Edge functions

For directed edge \(A\rightarrow B\), define:

\[
E_{AB}(P)
=
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,P).
\]

This tells which side of edge AB the point occupies.

For a consistently wound triangle, a point lies inside if the three edge-function signs are compatible.

Now notice:

\[
\gamma
=
\frac{
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,P)
}{
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,C)
}.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
\gamma=
\frac{E_{AB}(P)}{E_{AB}(C)}.
}
\]

Likewise the other barycentric coordinates correspond to normalized edge functions for their opposite edges.

This is a huge connection:

\[
\boxed{
\text{edge test}
\rightarrow
\text{signed area}
\rightarrow
\text{barycentric weight}
}
\]

using essentially the same determinant structure.

# ONE COMPLETE EXAMPLE: ORIENTATION → BARYCENTRIC → ATTRIBUTE

Take:

\[
A=(0,0),
\]

\[
B=(4,0),
\]

\[
C=(0,4).
\]

The triangle has:

\[
D=
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,B,C)
=
4(4)
=
16.
\]

Positive orientation.

Take point:

\[
P=(1,2).
\]

Compute \(\alpha\):

\[
\operatorname{orient2D}(P,B,C).
\]

Vectors from \(P\):

\[
B-P=(3,-2),
\]

\[
C-P=(-1,2).
\]

Cross scalar:

\[
3(2)-(-2)(-1)
=
6-2
=
4.
\]

Thus:

\[
\alpha=\frac4{16}=\frac14.
\]

Now \(\beta\):

\[
\operatorname{orient2D}(A,P,C).
\]

Vectors:

\[
P-A=(1,2),
\]

\[
C-A=(0,4).
\]

Cross:

\[
1(4)-2(0)=4.
\]

Thus:

\[
\beta=\frac14.
\]

Finally:

\[
\gamma=1-\alpha-\beta
=
\frac12.
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
=
\left(
\frac14,\frac14,\frac12
\right).
}
\]

Check point reconstruction:

\[
\frac14(0,0)
+
\frac14(4,0)
+
\frac12(0,4).
\]

This gives:

\[
(1,2).
\]

Correct.

Now assign colors:

\[
C_A=(1,0,0),
\]

\[
C_B=(0,1,0),
\]

\[
C_C=(0,0,1).
\]

Interpolate:

\[
C(P)
=
\frac14C_A
+
\frac14C_B
+
\frac12C_C.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
C(P)
=
(0.25,0.25,0.5).
}
\]

One chain produced:

```text
triangle orientation
      ↓
signed area denominator

sample point edge areas
      ↓
barycentric coordinates

same barycentric coordinates
      ↓
position reconstruction
color interpolation
UV interpolation
normal interpolation
...
```

That is why these Parts belong together.

# HANDEDNESS AND BARYCENTRICS: DO BARYCENTRICS CHANGE UNDER A MIRROR?

This is worth deriving.

Suppose the triangle and point are all transformed by the same invertible affine transformation, including a mirror.

Barycentric relation:

\[
P=\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C,
\]

with:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1.
\]

Apply affine transform:

\[
F(X)=MX+t.
\]

Then:

\[
F(P)
=
M(
\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
)+t.
\]

Distribute:

\[
=
\alpha MA+
\beta MB+
\gamma MC+t.
\]

Because weights sum to one:

\[
t=
(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)t.
\]

So:

\[
F(P)
=
\alpha(MA+t)
+
\beta(MB+t)
+
\gamma(MC+t).
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
F(P)
=
\alpha F(A)
+
\beta F(B)
+
\gamma F(C).
}
\]

The barycentric weights are unchanged.

Even under a reflection.

What changes under handedness reversal?

The signed-area numerators and denominator can all flip orientation sign.

But because the same orientation factor appears in numerator and denominator:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the barycentric ratios remain the same}.
}
\]

This is a beautiful separation:

\[
\boxed{
\text{orientation sign may flip;
affine coordinates survive}.
}
\]

# NUMERICAL STABILITY OF BARYCENTRICS

The dangerous case is a skinny or nearly degenerate triangle.

Suppose:

\[
C
\]

lies almost on line \(AB\).

Then total area:

\[
D
\]

is tiny.

Barycentric formulas divide by \(D\).

A tiny denominator means a small geometric perturbation can produce a large change in barycentric coordinates.

This is not only floating-point trouble.

The underlying coordinate system is ill-conditioned.

Imagine a triangle:

```text
A ---------------- B
         C almost on AB
```

There is almost no two-dimensional region left.

Trying to represent arbitrary nearby positions using that triangle is inherently unstable.

So again:

\[
\boxed{
\text{numerical instability reflects geometric degeneracy}.
}
\]

# A PRODUCTION BARYCENTRIC API

A reference implementation using dot products for `Vec3` could be:

```cpp
struct Barycentric3 {
    double a;
    double b;
    double c;
};

[[nodiscard]]
std::optional<Barycentric3>
barycentric(
    Vec3d A,
    Vec3d B,
    Vec3d C,
    Vec3d P,
    double degeneracyThreshold)
{
    const Vec3d v0 = B - A;
    const Vec3d v1 = C - A;
    const Vec3d v2 = P - A;

    const double d00 = dot(v0, v0);
    const double d01 = dot(v0, v1);
    const double d11 = dot(v1, v1);
    const double d20 = dot(v2, v0);
    const double d21 = dot(v2, v1);

    const double denom =
        d00 * d11 - d01 * d01;

    if (std::abs(denom) <= degeneracyThreshold) {
        return std::nullopt;
    }

    const double beta =
        (d11 * d20 - d01 * d21)
        / denom;

    const double gamma =
        (d00 * d21 - d01 * d20)
        / denom;

    const double alpha =
        1.0 - beta - gamma;

    return Barycentric3{
        alpha,
        beta,
        gamma
    };
}
```

But a serious implementation should not pretend one globally fixed `degeneracyThreshold` works for every world scale.

A scale-aware policy can compare area squared against quantities derived from edge lengths, or higher-precision/robust predicates can be used where topology depends on the result.

# COMPLEXITY AND HARDWARE

Every individual 2D/3D orientation or triangle-barycentric query is fixed-size arithmetic:

\[
O(1).
\]

For 2D orientation:

\[
2
\]

multiplications plus subtraction after coordinate differences.

For a dot-product barycentric solve in 3D, several dot products plus a handful of scalar arithmetic operations.

No dynamic allocation is intrinsically needed.

In a rasterizer, however, millions of sample evaluations make constant factors significant.

The important performance insight is that **edge functions vary linearly across screen space**. When moving one pixel horizontally or vertically, they can be updated incrementally rather than recomputed from scratch.

That later becomes a major rasterization optimization.

The current Part's job is to make you understand why such incremental edge arithmetic is possible: edge functions are affine-linear functions.

# DEBUGGING ATTACK TREE

Suppose a triangle appears back-facing unexpectedly. First inspect the actual coordinate convention and whether screen Y orientation differs from world/math Y. Then inspect triangle index winding and whether a handedness-reflecting transform occurred.

Suppose point-in-triangle tests fail only near edges. Inspect floating-point predicate tolerance and whether your algorithm requires robust orientation signs.

Suppose barycentrics do not sum to one. That usually indicates an algebra/implementation error or severe numerical corruption.

Suppose barycentrics become enormous. Inspect triangle area: the triangle may be nearly degenerate.

Suppose colors interpolate correctly but texture coordinates warp under perspective. The barycentric computation itself may be correct; the missing step is perspective-correct interpolation.

Suppose interpolated normals darken lighting. Check whether they were renormalized after interpolation.

Suppose a mirrored model's barycentric interpolation is correct but its culling/normals are reversed. That is exactly possible: affine barycentric weights survive the mirror, while orientation-sensitive quantities flip.

That separation is an important debugging skill.

# THE ENTIRE 564–570 MODEL

You should now see one continuous structure:

\[
\boxed{
\text{ordered basis}
}
\]

has an orientation:

\[
\boxed{
\text{right-handed or left-handed}.
}
\]

Orientation in 2D becomes:

\[
\boxed{
\text{signed area}.
}
\]

Orientation in 3D becomes:

\[
\boxed{
\text{signed volume}.
}
\]

For a triangle, those signed areas can then be normalized:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\text{sub-area}}{\text{whole area}}
}
\]

to produce:

\[
\boxed{
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
}
\]

with:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1.
\]

Those weights locate a point:

\[
\boxed{
P=\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C
}
\]

and then propagate any affine attribute:

\[
\boxed{
q(P)=\alpha q_A+\beta q_B+\gamma q_C.
}
\]

This is the bridge from **orientation mathematics** to the inner machinery of a triangle rasterizer.
